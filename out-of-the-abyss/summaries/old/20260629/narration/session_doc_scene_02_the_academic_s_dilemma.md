Three roads lay open in front of us, and none of them were clean. We could turn the whole thing over to Kalan and tell him to go for it. We could go to A'lai Aivenmore and say, quietly, that we knew things. Or we could march straight up to the Bookwyrm herself and demand justice and satisfaction. I filed all three and waited to see which way the room leaned.

Thorin leaned first. Hand it to Kalan, he said — let the man save face, seeing as he'd been fired, with the understanding that they'd make good on the reward for cracking the case. A decent instinct. Give the credit to the one who needs it, keep the coin. Someone said the plan sounded good, and that was that; the party had chosen.

Daz offered the contingency he always offers. "If things go sideways, we can always start hacking and slashing." I have stopped writing that one down. It is a standing entry.

Thorin wasn't done chewing. He turned the fame question over — if we made a big deal of being the ones who solved it, that fed the legend, but did we want to be famous or fly under the radar? "We're already infamous underground," he said. "It didn't seem to serve us well." No. It did not. I named us the Ember Vanguard once, as a joke, and the joke got away from me and started killing people in cities I've never seen. I said nothing. He was right.

So we handed it to Kalan, and Kalan was not grateful. He went disgruntled the way academics go disgruntled — inward, sour, already composing the rebuttal. "You have a confession, a heart, and a cleaver," he said, and then he took all three apart. The confession was one spell. The weakest channel, by the standard he himself had written. The heart and the cleaver proved someone had covered this up cleverly — neither proved who. No one could put the Bookwyrm in that room. No cloak, no witness that would hold, nothing she couldn't turn back on us in a single sentence. "By my own paper," he said, almost wounded by it, "she walks."

"Alright," Thorin said, "so we gotta nail Bookwyrm."

Zalthir doesn't miss the seam in a thing. "How did he open that little diatribe?" he asked. A confession, a heart, a cleaver. "But she didn't discount the confession." He'd caught it — the confession hadn't been called false, only insufficient.

And there it was: the thrice-fold proof. You cannot lean on one source of truth, the paper says. You need three different kinds. My old training had a formulation for objects like this. The purpose of a maxim is not to tell you what to do — it is to tell you what you are doing. Kalan's paper wasn't telling him the Bookwyrm was innocent. It was telling him, in his own hand, that he had proven nothing. And worse — the protocol had never been approved. It was declined.

Thorin pushed. A confession is a confession. But you know what matters more to an academic, Kalan asked — putting the murderer in a cell, or staying true to the research proposal? Which was declined. He said the word twice, like a bruise he kept pressing. Then came the crack in the door: the person who declined it was dead. Janussi. Death has a way of reopening files.

Zalthir sketched a plan — take Kalan, wrap the Bookwyrm in a zone of truth, walk in and talk. "You could drive," he offered. Thorin wanted to know if the truth could be beaten. It could now — she could shake it off, but we'd know the instant she did. "We don't know if she's telling the truth in that case," Zalthir said, "but we would know if she can lie." A narrow instrument. I liked it better than most.

Kalan would not sneak. "I will not sneak this time. We will walk in, and we will boldly demand that she submit to a zone of truth." He'd cheated last time — stood outside the room and cast it while Alkrist never understood what was happening until it was too late. That was why it had worked. And now, offered the same trick against a bigger target, he balked. Zalthir found the reason, because there always is one under the principle: Kalan didn't want to twist the Bookwyrm, have her wriggle free, and end up his boss. He would not do it without her permission. And there — the whole thing, laid bare. Not scholarship. Career.

Then the patient play. The Bookwyrm was only the acting head, he reminded us. Tomorrow there was a naming ceremony, and with the suspicion over her name — the confession, the cleaver, the blood — he suspected the vote would go elsewhere. "Sometimes the clever play is to be patient." The stench of the suspicion would be all over her. Fembris Lancer would likely be elected instead, and the first thing she'd want was to be rid of the Bookwyrm.

"Wait and see if people figure it out," Daz said.

"Yes."

This is not the will of any god. This is the will of the people. The crime wouldn't decide it — the ceremony would. And then Kalan reached the ugly heart of it: if she were removed from her seat, that would be far more damning than any crime we could convict her of. To be unmade in front of everyone is worse than a cell. I wrote that one down exactly.

Zalthir, who keeps a conscience where the rest of us keep tactics, raised his hand against it. Wasn't that immoral — to block the Bookwyrm from the role if she were innocent? "Which is why we will give her the opportunity to decline," Kalan said. A choice that condemns her either way. Agree, and she's innocent with nothing to hide. Decline, and the refusal itself sinks her. Zalthir said the logic back to him, and Kalan held strong conviction the other Avowed would never elect her. When Zalthir asked whether he'd truly disclose everything, Kalan didn't blink. "Of course, I will tell them everything. I will, in fact, give it to Bookwyrm herself." "Well," Zalthir said, "I don't know if that's productive, but okay." I did. A man who files the whole report, even to the accused — I know the type. I am the type.

Thorin and Zalthir worked the crime backward — the heart lifted out and set in the chalice of the statue, the Bookwyrm shielding Alkrist, her nephew, who had done the killing. Thorin hunted for something physical to tie her to it. Kalan stopped him gently. "My friends," he said, "although the Bookwyrm should be punished, the reality is that all she did was hide a crime. She didn't commit a crime." Conspiracy after the fact, Thorin argued — she's culpable. And Kalan gave the cynic's answer again: removal would be more damning than conviction. Or we could do both. That he'd leave to the reader who followed her.

Undeterred, Thorin wanted a book. Some magic tome, a powerful enough spell to crack it open. Kalan pulled out his copy of the thrice-fold proof and shoved it in Thorin's face, pointing at the lines. "There is no spell that'll get you the evidence that will solve the crime — do you not understand?" A man brandishing his own gospel. I know a few of those, too.

Then Thorin found the angle that finally pleased him. Reverse it. Go in, name the guilty, take the reward — and one of us uses that reward to acquire the book that does her in, after the fact. Kalan brightened like a lamp. That was much better. Up front, she'd suspect; but ask for a book, and the book comes from the new head of Candlekeep, who'll be delighted to hand it over. He didn't know which yet. "I will come up with such a book before you leave," he promised.

Thorin savored it. She'd think she'd gotten away with it. Sad about Alkrist, sure — but the boy confessed, so it is what it is. And then, when she least suspected, we take our reward and turn it over on her. High drama. A good closing. One more thing.

"Can I get, like, a really frumpy trench coat?"

That's Colombo, someone said — not the monk, the other one.

"Just one more thing."

I like it, they went round the circle. Seems reasonable. And I did, in the end. A patient play, a paper for scripture, and a trap wearing the face of a choice. I took notes on all of it. Bahamut may or may not have been reading over my shoulder. I filed the report either way.
