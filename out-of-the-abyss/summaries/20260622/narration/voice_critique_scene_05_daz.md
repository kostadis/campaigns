**Suggested rewrite:** "A mage hand, which was interesting. She was bedridden and shuddering under a fever, yet still running a familiar and a mage hand from across the room."

### [18] Voice spec conflict — thought-intrusion frequency

> I had wondered when Menzoberranzan would enter the room. The suspicion was entirely reasonable — the city I came from is not known for delivering bad news to people out of charitable concern.

and, later in the same scene:

> Thorin said something quiet about how any drow would understand exactly what she was describing. He wasn't wrong. Being the easy frame is something Menzoberranzan prepares you to recognize, if not always to avoid.

**Why:** The spec's "two thought sources" section caps the Menzoberranzan-training device explicitly: "Use this sparingly: at most one thought intrusion per scene." Both quoted passages are that device (a cold, method-flavored assessment attributed to the city's training) — this scene uses it twice.
**Suggested rewrite:** Cut the second instance's explicit Menzoberranzan framing and let it stand as plain observation: "Thorin said something quiet about how any drow would understand exactly what she was describing. He wasn't wrong. She was describing a category he already knew by heart."

### [19] Tell-not-show emotional commentary

> She was genuinely upset, I could see that, and genuinely shocked. But underneath both was something else: relief.

**Why:** Three emotions (upset, shocked, relief) are named outright rather than rendered, one paragraph before the narration correctly *shows* a reaction ("The face that followed was the specific expression of someone who thought they had all the cards..."). The spec's model for this is rendering the tell, not stacking adjectives.
**Suggested rewrite:** "Upset and shocked, plainly — that much anyone could read. Underneath both was something she hadn't meant to let through: relief."

### [20] Generic/cliché prose

> But I found the right framing, the right register, put everything I had into making the truth land as true, and something clicked. The kind of clean landing you feel before you know it's worked.

**Why:** "Clean landing" is a stock aviation/gymnastics idiom with no anchor in Daz's established metaphor families (audit, currency, tactical precision) — it could belong to any narrator's prose. It also feeds a "clean" overuse pattern that runs across both scenes (see [22]).
**Suggested rewrite:** "But I found the right framing, the right register, put everything I had into making the truth land as true, and something clicked into place — the kind of entry you know is correct before you've even totaled the column."

### [21] Generic/cliché prose

> she kept talking through it the way people do when they're trying to outrun the grief.

**Why:** "Outrun the grief" is a common literary idiom, not specific to Daz's vocabulary or drow sensibility, and reads as generic where the surrounding sentences (breaking down "not performance," the "frumpy old boar" aside) are more particular.
**Suggested rewrite:** "she kept talking through it the way people do when the alternative is sitting still with it."

### [22] Repetition/convergence — cross-scene

> ...The kind of *clean* landing you feel before you know it's worked. (line 37)
> ...though not quite as *cleanly* as the first time. (line 61)
> The contradiction was *clean*: if she was with her son... (line 79)
> *Clean* and precise, the way she delivered it. (line 89)

**Why:** "Clean/cleanly" appears four times in this scene's narration alone, and three more times in scene 08's narration (seven total across the two documents) — functioning as an unexamined default adjective for "correct/successful/true." The spec's author's note warns against exactly this pattern for the bookkeeping vocabulary ("rather than letting one noun dominate a scene or the document"); the same discipline should apply here, since "clean" is crowding out more specific audit-family words the spec explicitly offers (tally, account, balance, debt).
**Suggested rewrite:** Vary per instance — "landed true," "not quite as precisely," "the contradiction balanced: if she was with her son...," "Exact, the way she delivered it." Reserve "clean" for at most one use per scene.

### [23] Voice spec conflict — vocabulary rotation

> I filed that in the column marked *not incapacitated.* (line 11)
> I filed the compliment and moved on. (line 23)
> I marked Daral in the column headed *possible but unlikely* (line 43)
> I filed that in a column I hadn't expected to need this early. (line 93)

**Why:** The spec's author's note is explicit: "rotate the bookkeeping vocabulary as you narrate — audit, tally, account, column, balance, debt, the running list — rather than letting one noun ('ledger') dominate a scene." This scene uses only "column" and "filed," six times, with zero variation into the other five sanctioned nouns.
**Suggested rewrite:** e.g., line 43 → "I entered Daral on the tally marked *possible but unlikely*"; line 93 → "I opened an account I hadn't expected to need this early."

## Verdict

The strongest recurring problem is mechanical, not tonal: seventeen narration-level em-dashes need conversion, several of them wrapped around lines that are otherwise pitch-perfect Daz (the "I will not call her a mark" substitution, the audit-column list of suspects). Underneath that, the scene shows real voice discipline — Sylvira's read, the "most straightforwardly true thing I'd said" beat, the closing suspect inventory — but it leans on "clean" as a reflexive verdict-adjective and narrows the spec's seven-word bookkeeping vocabulary down to just two. This is a spot-edit pass, not a re-narration: fix the dashes, cut one of the two Menzoberranzan intrusions, and diversify "clean" and the audit nouns, and the scene holds up.
