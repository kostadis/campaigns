# Chapter 60: I Have a Chair


---

## Grygum — The Silence of Candlekeep

Tadric was on his feet and breathing evenly. The healing had taken. It usually does when I'm the one applying it, and I don't advertise why.

He pressed the second key into our hands before anyone asked for it.

"I held it all night, and I didn't know what it was. Now that I know what it is, I'm not giving it to you and going to bed."

Fair. Wrong, but fair.

"You could be helpful," I said. "It's also dangerous." Both things were true. I said them in the order that was kindest.

"I faced Moziqodo! I faced the full… I… no, I, I, I will—"

The desperate desire to be heroic was all over him. I've seen it on acolytes. I've seen it on prisoners. You don't argue with it. You manage it.

"Well, come along. I do have resurrection magic if worst comes to worst."

He stared at me. "If Daral Yashenti was here—" and then, "But what about me?"

I answered the part I understood. "Well, I didn't have it then."

Theologically airtight. Judging by his face, not the comfort he was after.

And then the keep did something I had never heard it do.

The acoustics of Candlekeep carry sound a long way: stone corridors, high vaults, everything echoes into everything. What they carried to us, from far off, was an absence. The Endless Chant had stopped.

"Was this ever mentioned? I don't remember this."

"I mentioned it the first day we were there," Daz said. "Maybe."

He may have. Here is the honest accounting: I hadn't noticed the chant for the last three days. That is how a constant works on the mind. It becomes the room. Once it stopped, I noticed that it had stopped, the way you notice a heartbeat by its skipping.

The Avowed read the prophecies of Alaundo continuously. When a prophecy comes true, they delete it, and they keep reading. Which meant a silence had exactly two explanations. Either every prophecy left on the list had come true tonight, or nobody was left to read them.

Neither explanation improved with reflection. I put it in my notes anyway. Bahamut may or may not have been listening. The chant certainly wasn't.

Tadric had gone still. "This never happens," he said, and he said it like a man watching water run uphill.

Then his eyes found Moziqodo's body, and he arrived at the other problem.

"Sylvira Savikas… someone needs to tell Sylvira Savikas that her son is dead."

He looked at the four of us.

"Oh. Well. Ugh."

There was an awkward silence, and then, one at a time, the four of us looked at Dawnbringer. The news had several complicating features. We were most of them.

"It might best be someone who knows Sylvira Savikas," I said.

Daz picked it up smoothly — whoever told her would want to be empathetic, would have the best understanding of how to be empathetic to her as they explained this unfortunate news. It was passing the buck. We all knew it was passing the buck. Daz made it sound like pastoral care, which is a skill I respect professionally.

"I'm happy to, you know, back you up, but… I'll go with you," I told Tadric. An offer of support for a task no one was performing tonight costs very little. I made it sincerely. Both things can be true.

"We'll take care of it later," Daz said.

"Yeah," I said. "It's a later… it's a later—"

It was a later kind of problem. A'lai Aivenmore, up in the High Tower with the first key, waiting on a second key that was now in our possession — that was a now kind of problem. A mother's grief keeps. A man with one key and an appointment does not.

My old teacher used to interrupt at the worst possible moment to ask what, exactly, I thought I was measuring. Standing in a keep that had gone silent for the first time in centuries, holding a dead man's key, I could have told him: the tower. We were measuring the tower.

So we went. Tadric, fortunately, knew where the High Tower was, and he took the lead, walking a little too fast, a little too upright, a man determined to be in the story.

"I'll cast Bless," I said, "on the three of us who are here."

A small request for Bahamut's attention, made politely, spread as far as it goes. He may introduce himself properly one of these days. In the meantime, the warmth settled over us, and we followed Tadric into a keep that had, for the first time since Alaundo, nothing at all to say.

## Daz — The Path to the High Tower

Tadric handed over the second key and refused to stay behind.

I considered arguing and decided against it. A man who hands you a key and then insists on climbing the stairs after it is not going to be talked out of the stairs. And people who invite themselves along usually know something about the destination they haven't said yet. I let him come and I kept an eye on him, two halves of the same decision.

At the foot of the approach, Grygum stopped. "I was going to see if I wanted to prepare any… spells," he said, mostly to himself, and then, arriving somewhere: "Yeah, I will, I will cast… I'll cast Bless on… the three of us who are here."

Warmth settled across my shoulders like a hand. Sound allocation: cheap, broad, works on everything from a crossbow to a curse.

We headed toward the walkway. I saw the boots first, then the rest of him. A Watcher, down, not moving, some distance ahead, arranged the way bodies arrange themselves when nobody arranged them.

"Does he… does it… does he look conspicuously out of place, or that maybe he has some info?" I said, already walking.

He did not look out of place. He looked dead from an arrow.

"So the arrow is out of place, maybe."

"I think we spend a little bit of time investigating," I said.

I want to be precise about what that means. I did not squint, or pace, or study the floor for effect. The noticing does itself now. That is what the education was for. Milo Goodbarrel has a chapter on it, which I have read more times than I would admit: a constable glances at the unshaved back of a coachman's neck and knows the man's wife is dead, because a wife would have told him the back of his neck needed a shave. I used to think Milo was embellishing. Everything testifies, whether or not it is asked to.

*I can figure shit out.* That is the whole of it, stripped of its uniform.

*So — what do I aha about the arrow-ridden guard?*

The aha was the fletching.

The crossbow bolt in the Watcher was fletched wrong. Not from the area: not the pattern, not the cut. And not consistent with the bolts the Watchers themselves carry, which I had noticed on the way into the castle, because I notice armaments the way other people notice weather. Whoever fired this came from outside Candlekeep.

"Okay, so, knowing what we know, that probably means it's outside actors that are up to all this mischief," I said.

Grygum was already circling the body with his own questions. "How old… It was fired from outside? Are we on a wall? Was it fired from below?"

He looked up, checking for open sky and finding vaulted stone instead. "Okay, are we indoors?"

We were. The walkway ran inside the tower, and we had come up a flight of stairs to reach it. Grygum studied the ceiling, then the walls, arriving where I had already arrived.

"It's quite unusual for someone to be killed with an arrow indoors," he said.

It is. Indoor archery narrows the possibilities pleasantly. The bolt had entered him from the direction we were approaching, which meant whoever loosed it had climbed the same stairs we had just climbed, put a bolt in the Watcher, and kept advancing forward. We were not intercepting anyone. We were following them.

"The arrow was shot from the direction that we're coming from?" Grygum asked. "Yep. Yes. Okay."

He crouched beside the body. "Got it. So, I'm not a medical expert or anything, but… oh, well, actually, I am. I have plus 5 medicine." He touched the Watcher's jaw, then his hands, and made his assessment out loud, the whole way through, because he does. "How long ago do I think he was shot?" A pause. Not very long. Within the last half hour.

Half an hour. I added it to the running tally and did not enjoy the total: outside actors, foreign fletching, a route identical to ours, a lead of thirty minutes at most. A gap you could close, or a trap you could walk into, depending on who wrote the report afterward.

"Okay, so let's look out for somebody with a bow," Grygum said. "Cautiously, yeah."

We went on, slower now, spread out, prepared for anything, which is the polite way of saying prepared for nothing in particular. The stairs kept going up.

So did we.

## Zalthir — Infiltration of the High Tower Lobby

The watcher had died with an arrow in him, so we went up quietly.

The Avowed called this room the Skyscraping Lobby. For once a name was honest: shelves climbing into lamplight, a staircase along the wall, a second-floor landing, more open air than any room needs. Months of tunnel ceilings teach you that vertical rooms are honest about nothing except how far you can fall.

"Skyscraping?" Grygum whispered.

Then, working it out: "Oh, okay, it's the… the… yes. The entrance. Basically."

He was not wrong. A grand mouth with valuables in it and no honest cover.

Two raiders were working the shelves. One held a written manifest; the other pulled books and bagged them. Their swords had blood on them. They weren't looting. They were shopping. Someone had written that list, and the list was the interesting part.

"I suppose they haven't noticed us yet," Grygum said. They hadn't.

Up the stairs, on the landing, a wizard stood in front of a sealed door. We knew him. A'lai Aivenmore.

"Oh," Grygum breathed. "This is the guy who has the key."

"He is through the second door?"

He was not. The door behind him was still sealed, and he stood in front of it the way you stand at a gate waiting for a runner. He needed the second key. The second key was coming with Moziqodo. The other key was in Daz's pocket.

"Oh, okay," Grygum said. "So we have the other key."

I watched the wizard wait. Unhurried. Comfortable. The most dangerous person in a room isn't the one with the blade drawn. It's the one who has already decided they've won. A'lai Aivenmore had decided.

Daz, low: "So, it's safe to assume that one of these people that's in front of us is one of the people that shot the guard."

I looked again. A bow, slung across the second raider's back, half under the bag strap. It had been there the whole time and I had walked my eyes past it. There was a monastery class on inventorying a room before your foot crosses its threshold. Brother Aldas taught it — or possibly Brother Harren. I remember the light through the shutters. Not the content.

"Okay, well that clears up a mystery," Grygum said. "Good."

Then: "I'm more worried about the wizard than about these raiders. We're probably more than a match for them."

Right on both counts. Everything we had heard made A'lai Aivenmore a powerful wizard. And powerful wizards are more obsessed with staying alive than with fighting to the death. It is the most reliable thing about them.

"Just like dragons," Grygum said.

Thorin squinted up at the landing. "So is he the one with the third eye?"

The wizard had two. Nobody asked where Thorin had met one with three.

"One thing we could do, guys," Grygum said, "is try to eliminate the raiders so that we can focus on A'lai Aivenmore — so I could cast some kind of spell to try to get them in one spell casting. Because I suspect that we just need to fight them and kill them."

Daz said, "How tall is the wizard?"

"He wasn't expecting that question," Grygum murmured. Nobody had been.

Somewhere between five and six feet, by my eye. "Human," Daz said. Then: "I had a plan for maybe boxing — literally boxing the wizard in — while we took care of the other guys, just to make sure we were clear. But I think I'll wait on that. Maybe use it for something else. I like the plan of knocking down the DPS towards us by getting the two weaker guys out of the way."

"Excellent," Grygum said.

My concern was neither the raiders nor the wizard. From the doorway I could not see under the landing. I could not see behind the far shelves or the blind side of the stair. Rooms like this are never as empty as their visible portion, and the empty portion of this one was large. Grygum followed my eyes into the corners and said, "Oh."

The wizard stood ten, fifteen feet above the floor. He would see us the moment we crossed the threshold. He was watching the main door for Moziqodo, expecting him to run in at any point with the key.

"Well, he's going to be pretty surprised by us, then," Grygum said.

Glabbagool stirred against my left forearm. "Is this where we use the flying spell?"

I didn't answer. The staircase reached the landing, and there was shadow enough on the wall below it. I had cheaper ways up. The enthusiasm was new, though. I marked it.

Grygum was working his geometry. "So, with the spells that I've got, I could get both the thugs if I could get at the end of a line of them. Or I could target any one of the people in the room. They're not lined up for a good aim from the door, though. So maybe I should aim for A'lai Aivenmore instead, and then the rest of us can work on the thugs? What do you think, guys?"

"So what was the plan again?" Thorin said.

"Target A'lai Aivenmore instead of the thugs."

"Who? Me? You?"

"Me with my spell. I'm assuming that I can get a spell off before everybody realizes that we're there."

"Yeah, and I'm happy to sort of distract and try and kite the thugs a little bit."

Sound economy. Remove the cheap pieces first; isolate the expensive one. I said nothing, which was approval.

"So, should I try to cast a spell now?" Grygum said. "I was thinking Guiding Bolt, toward A'lai Aivenmore."

He held his breath and leaned into the doorway. The wizard hadn't noticed us. He'd heard feet on the stairs and thought nothing of it, because he was expecting feet on the stairs. The bolt of radiance crossed the lobby and broke a hand's width from the wizard's chest, scattering against a shimmer of warding. Mage armor. He had come dressed.

"I sort of jumped the gun on that," Grygum muttered. "Oops."

Beside me, Daz was already weighing the next thing — some working that would set the wizard's mind against his own craft. He studied the man on the landing, the composure, the warding he had worn into the room before anyone gave him a reason to, and let it go. A mind like that does not falter often enough to bet on.

"Alright," Daz said. "I'll probably not cast that unless we get desperate."

Desperate keeps its own schedule. I found a patch of shadow deep enough to step through, and waited for the room to answer.

## Daz — Confrontation at the High Tower

Powerful wizards share one trait with Menzoberranzan matrons: they are far more invested in staying alive than in dying well. A'lai Aivenmore would be no different, and that was the whole of my plan.

I ran the audit while the room broke into motion. Anything that tested his intellect would be a formality; his mind was the best-defended thing in this tower. His arms, though. Thirty-one years of lifting books. So: Maximilian's Earthen Grasp, a hand of compacted soil rising out of the flagstones to seize him, and let his body answer for what his mind could not.

He unraveled it mid-syllable.

"Oh, he can counterspell?"

I had never counterspelled anything in my life. It had honestly never come up.

But a reaction is a resource, and he had just spent one. When the next opening came, I cast the same spell again. The leverage was elegant: to stop my cheap spell he had to burn another of real weight, and either way, nothing bigger was coming out of him that round. He weighed it and chose to take it. The hand closed. He went down hard into its grip, battered and pinned.

"He can't use Misty Step, he's restrained," I said — and was wrong. The spell doesn't drag you through the space between; it simply puts you somewhere else. He appeared across the chamber, out of the hand's reach, breathing badly. Two of his good spells gone. No fireball was coming tonight. *At least we have that.*

"I restrained him with a hand that came out of the ground," I told Thorin. "He took a beating for it, and then Misty stepped his ass to the other side of the room, which got him out of the hand."

His answer was arcane bursts: the cheap, endless kind. Two of them slammed into me and I felt my margin shrink by a third in the space of a breath. Then he turned the same treatment on Zalthir, missed once, and landed one that staggered even the monk.

And then he stopped.

"You've been so thorough. I want that on the record before anything else happens, because I don't expect either of us will have time for compliments. In the moment."

"Oh, I always have time for compliments," Grygum said. "I mean, I also accept constructive criticism."

"Oh, sorry, I'm just being a smartass, but why did you feel a need to kill everybody? What, is it just megalomania? Are you just trying to improve your impact factor in the journals?"

"You understand me," A'lai said. And then it poured out of him. "Janussi gave me 10 days! Do you understand what he was offering me? Mercy! Genuinely, he thought 10 days was kind!"

"10 days for what?" Grygum asked.

"I have been at Candlekeep for 31 years. I do not have a house, or a family, or a country. I have a chair! He proposed to take the chair! And I found, I was surprised at this, that I would do a great deal. To keep it!"

"So he… this is just… this is the dude with the red stapler, and he snapped," I said. No one asked me to elaborate.

"I didn't set out to be a murderer. I didn't set out to be exiled."

"I understand now, you didn't… you were going to lose your academic honor and privilege," Grygum said.

"Right! Exactly! Thank you!"

"We should let this guy go. It's all justified."

"Thank you! Thank you!"

I watched Grygum work the man like a lockpick and decided to press. "Yeah, that's true. So, is there anything we can do to help you and to end this amicably, without us needing to destroy you and your thugs here?"

"You keep saying, I!" A'lai snapped. "I have been removing books from this library for 11 years, and I have never once chosen which ones. A list arrives, I fill it. The courier who brings the list wears a glove on his right hand, and under the glove the hand is metal, and I have never in 11 years worked up the nerve to ask why."

"Who… who do you work for, then?" Grygum asked.

"His name is Manshoon. You have not heard of it. That is not an accident. It is a career."

"Is this a multi-level marketing scheme?" Thorin said. "Copperware to sell us?"

I barely heard him, because the name was doing something in my head. *But that's not possible.* The Zhentarim. The Moonsea. The man who owned Zhentil Keep. A wizard who has been dead more than once. Being a Drow, you pick some stuff up.

"Well, if he's been dead more than once, then maybe he's gonna be dead one more time," I said.

A'lai produced the sapphire. "He wants one book out of this keep, and he was always going to have it. I was simply the version where nobody… had to die." He looked around the room: Bookwyrm, Janussi, the guards, and where Daral Yashenti had very nearly joined them. Then he amended himself. "Well, fewer people had to die."

Then he raised the sapphire to smash it.

*I have an alternative proposal.* Telekinesis — the heaviest thing I carry, and I spent it without hesitating. He strained against a grip he couldn't see, and lost, and the sapphire tore out of his fingers and crossed the room into my hand.

"You, you have the useful spell," Grygum said. "I wish I'd had something like that. Good job, though."

Now the shell game. I ducked back toward the hallway, every line of my body announcing retreat, Misty Stepped to close the gap, and stumbled into Grygum: clumsy, apologetic, and in the half-second of contact pressing the sapphire into his hand so that he knew exactly what he held. He had the other key already. If everything converged on him, everything would be in one trustworthy place. Nobody saw it. I noted the symmetry: Grygum had once handed me a fake key with a perfectly straight face, and here I was planting a real sapphire on him. The item was sorted into the correct drawer, and the drawer was an orc.

"I now have one key and one sapphire," Grygum murmured.

A'lai, discovering himself robbed, came apart. Bolts of raw arcana filled the room, and most of them found Zalthir. The monk dropped where he stood. Glabbagool flowed over him and refused to move.

"That's my bad. I was a little bitch and grabbed the sapphire and then ran away."

"So I think what I'm gonna do is… try and actually just take out the thugs, because I don't want them sort of creating chaos," Thorin said. "What do you guys think?" Up close, the raiders resolved into what they were: Zhentarim men, hired hands of the same career we'd just been introduced to. Dawnbringer bit into one, bright but not blazing. "Because it's not undead, they're not undead, right?" Thorin asked, mid-swing, of no one. He snarled something meant to break the man's nerve. The man's nerve held; he was Zhentarim, after all. So Thorin simply hit him again.

Grygum charged past me and threw Tasha's Caustic Brew across both thugs, his blessing lapsing as he let it go. Neither man was fast enough. One of them dissolved at the start of his next step, dead before he understood the problem. The other stayed on his feet, dripping.

"I will note that they're covered in acid," Grygum said.

The surviving thug stood there, smoking. Zalthir lay under Glabbagool. And somewhere across the room, A'lai Aivenmore was still standing, empty-handed and very upset about it.
