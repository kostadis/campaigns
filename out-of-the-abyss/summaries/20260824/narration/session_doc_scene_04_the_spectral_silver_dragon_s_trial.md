We were on our way to the stairs when somebody coughed.

"That's not the guy that just questioned us, somebody else," Daz said. "Where's it coming from?"

Outside. Behind us. From the chamber where the lava ran.

"Like, sorry, outside, like, where we came from, behind us?" Zalthir asked. "Can we see who's coughing?"

Nobody volunteered to walk back toward the lava. "No, just turn around and look," Zalthir said.

We turned around and looked.

A spectral silver dragon stood in the lava chamber. The lava ran straight through her and came out the other side, still lava, entirely unimproved by the passage.

"That's cool," I said. And then, because a cleric should be precise about miracles: "Per… through what part of her?"

Through the middle of her. In one flank and out the other. She watched us watch it happen, and she looked very grouchy about the whole arrangement.

"I would be if lava was going through me, too," I said.

"Someone entered this chamber without being a reader," she said.

"Or would, like, a Candlekeep avowed or something? Is that what they're upset about?" Daz asked.

"NO!"

"A reader of what?" Daz tried, and then offered his own reading history as a credential. It did not move her.

I tried a guess of my own. "…pointed as a reader or something?" And then the absurdity of it caught up with me. "You mean you're, like, 1500 feet below the Candlekeep, and you're worried about academic appointments?" I had not expected the dead to keep up with titles.

She corrected the record herself.

"Manshoon entered my keep."

"Oh — that guy," Daz said.

That guy exactly.

"We don't like him either," I said. Common cause travels fast with dragons. Also, it was true.

"Because the protections fell when he entered. Okay," Daz said. "What room did they enter?"

"He's still inside."

"Interesting. Do you know where?"

"No."

"We would like to… remove them," Daz said.

"You would?"

"That is part of our goal, yeah."

"Well, I'm glad to hear that."

While she warmed to us by a degree or two, something else settled into place. She was in the poem. She was one of the verses.

"One of the verses — oh, of the poem," Daz said.

"Let me pull it back up," I said. I had the verse written down. Of course I had the verse written down.

Thorin recited it. "To verify the chosen reader's claims." And then: "But those well-versed in Candlekeep's lore may fearlessly pass the obsidian door."

"Is… oh, is… Have we already seen an obsidian door? Is there one here?" I asked. We had. We had just walked through it.

"So which one is she, then?" Zalthir asked.

She was the unseen ears.

"Oh, so she's the one who dropped the floor under us?"

She was. And we had said the magic words — she was the last line of the verse.

"She's seen now, so…" Zalthir said, and let the paradox lie where it fell.

"One of you is the reader, the rest of you are witness. Which is which? And say it in the right order."

"Oh — one of us read the, read the words," I said.

"Or is Reader, like, a—" Thorin started. "…a title in the keep, right?"

"But we read the words aloud, I think," I said.

"Someone did," Zalthir said.

"I have a feeling it was me, but I don't remember for sure," I said. It had been me. The words had gone out of my mouth and the stairs had not eaten us afterward, which is the kind of correlation a working priest learns to respect.

"Alright, I am the reader," I said. "I can read it again if you'd like."

"Tell me something you know about Candlekeep."

Anything would do — the Endless Chant, the books, any honest proof of knowing. So I talked about books. The ones we had looked into, the ones we had chased through this keep lately, the ones other people had gone to some trouble to keep hidden. I have spent this whole visit up to my elbows in other people's shelves. It made for good material.

"Very well, reader!"

She paused. "You may… Grant a request."

"Wait," Zalthir said. "So we'll get a request."

"Does she know anything about this whole murder mystery? There were some loose ends that we needed to tie up," I said.

The menu arrived instead. She could hold the door — nothing leaves the whole area until she says so. Zalthir floated the idea of asking her to join the party outright.

Thorin, meanwhile, had a professional question. "Did you ever fight the — the owlbear?"

"No."

"Like, no, we grabbed lunch together," Thorin supplied, on her behalf. "He's my bud, what are you talking about?"

Hold the door, then. Or fight beside us.

"Well, if the bad guy's already in there, right? Like, we don't need her to hold the door. Hold it against whom?" Thorin said.

Against leaving. Manshoon could slip out while we were busy dying somewhere below.

"Oh."

"Could — could she join forces with us long enough to try to defeat, what, the guy who's down here before us?" I asked. Zalthir pointed out that fighting beside us had already been on the table.

"Yeah, I think that's more useful than just standing there waiting, like, playing goalie, you know what I'm saying?" Thorin said.

"I think so, too," I said, and I put the request to her plainly: fight beside us. Help us defeat the Interloper.

"Wait, wait, sorry, hold on — what's that middle option?" Zalthir said.

"Are we all who we say we are? Oh — is one of our party, like, not where they're supposed to be?" Thorin said.

"Are we a mimic? Like, what's it?" Zalthir said.

"The… the useless dude?" Thorin offered.

"Oh, is the useless dude the bad guy, maybe?" Daz said. "Or somebody replaced Glabbagool with a mimic?"

Glabbagool would be wounded to his center to hear it. He is carrying a key inside himself and he is proud of the responsibility.

"But don't we have some mystery about one of us? I'm trying to remember who," I said. There was one. Daz. The patron, the struck name, the coin that follows him around the Underdark like weather.

"Boom," Zalthir said.

"Maybe we could find out more about Daz's mystery," I said. "Oh — 'tell us what he took' is interesting too, because presumably he came down here for a reason. And we don't know why."

"But she already said she doesn't know where he is. Would she know what he took?" Zalthir said.

"I mean, those are different questions. If you took something and left, I'm sure," I said.

She sat there. Waiting for one of us to ask something.

"Do we need to figure out what we want to ask?" Daz said. "Can we ask more than one? Is there a chance for us to…"

Her irritation came off her like heat off the lava. "Well, since you don't know what to ask for, and it's been centuries since anyone came here." She looked at Zalthir. "Look, the calculating type." She looked at Daz. "And you do look as if you're always searching for an angle."

And then she noticed me. Cleric of Bahamut. Standing right there.

"Tis true," I said.

"And you are a cleric of the great god Bahamut, the platinum god who soars above all, and…"

"I dare not deny it."

"Yes, and you are… are you an orc? I can never remember."

"Yes."

"And an orc, no less."

"Well, the orcs have occasionally allied with the dragons," I said. The precedent is thin. It is not zero. I stood on it anyway.

Then the real offer. She could hold the curtain — widen the anti-magic band to thirty feet, for ten minutes. Against a wizard, that is not a curtain. That is a confiscation.

"That could be very, very true," Daz said.

"It does sound very useful, yeah," Thorin said.

She paused. Paused longer. "Where do you stand on the question of the age?"

There is exactly one question in all of Faerûn I have an answer to that is entirely mine.

"Yes — I founded a new school that holds that there are certain fixed points among which we may make our own paths," I said.

She paused again. "Why, wisdom — wisdom truly flows from Bahamut into you, cleric of Bahamut."

Vareth publishes my theology under his own gloss and collects the credit topside. A Sentinel Wyrm of Candlekeep, dead for centuries, heard the doctrine once and called it wisdom flowing from Bahamut. Bahamut may or may not have been listening all these years. She was. I would enter that in the notes exactly as she said it.

"I— I feel truly inspired. Right, okay," I said. "Witness the power of my God."

She contemplated, and laid it all out. Hold the curtain — extend the anti-magic zone. Prevent Manshoon from teleporting out. Tell us what he took. Tell us whether everyone was who they said they were. And to fighting beside us: "I was bound to be witness, not weapon. If I could have stopped him, I would have stopped him at dawn."

"Oh," Zalthir said.

An observer, then. The menu was down to four.

"You know, this whole—" I started. "…anti-magic thing sounds terribly useful against a powerful wizard. As much as it inconveniences me, I'll just fight in other ways." Inside that curtain I am an orc with a mace and a loud opinion. Outside it, Manshoon is Manshoon.

"It inconveniences me, too, but…" Daz said. "Like, if it's a super powerful wizard, it's probably not a great idea for us to be going up."

"Absolutely," I said. Manshoon has prepared longer than any of us have been alive. Protanther studied one chessboard for four hundred years and lost to a dwarf who did not know the rules. Preparation has blind spots. A curtain that unmakes magic hands him a board he has never studied.

"Can she shape the field so that… our…" Daz began. She could not. Extend only. And the price of the curtain was said out loud where we could all hear it: Manshoon could escape. We were trading the lock on the exits for the silence inside. We made the trade with our eyes open.

"Well, controlling where people move is kind of my thing," Zalthir said.

"Let him try," the dragon said.

"Oh, snap," Zalthir said.

"Let him try."

"But can we delay it so we can surprise—" Zalthir asked. We could. She would hold the curtain until we called for it. Banked, not spent.

"Seems… seems like a good idea," I said.

She smiled. "Well chosen! Well chosen!"

"Well chosen."
