We came into the chamber and the air did that thing where a room is holding its breath at you. I've walked into enough held-breath rooms by now.

"Nothing happens, right?"

Nothing happened. Grygum announced the anticlimax out loud, like he was already writing the report, and honestly the report was accurate.

The room had one feature worth reading: a tiny iron statue of a winged owlbear sitting on top of an eight-foot stone pedestal, with a staircase running up behind it to the next floor. A statue parked in front of the only way up isn't decoration. That's a checkpoint.

Then the big iron eyelids opened. White, glowing eyes. The wings unfolded and the thing flew straight up and hovered twelve feet off the ground, which for a statue the size of a lapdog is a solid entrance.

"Halt, Seekers! Before you consult the mysteries stored herein, you must prove yourselves a worthy keeper of secrets. Three questions you must answer before I let you pass."

"Tip-top A-plus DnD right here," I said. I meant it as a compliment.

"Is that right?" Grygum said.

"Questions three, you must answer."

The owlbear leaned all the way in. "Yes, three questions you must answer. Not two, not one, not zero. If thou shalt answer zero, one, or two, bad things shall happen."

"Where is the only refuge for flames in Candlekeep?"

"Do we know that from that word puzzle thing that we had?" Daz said.

"Was it on there?" Zalthir said.

"Everybody write down the text for that?"

"I know you told us about this at one point," I said, "but I don't remember." True then, true now.

Daz got there — the hearth. The one place in this whole keep he hadn't been cold. Grygum arrived at the same answer by his own road, a beat behind. Good. That's two roads to the same rock.

"Well done! Well done! Well done!"

Edvaldo said he'd known that one, then relocated himself: "If you're going to answer the questions, I'm going to just… over here." Smart man. Don't stand where the questions can hit you.

"Which holy triumvirate patrons the great library?"

"Oh, I'm good at that," Grygum said.

I was not. Gods aren't my ground. "I got nothing to add," I said, and meant that too.

Grygum had it cold — Oghma, Deneir, and Milil. "Of course I remember that." And then I watched him hold back the whole lecture on how the three of them founded the place. You could see what the holding cost him.

The third question, the guardian announced, was the most difficult of all. Then: "Once upon a time… Every member… oh, sorry."

"Comment and a question," I said, hand up.

"Sorry, sorry — I was reading from the answer key. My mistake."

An answer key. I shut my mouth.

"It's been a while, it's been a while, it's been a while — but don't you worry. If you fail, I will tear you to shreds and destroy you. I've been waiting a long time for this…" It gathered itself. "I've been waiting a long time to do this. This is something I have trained for in my head."

"That would be no less than your duty," Grygum said, grave as a funeral.

"Exactly. I was built for this!"

"How many feet does the High Tower of Exaltation reach above the sea?"

"Would we know that?" Zalthir said.

"We got, like, all the scholars with us, right?" One scholar, technically. But still.

"Yeah, but this Candlekeep scholar — he would."

We all turned and stared at Edvaldo Sedanur. Apparently every member of the Avowed had once been expected to know this cold.

"Evaldo, this seems like the sort of question that you would know the answer to," Grygum said.

"I'm sure they all have a slightly different answer, maybe," Daz said.

But memorizing dry details had fallen out of fashion around here. "So you guys don't know," Daz said.

"But — but I do have, I do have… I do know something." Edvaldo squinted. "It's over 500 feet?"

"It isn't some kind of trick — like the tower is actually below sea level, or something like that, right?" Grygum asked. Nobody could say why a tower's height counted as a question about nature, either. Zalthir shrugged. Reasons.

Over five hundred was enough of an anchor. Height is terrain, and I've spent my whole life standing at the bottom of big things knowing exactly how big. I got there. Daz got there too.

"You have answered all three questions correctly. You may pass."

"Cool," Daz said.

"Could we have taken him?" I asked.

"I mean, I was tempted to just try anyway," Zalthir said. Then: "We want to, Thorin? Could be fun."

"You know, what you got on him?"

"You could… you could still just attack him if he failed," Grygum offered.

"But we don't know what's ahead. We don't want to, like… This is the — this is the problem with DnD. You're always like, should I use this spell, or should I save it for later? And you never know."

"I mean, we can always… at this point, we can save doing something stupid for later," Grygum said.

"That's fair," Zalthir said. "Maybe he'll stay here. Worst case, get him on the way back."

The giants had a saying: a rock in the wrong place is just a rock, but a rock in the right place is a wall. The owlbear was a rock in the right place. You don't knock those down for fun.

So instead I made conversation. Asked it what was next, whether its duties were done down here. "When do you get off?"

"Yeah, do you think these guys have shifts?" Grygum said.

"Although this was all very entertaining," the guardian said, and let the rest of the sentence go where sentences go when an iron owlbear is done with you.

Later — after the dragon, coming back through — we noticed what we'd walked right past the first time: a damaged owlbear lying broken on the floor. Manshoon had destroyed the guardian, and a new one—

"Took his…" Daz said.

Took his spot. Our riddle-master was the replacement. *I was built for this,* it had told us. The one before it probably said the same thing once. I kept that.
