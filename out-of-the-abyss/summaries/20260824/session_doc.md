# Chapter 65: The Long Descent


---

## Zalthir — The Long Descent

Thorin was falling.

The stairs had done exactly what Thorin suspected they would. A section the length of a cart hinged open under everyone standing between the ninety-third and the hundred-and-second step. Daz, safely above, threw Telekinesis at the problem and arrested the fall; the rest of us got a hand on stone and scrambled clear. Thorin didn't. He lost his footing entirely and went down into the well, and the dark took him whole.

Grygum went through his prayers, looking for one that reached that far down.

"Nothing."

I was already at the edge. The plan, such as it was, was to grab him. I have wings. I almost never use them: I position, then I walk the last distance. This was a position problem, and the position was down.

I stepped off.

Grygum's voice came after me: "Are… are you trying to say you can fall faster than him?"

A fair question.

Glabbagool, fused to my forearm, screamed "WEE!" Somewhere far below, thin and receding, Dawnbringer: "NOT THIS AGAIN!"

Thorin had gone in with nothing left. Every out he had, spent on the way through the gap. Everything anyone tried had failed, all of it, at once. It was almost impressive.

There was a class about this at the monastery: aerial pursuit, or falling with intent, something in that family. Brother Ostrin taught it, or possibly the short one with the burn scar. I wasn't listening. What I knew without the class was simple enough: wings do not catch a body that is already falling. You catch a faller by falling with him. So I folded the wings and dropped.

The dark was total. Good. Total dark is my ground. Five hundred feet down the shaft stopped being a distance and became a door, and I remembered I could teleport. Shadow Step. I stepped through the dark and came out beside Thorin, and he grabbed me. Held on, both arms, an actual hug. We spread out into a controlled fall together.

Glabbagool offered that one should always be Zen.

"Yes, we're very… we're very zen at the moment," I said.

Glabbagool wondered whether this was too much hugging.

"There are worse things about Google," I said.

Thorin patted his chest and established, to his own satisfaction, that he was not dead. Then, in the ooze's defense: "I mean, Glabbagool's all about the hugging, let's be clear." And, warming to it: "What is this oozing without being ooze?"

Below us the well kept going. Another five hundred feet, at least.

"I mean, I think the point was to go down, wasn't it?" I said. "How about we go gracefully down and just see what's there?"

From far above, Grygum: "Hey, you guys okay down there?"

"So far."

Thorin shouted up that he was still kicking. The scholar we'd dragged along called "Come back!" down the shaft, and Grygum shouted something the well ate half of.

"We could at least go scout a bit," I said.

"Sounds good," Thorin said.

We dropped the last five hundred feet and the air caught us. Thick, sudden, gentle. A featherfall woven into the bottom of the shaft, sitting there the whole time like a net under a rope walk.

"Oh," I said. "So there was no danger here."

The well did not apologize.

We shouted the news up, and then came the jumping.

"Oh, God, that's gonna be hard for me," Grygum said.

"Literally? Like, should someone push you, or…"

"I'll just say a quick prayer, and I don't know… Take it from me." A pause, the length of a prayer. "And… and hope that the featherfall works for me, too."

He jumped on faith. It has not failed him yet.

Daz went next, reasoning it out loud on the way over the edge. "Yeah, I mean, I don't want to get split from the party. My whole, like, trying to hold the thing up and save us all didn't work, so…"

"If all your friends jumped off the stairs, would you?" Grygum asked.

"Yeah," Daz said.

"Oh, shit," I said.

Then Edvaldo, his voice wobbling down the shaft: "I've — you know, this is… this is an area that has been… I didn't even know it existed."

"Come on in, the water's fine," Thorin called up.

"I intend to write a very long monograph."

He jumped too. The featherfall took them one by one and set them down soft, a thousand feet below anything Candlekeep had a map for.

A single tunnel led straight ahead. The point, as I'd said, was to go down. Here we were.

## Daz — The Lava Chamber and the Obsidian Tower

The heat arrived before the cavern did. The passage exhaled it at us, thick and acrid and sweltering, and then the walls opened and we were standing on a ledge halfway up a cylindrical chamber, two hundred feet floor to ceiling, a hundred across. A hundred feet below the ledge, a lake of molten lava steamed. It was the only light in the room, and also the only way to die in it. Efficient.

Directly ahead, in the center of the chamber, stood a three-floor stone tower, similar in style to some of the older spires in the Great Library. It sat on a thin pedestal of glassy black obsidian that soared straight up out of the lava pool.

Grygum considered it. "In the… lava? Yes."

A fair question. Whoever raised the pedestal had an answer; the answer was not posted anywhere I could see.

Edvaldo had an answer, though. Edvaldo has an answer for everything, and he delivers all of them. "Oh, yes! This is a classic example—" and he was off, lecturing on an architectural style invented by some Candlekeep architect whose name the lava swallowed. "Many of these samples have been destroyed as a result of needless rehabilitation and expansion of towers as we fought for a constrained space in the library." He took in the tower, the pedestal, the molten lake beneath it, and rendered his verdict: "This, in and of itself, was worth the trip."

The trip was not over. I chose not to point this out.

Instead I said, mostly to Grygum, "I'm pretty sure there are some spells that allow you to, like, listen to what somebody's saying from across the room. They need to have spells that allow you to just mute people around you."

Grygum said nothing, which from him is encouragement.

"Or, like, zapping it, you know?" I sketched the gesture in the hot air. "Mixer board for everybody that's standing around you."

Somewhere in the eight schools there is room for it. Sorcere would have refused to teach it on principle: in Menzoberranzan, the right to talk over other people is a rank, and nobody sells the counterspell to underclassmen.

Edvaldo lectured on.

The rest of the inventory, while he talked: a double door of the same glassy obsidian stood opposite us, across a gap too wide to jump, with no obvious way of getting there. A narrow walkway ringed the tower's base, and from the back of it a flat stone bridge ran to a second tunnel, opposite the one we had come down.

"And we'd… we'd have to figure out how to get over to it somehow," Grygum said.

The ledge we stood on extended barely two paces into the cavern, and embedded in its stone were cogs. Mechanical components, set flush into the rock.

"Are mechanical components embedded in the stone?" I said.

We had come down here chasing mechanist dust, among other things, and we were carrying the mechanist tool out of the House of Mechanus. When the environment starts rhyming with your inventory, someone designed the environment.

"So, I think it would be worthwhile looking at the mechanical components," Grygum said. "Any idea what they're for?"

Everyone looked at me. Correctly.

I got down on the stone and did the full audit. Gear train, pawl, ratchet. A toothed track that disappeared under the lip of the ledge and, by its gauge and bearing, ran out beneath the open air toward the tower. At the heart of the assembly, a socket: cut for no hand, no crowbar, and no spell. Cut for exactly the tool in our pack. The conclusion assembled itself: the whole mechanism existed to extend the bridge.

"Oh, well, that would be more convenient than just trying to jump across the lava," Grygum said.

"So if we use the tool, we can basically expand the bridge. Yes, yes. And we don't have to… okay."

"Try it."

"Let's do it."

It was while seating the tool that I found the plaque, one of those warnings this place leaves like a signature. The entire airspace was surrounded by an anti-magic field.

So: no flying anyone across. No Misty Step, no Feather Fall as insurance, no levitating Edvaldo out over the lake and leaving him there. A span of open air in which everything I have spent my life learning is a rumor. Fortunate we brought the tool. The tool did not care what I thought about that.

"Wasn't that supposed to be this whole, this whole central area?" Grygum asked. "But I guess we… what we found out was that it was disabled anyway."

Different field. The one over Candlekeep was a force wall: it stopped teleportation passing in or out, and it stopped fire from being cast, and it was already down. This one was local and general. It did not discriminate by school. It simply refused.

I turned the tool. The mechanism woke, and the bridge ground out across the gap, stone dragging over stone, loud enough to introduce us to anything in the tower with ears.

We crossed. The double doors opened, and we were inside. The tower's stone walls were unadorned except for several sconces, lit with continual flame spells.

And four mounted crossbows.

Unadorned, except for the murder fixtures. I kept my eyes on them while the back of my mind worked a smaller problem: the sconces were burning, and continual flame is a spell. If the field owned this airspace, those sconces should have been dead stone. They weren't, and from the doorway I could read why. The field's reach was shorter than the plaque implied. The near third of the crossing, not the whole of it.

"Still needed the bridge, but we have some magic at the edges," I said.

*At least we have that.*

## Thorin Giantfriend — Riddles of the Iron Guardian

We came into the chamber and the air did that thing where a room is holding its breath at you. I've walked into enough held-breath rooms by now.

"Nothing happens, right?"

Nothing happened. Grygum announced the anticlimax out loud, like he was already writing the report, and honestly the report was accurate.

The room had one feature worth reading: a tiny iron statue of a winged owlbear sitting on top of a stone pedestal taller than a man, with a staircase running up behind it to the next floor. A statue parked in front of the only way up isn't decoration. That's a checkpoint.

Then the big iron eyelids opened. White, glowing eyes. The wings unfolded and the thing flew straight up and hovered out of easy reach overhead, which for a statue the size of a lapdog is a solid entrance.

"Halt, Seekers! Before you consult the mysteries stored herein, you must prove yourselves a worthy keeper of secrets. Three questions you must answer before I let you pass."

"Tip-top A-plus DnD right here," I said. I meant it as a compliment.

"Is that right?" Grygum said.

"Questions three, you must answer."

The owlbear leaned all the way in. "Yes, three questions you must answer. Not two, not one, not zero. If thou shalt answer zero, one, or two, bad things shall happen."

"Where is the only refuge for flames in Candlekeep?"

"Do we know that from that word puzzle thing that we had?" Daz said.

"Was it on there?" Zalthir said.

"Everybody write down the text for that?"

"I know you told us about this at one point," I said, "but I don't remember." True then, true now.

Daz got there. The hearth. The one place in this whole keep he hadn't been cold. Grygum arrived at the same answer by his own road, a beat behind. Good. That's two roads to the same rock.

"Well done! Well done! Well done!"

Edvaldo said he'd known that one, then relocated himself: "If you're going to answer the questions, I'm going to just… over here." Smart man. Don't stand where the questions can hit you.

"Which holy triumvirate patrons the great library?"

"Oh, I'm good at that," Grygum said.

I was not. Gods aren't my ground. "I got nothing to add," I said, and meant that too.

Grygum had it cold: Oghma, Deneir, and Milil. "Of course I remember that." And then I watched him hold back the whole lecture on how the three of them founded the place. You could see what the holding cost him.

The third question, the guardian announced, was the most difficult of all. Then: "Once upon a time… Every member… oh, sorry."

"Comment and a question," I said, hand up.

"Sorry, sorry — I was reading from the answer key. My mistake."

An answer key. I shut my mouth.

"It's been a while, it's been a while, it's been a while — but don't you worry. If you fail, I will tear you to shreds and destroy you. I've been waiting a long time for this…" It gathered itself. "I've been waiting a long time to do this. This is something I have trained for in my head."

"That would be no less than your duty," Grygum said, grave as a funeral.

"Exactly. I was built for this!"

"How many feet does the High Tower of Exaltation reach above the sea?"

"Would we know that?" Zalthir said.

"We got, like, all the scholars with us, right?" One scholar, technically. But still.

"Yeah, but this Candlekeep scholar — he would."

We all turned and stared at Edvaldo Sedanur. Apparently every member of the Avowed had once been expected to know this cold.

"Evaldo, this seems like the sort of question that you would know the answer to," Grygum said.

"I'm sure they all have a slightly different answer, maybe," Daz said.

But memorizing dry details had fallen out of fashion around here. "So you guys don't know," Daz said.

"But — but I do have, I do have… I do know something." Edvaldo squinted. "It's over 500 feet?"

"It isn't some kind of trick — like the tower is actually below sea level, or something like that, right?" Grygum asked. Nobody could say why a tower's height counted as a question about nature, either. Zalthir shrugged. Nobody pushed it.

Over five hundred was enough of an anchor. Height is terrain, and I've spent my whole life standing at the bottom of big things knowing exactly how big. I got there. Daz got there too.

"You have answered all three questions correctly. You may pass."

"Cool," Daz said.

"Could we have taken him?" I asked.

"I mean, I was tempted to just try anyway," Zalthir said. Then: "We want to, Thorin? Could be fun."

"You know, what you got on him?"

"You could… you could still just attack him if he failed," Grygum offered.

"But we don't know what's ahead. We don't want to, like… This is the — this is the problem with DnD. You're always like, should I use this spell, or should I save it for later? And you never know."

"I mean, we can always… at this point, we can save doing something stupid for later," Grygum said.

"That's fair," Zalthir said. "Maybe he'll stay here. Worst case, get him on the way back."

The giants had a saying: a rock in the wrong place is just a rock, but a rock in the right place is a wall. The owlbear was a rock in the right place. You don't knock those down for fun.

So instead I made conversation. Asked it what was next, whether its duties were done down here. "When do you get off?"

"Yeah, do you think these guys have shifts?" Grygum said.

"Although this was all very entertaining," the guardian said, and didn't finish. Iron owlbears don't have to.

Later, after the dragon, coming back through, we noticed what we'd walked right past the first time: a damaged owlbear lying broken on the floor. Manshoon had destroyed the guardian, and a new one…

"Took his…" Daz said.

Took his spot. Our riddle-master was the replacement. *I was built for this,* it had told us. The one before it probably said the same thing once. I kept that.

## Grygum — The Spectral Silver Dragon's Trial

We were on our way to the stairs when somebody coughed.

"That's not the guy that just questioned us, somebody else," Daz said. "Where's it coming from?"

Outside. Behind us. From the chamber where the lava ran.

"Like, sorry, outside, like, where we came from, behind us?" Zalthir asked. "Can we see who's coughing?"

Nobody volunteered to walk back toward the lava. "No, just turn around and look," Zalthir said.

We turned around and looked.

A spectral silver dragon stood in the lava chamber. The lava ran straight through her and came out the other side, still lava, entirely unimproved by the passage.

"That's cool," I said. And then, because a cleric should be precise about miracles: "Per… through what part of her?"

Through the middle of her. In one flank and out the other. She watched us watch it happen, and she looked very grouchy about the whole arrangement.

"I would be if lava was going through me, too," I said.

"Someone entered this chamber without being a reader," she said.

"Or would, like, a Candlekeep avowed or something? Is that what they're upset about?" Daz asked.

"NO!"

"A reader of what?" Daz tried, and then offered his own reading history as a credential. It did not move her.

I tried a guess of my own. "…pointed as a reader or something?" And then the absurdity of it caught up with me. "You mean you're, like, a quarter-mile below Candlekeep, and you're worried about academic appointments?" I had not expected the dead to keep up with titles.

She corrected the record herself.

"Manshoon entered my keep."

"Oh — that guy," Daz said.

That guy exactly.

"We don't like him either," I said. Common cause travels fast with dragons. Also, it was true.

"Because the protections fell when he entered. Okay," Daz said. "What room did they enter?"

"He's still inside."

"Interesting. Do you know where?"

"No."

"We would like to… remove them," Daz said.

"You would?"

"That is part of our goal, yeah."

"Well, I'm glad to hear that."

While she warmed to us by a degree or two, something else settled into place. She was in the poem. She was one of the verses.

"One of the verses — oh, of the poem," Daz said.

"Let me pull it back up," I said. I had the verse written down. Of course I had the verse written down.

Thorin recited it. "To verify the chosen reader's claims." And then: "But those well-versed in Candlekeep's lore may fearlessly pass the obsidian door."

"Is… oh, is… Have we already seen an obsidian door? Is there one here?" I asked. We had. We had just walked through it.

"So which one is she, then?" Zalthir asked.

She was the unseen ears.

"Oh, so she's the one who dropped the floor under us?"

She was. And we had said the magic words: she was the last line of the verse.

"She's seen now, so…" Zalthir said, and let the paradox lie where it fell.

"One of you is the reader, the rest of you are witness. Which is which? And say it in the right order."

"Oh — one of us read the, read the words," I said.

"Or is Reader, like, a—" Thorin started. "…a title in the keep, right?"

"But we read the words aloud, I think," I said.

"Someone did," Zalthir said.

"I have a feeling it was me, but I don't remember for sure," I said. It had been me. The words had gone out of my mouth and the stairs had not eaten us afterward. I have learned to respect a correlation like that.

"Alright, I am the reader," I said. "I can read it again if you'd like."

"Tell me something you know about Candlekeep."

Anything would do: the Endless Chant, the books, any honest proof of knowing. So I talked about books. The ones we had looked into, the ones we had chased through this keep lately, the ones other people had gone to some trouble to keep hidden. I have spent this whole visit up to my elbows in other people's shelves. It made for good material.

"Very well, reader!"

She paused. "You may… Grant a request."

"Wait," Zalthir said. "So we'll get a request."

"Does she know anything about this whole murder mystery? There were some loose ends that we needed to tie up," I said.

The menu arrived instead. One offer was the door. Nothing leaves the whole area until she says so. Zalthir floated the idea of asking her to join the party outright.

Thorin, meanwhile, had a professional question. "Did you ever fight the — the owlbear?"

"No."

"Like, no, we grabbed lunch together," Thorin supplied, on her behalf. "He's my bud, what are you talking about?"

Hold the door, then. Or fight beside us.

"Well, if the bad guy's already in there, right? Like, we don't need her to hold the door. Hold it against whom?" Thorin said.

Against leaving. Manshoon could slip out while we were busy dying somewhere below.

"Oh."

"Could — could she join forces with us long enough to try to defeat, what, the guy who's down here before us?" I asked. Zalthir pointed out that fighting beside us had already been on the table.

"Yeah, I think that's more useful than just standing there waiting, like, playing goalie, you know what I'm saying?" Thorin said.

"I think so, too," I said, and I put the request to her plainly: fight beside us. Help us defeat the Interloper.

"Wait, wait, sorry, hold on — what's that middle option?" Zalthir said.

"Are we all who we say we are? Oh — is one of our party, like, not where they're supposed to be?" Thorin said.

"Are we a mimic? Like, what's it?" Zalthir said.

"The… the useless dude?" Thorin offered.

"Oh, is the useless dude the bad guy, maybe?" Daz said. "Or somebody replaced Glabbagool with a mimic?"

Glabbagool would be wounded to his center to hear it. He is carrying a key inside himself and he is proud of the responsibility.

"But don't we have some mystery about one of us? I'm trying to remember who," I said. There was one. Daz. The patron, the struck name, the coin that follows him around the Underdark like weather.

"Boom," Zalthir said.

"Maybe we could find out more about Daz's mystery," I said. "Oh — 'tell us what he took' is interesting too, because presumably he came down here for a reason. And we don't know why."

"But she already said she doesn't know where he is. Would she know what he took?" Zalthir said.

"I mean, those are different questions. If you took something and left, I'm sure," I said.

She sat there. Waiting for one of us to ask something.

"Do we need to figure out what we want to ask?" Daz said. "Can we ask more than one? Is there a chance for us to…"

Her irritation came off her like heat off the lava. "Well, since you don't know what to ask for, and it's been centuries since anyone came here." She looked at Zalthir. "Look, the calculating type." She looked at Daz. "And you do look as if you're always searching for an angle."

And then she noticed me. Cleric of Bahamut. Standing right there.

"Tis true," I said.

"And you are a cleric of the great god Bahamut, the platinum god who soars above all, and…"

"I dare not deny it."

"Yes, and you are… are you an orc? I can never remember."

"Yes."

"And an orc, no less."

"Well, the orcs have occasionally allied with the dragons," I said. The precedent is thin. It is not zero. I stood on it anyway.

Then the real offer. She could hold the curtain: widen the dead band to cover the whole bridge, and hold it long enough to matter. Against a wizard, that is not a curtain. That is a confiscation.

"That could be very, very true," Daz said.

"It does sound very useful, yeah," Thorin said.

She paused. Paused longer. "Where do you stand on the question of the age?"

There is exactly one question in all of Faerûn I have an answer to that is entirely mine.

"Yes — I founded a new school that holds that there are certain fixed points among which we may make our own paths," I said.

She paused again. "Why, wisdom — wisdom truly flows from Bahamut into you, cleric of Bahamut."

Vareth publishes my theology under his own gloss and collects the credit topside. A Sentinel Wyrm of Candlekeep, dead for centuries, heard the doctrine once and called it wisdom flowing from Bahamut. Bahamut may or may not have been listening all these years. She was. I would enter that in the notes exactly as she said it.

"I— I feel truly inspired. Right, okay," I said. "Witness the power of my God."

She contemplated, and laid it all out. The curtain, then. Extend the anti-magic zone. Prevent Manshoon from teleporting out. Tell us what he took. Tell us whether everyone was who they said they were. And to fighting beside us: "I was bound to be witness, not weapon. If I could have stopped him, I would have stopped him at dawn."

"Oh," Zalthir said.

An observer, then. The menu was down to four.

"You know, this whole—" I started. "…anti-magic thing sounds terribly useful against a powerful wizard. As much as it inconveniences me, I'll just fight in other ways." Inside that curtain I am an orc with a mace and a loud opinion. Outside it, Manshoon is Manshoon.

"It inconveniences me, too, but…" Daz said. "Like, if it's a super powerful wizard, it's probably not a great idea for us to be going up."

"Absolutely," I said. Manshoon has prepared longer than any of us have been alive. Protanther studied one chessboard for four hundred years and lost to a dwarf who did not know the rules. Preparation has blind spots. A curtain that unmakes magic hands him a board he has never studied.

"Can she shape the field so that… our…" Daz began. She could not. Extend only. And the price of the curtain was said out loud where we could all hear it: Manshoon could escape. We were trading the lock on the exits for the silence inside. We knew what we were paying and we paid it.

"Well, controlling where people move is kind of my thing," Zalthir said.

"Let him try," the dragon said.

"Oh, snap," Zalthir said.

"Let him try."

"But can we delay it so we can surprise—" Zalthir asked. We could. She would hold the curtain until we called for it. Banked, not spent.

"Seems… seems like a good idea," I said.

She smiled. "Well chosen! Well chosen!"

"Well chosen."

## Daz — The Vault and Manshoon's Duel

The stairs kept going up, and the ceiling stayed generous, which at Candlekeep passes for a warm welcome. Behind me Grygum asked the question I was already turning over: "And the anti-magic goes all the way up? Is that the idea?"

Nobody answered him. The top of the tower answered for us.

Another plain circular room lit with continual light, its windows sealed shut. A single bookcase mounted against the eastern wall, desks and a chair in the middle. And on those shelves: a hundred tomes. Every dangerous, magical tome you could possibly imagine, collected in one chamber the way Menzoberranzan collects poisons: catalogued, contained, and one bad afternoon from general release. I tallied the room out of habit. A hundred tomes. One exit. One archwizard.

"Oh, I'm very excited," Grygum said.

A note on the desk, in archaic Common: "This vault was built to contain Candlekeep's most dangerous secrets. Consult here what can benefit civilization, but leave the tomes on these shelves. Whilst the most holy, unholy knowledge can be used for good should circumstances lead you, keeper of secrets, to believe that such dogma might be grasped by foul hands, you will find the means to destroy the contents of this vault in the chamber above."

And Manshoon, sitting there reading. He did not look up immediately. That is a Menzoberranzan move, and I respected it exactly as much as it deserved.

"By all means, browse," he said. "The trick is leaving with what you came for. Did you come for the book, or did you come for me?"

"Indeed — we came for you; we did not know of the books," said Grygum.

"You've come for me and not for the books?"

"The books are but a bonus."

An orc cleric answering an archwizard with the plain truth, in a room built to keep the truth locked up. Manshoon sighed. The sigh carried a whole opinion: *wizards*, it said, the way one says it about a delinquent House. By the time it ended, everyone in the room had committed to violence.

"That's an intense sigh!" said Zalthir.

"Oh, no," I said.

We were all coiled. I was quickest to it; Grygum, honest to the last, was slowest. And a cold thought arrived while my hands were already moving: "And we're currently in the zone of no magic, or, magical stopping." I said it out loud, which tells you how much I liked the thought. But no. Magic worked in here. It had to. A vault of living spellbooks inside a dead field is a contradiction even Candlekeep wouldn't shelve. "Okay, good point, yeah," I conceded, mostly to myself.

"I thought maybe they were just paused," Grygum offered.

I was first to act, and I was still choosing. What do you throw at Manshoon? He is not just a high-level wizard. He is a legend who has come back from the dead more times than anyone has bothered to count, and my heaviest working was already spent hours ago. While I weighed it, Grygum stepped into the pause with complete earnestness: "So, before we really get into the fighting, I want to propose to him that there are a lot of valuable books in this room. It would be a shame to damage any of them. What if we — what if we moved into the other rooms so they'd be safer?"

"That's a good proposal," I said, and watched Manshoon to see how it landed. Then, quietly, for the record: "It deserves an inspiration point, in my opinion." It did. Grygum had just proposed a venue change to a duel already in progress, and he meant every word, and that is precisely why it worked.

"Yes, you're correct. We should not damage these books; they are of priceless origin."

"Indeed, indeed," said Grygum.

"And clearly, one of us will leave with the book they want."

"Correct," I said.

"Let's all just leave them here for now. We can come back after we kill you."

"Or I killed you."

"Or, yes, yes, I admit it is a possibility."

"Well, why don't you go down ahead?"

"Sure," said Grygum. "Good luck," said Zalthir, dry as slate, and Grygum added, "I'm more civilized this way."

On the stairs, Thorin caught up to the plot. "Great, I missed that. We sent them all away?"

"Convinced him to go downstairs so we don't damage the books," said Zalthir.

"We're all moving the fight, because, like, you know…"

"Yeah, now he's in an area where…" I let it trail. The dead field sat on the bridge, starting a few paces out from the tower door. Manshoon eyed our eagerness on the way down. "Causing glyphs of warding to explode, people to die — this would be a disaster," he said, suspicious of how agreeable we were being.

"A reasonable request," said Zalthir.

We took our positions. Manshoon came down last, and he did not come down honest: duplicates drifted around him, Mirror Image, three of him where one had been. He arrived standing over me.

"Well! Now we have found a place to have the magical duel."

"Yes, we have," I said.

I have seen this in Menzoberranzan: two casters, a courtyard, everyone else pretending to be furniture, waiting for a hand to move first.

"Where's the owlbear there?" said Zalthir. The iron guardian was sitting this one out, its questions answered, its duty discharged, apparently content to watch and, I gathered, offer commentary.

The duplicates were the tell. Darts and bolts would have to guess which body. So I went where the duplicates couldn't follow: his mind. Phantasmal Killer, overfed a level past what it asks, a nightmare fitted to whatever a man who keeps dying is most afraid of. I wanted him flinching. I wanted every roll of his shaded by fear for the rest of this fight.

He paused. I watched him weigh unmaking it before it landed, and decline to spend that on me. Then he set himself against it. It was a hard save, and he made it by the width of nothing, on a legend's margin, and it still cost him: the fear got one hand on him before his discipline closed over it, and I saw the hurt register. But the nightmare guttered out, and my grip on him went with it.

"I think we can use this anti-magic field to our advantage," Grygum called, "doing things like firing at him and then ducking out the door so he can't get back at us as effectively."

Exactly. "No, no, after I hit him, I want to start heading towards the bridge. I think the more we can, like, hit him a little and then suck him out into the bridge, then we can hit him with crossbows or something."

So I backed out the door and onto the bridge, into those first few paces, into the dead band, where nothing of mine works. A wizard walking voluntarily into the one place his craft does not exist. I will not call it disarming myself. I will call it an investment in position: four mounted crossbows on this tower, and a legend who would have to come to me to matter.

The dead air closed over me. Nothing of mine reached past it.

Thorin was next.
