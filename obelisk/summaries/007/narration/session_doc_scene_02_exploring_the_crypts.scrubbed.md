Three sarcophagi. Two doors that mattered, and one of them was the reason I was still counting my own breaths carefully. The skeleton that had put me on the back foot was down, but the margin it left me was thin, and I do not like thin margins. I like knowing where everything is.

"So… the bottom… East door is where the skeleton's from," I said. Nobody argued. "So… both of the skeletons ran through the open door at the bottom. So, first we'll inspect the… sarcophagi, and then go to the same door where the other two skeletons ran away."

That is my order of operations everywhere. First the boxes, then the doors. A door can wait. A box in a room you have already paid for cannot. You paid for it, you open it.

We were pulling the lids when the two skeletons came back through the far doorway.

"Ohh," I said. Then, because they were standing there and I was standing here and nobody was doing anything: "Hey."

They did not move. In the brighter light I understood why, and I felt the red cloak on my shoulders the way you feel a coin you had forgotten in your pocket. They saw the cloaks. Old bones, standing guard for whoever built this place, and the instructions had said *red cloaks pass.* We had been fighting our way through a door we could have walked through. I noted the price of that and said nothing. The dead do not renegotiate, and I was not going to be the one to remind them of the earlier misunderstanding.

"I'll deal with them in the next room, but—" I said, and left it there. As long as we did not raise a hand, they would not. Two guards, unpaid, incorruptible, working for a livery we happened to be wearing. In my line of work you take that arrangement and you do not ask questions of it.

The middle sarcophagus held a woman. Human, long dead, and on the bones of her wrist a jeweled gold bracelet. I turned it once in the light. A hundred and forty gold, or near enough. I have handled enough of these to price them without a jeweler. Someone had loved her enough to bury her wearing it, and enough time had passed that the arithmetic had changed hands.

"Storylines," I said, when the question came of where it went. "Storylines." Everything we take goes in the record. A party that does not keep its ledger is a party that argues later, and I do not argue later. Room five, one bracelet, a hundred and forty. Done.

"Have you gotten all the…" I asked, sweeping the last of it. We had. All the loot from here, yes.

Then the noise, from the northeastern door.

"Okay, then yes, I'm gonna go… what's happening today?" I said, mostly to myself, and put my ear toward it. I did not have to strain. It came through clean and complete, every word of it, the way sound does when you have gone quiet enough inside to receive it. Voices. More than one. Screaming, but not the fighting kind.

*Please, help us. Please, help us.*

"I think they've been captured," I said. Captives. Behind a locked door in a Redbrand cellar, which meant they were an asset to somebody, which meant somebody would be back for them. None of that was our transaction. I was worn down to nearly nothing, we had a door full of hostile territory still unopened, and prisoners do not walk off. They keep.

"We can deal with them later," I said.

Sister Maela looked at me. Then she used the register, the one she keeps for questions about what kind of people we are.

"Boss? I think we should help the helpless, you know. We're the good guys, remember?"

I did not say that Gundren had not paid us to be the good guys. I did not say I was one bad exchange from the floor. "The good guys" is a story, like the cloaks are a story, and stories are things I sell, not things I buy.

She was not selling me a story. That is the difficulty with Sister M.

"Well, I'll do this for you, Sister M," I said. "For you."

Not on the merits. For her. There is a difference, and I keep the difference in the ledger too, where nobody else reads.

We moved toward the cells, and when the question came of who would go first, there was only one answer.

"I will open the door."
