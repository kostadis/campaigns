I priced the day so far. It came out ahead.

Then the floor took Pip.

He had gone down the hallway after the runner, and then he was not in the hallway anymore. There was a sound like a lid opening and a sound like a man arriving at the bottom of somewhere, and when I got to the edge there was a hole twenty feet deep with Pip in it, looking up at me with the expression of a man who has been overcharged.

He had landed well. A drop like that should cost more than it did — he was bruised and annoyed and nothing worse. Some days the ledger is kind.

"And I try to help him," I said, which was the plan in its entirety. I do not carry rope. People ask, sometimes, why a man in my line of work carries no rope, and the answer is that rope is heavy and I have always found something else. Today the something else was on my belt.

"I have a whip, okay, so I'm gonna use the whip as a rope," I said. "So that I can pull it off."

It is not a rope. It is shorter than a rope and it objects to being one. But Pip is strong and I braced well, and between us the arithmetic worked out — his weight against my footing, hand over hand up the pit wall until he came over the lip breathing hard.

Behind me, Veyra's fire went past the both of us and married the far wall. The runner kept running. Sister Maela came down the corridor at a pace I did not know she had, put her hands on Pip, and said something in the register she keeps for her god. The warmth went into him. He stood straighter. That woman's hands are worth more than everything in my pockets, and I have never told her so, and I will not start now.

The pit was still there, open and honest about itself for the first time.

"So, so we carefully move past the trap," I said. Once a trap has shown you its face, it is only a hole. "Around it, yes, yes, and being more careful." I checked the floor ahead of my feet twice anyway. "Avoid any other traps." Nothing asked anything of us. We went around.

Pip looked back at me, wanting to know whether we were still in the business of chasing this man.

"Yes, yes, I don't see anything where it's leading, but yes."

That is not a good reason to keep going. It is the reason we had.

The hallway ended in a door and the door opened onto a landing — a small one, fifteen feet above the floor of a large cellar, with stone steps going down in two short flights. I did the count. Another door beneath the stairs, on the north side. A big stone cistern taking up the western wall. Kegs and barrels lining the rest. Got it, got it.

"So, there is, like, a water…" I said, meaning the cistern, which is a word Veyra would have supplied faster. A tank. Standing water in a room full of drink. I noted it and did not look inside it, which is the kind of decision you remember later. "And I see some barrels, okay. The front door onto the west."

Two doors, then. Stairs between them. And no runner anywhere in sight.

I could not see enough from the doorway, so I came into the room properly and looked for him — scuff marks, a door still moving, anything. Nothing came to me. Then Veyra was at my shoulder, reading the room the way she reads everything, and with her eyes added to mine the answer surfaced: he had gone up. Back up to the landing level, through the door on the height.

The stairs would take us there. "Okay, let's follow this guy."

At the door I did what I always do, because a man who runs from you always goes somewhere, and somewhere always has a door, and a door will tell you what is behind it if you give it a moment. I put my ear against the wood and held still, and everything narrowed down to sound.

Murmuring. Not one voice. Several. A room's worth of them, low and urgent, the way men talk when someone has just come in out of breath with news.

I stepped back and did the sum out loud, because the others were watching me.

"This guy has told everybody."

Whatever surprise we had been carrying, he had spent it for us.

"There's no choice," I said. "He leaves us no choice other than to attack."

I say it that way when I have already decided.
