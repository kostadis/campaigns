“So we kept going.”

Old Owl Well fell behind us, and the hills began their long, stony argument against feet.

Wyvern Tor was easy enough to point toward. A dark rise against the sky, broken-backed and unpleasant. Finding one hidden camp somewhere among its lower slopes was a different matter. Ravines crossed ravines. Animal tracks became dry streambeds, then vanished beneath scree. Every ridge promised a view and delivered another ridge.

Zenvon took the lead.

This was sensible. He was our scout, and I trusted him with traps, locks, hidden doors, suspicious shadows, and any corridor narrow enough that only one of us ought to risk it first.

Open country was apparently a separate discipline.

We spent the better part of an hour following him through scrub and loose stone. The sun moved. The Tor did not appear appreciably closer. Once, I noticed marks in the earth that looked very much like our own passage from a short while before. I decided not to mention that. Restraint is important among travelling companions.

A few minutes later, we passed a thornbush with a forked branch I distinctly remembered.

“I don't think you know where you're going, buddy.”

I meant it affectionately.

Mostly.

Zenvon did not answer. He searched the folds of the land ahead, then chose another line through the hills. I followed because the alternatives were taking charge myself or admitting that I had no better idea where the camp was. Scholarship trains the mind to recognise incomplete evidence. It does not make every clump of grass confess where the marauders are hiding.

The ground steepened beneath us. Zenvon slipped between two shoulders of rock, paused, and changed direction with sudden certainty.

Everything clicked for him after that.

He found a narrow route almost invisible beneath the brush, sheltered from sight by the ridge above it. It curved through the broken ground instead of fighting over every rise. Our boots stopped dislodging stones. The wind came toward us rather than carrying our scent ahead. Even Pip could move along it without announcing himself to half the Sword Mountains.

Zenvon stopped and looked back at me.

He said nothing. He only stared, then pointed ahead.

There are silences that ask questions. His asked whether I intended to revise my published findings in light of new evidence.

I narrowed my eyes at him. He continued pointing.

“Well done.”

Reluctant approval is still approval. Professor Voss used to insist upon that distinction, usually after I had corrected him.

The first trace of the camp was not something we saw. Smoke touched the air as we climbed the rugged ridge: faint, stale, nearly lost beneath dust and sun-warmed stone. I drew a quieter breath and caught it again. Someone had a fire below us, banked low enough to avoid advertising it across the hills.

Zenvon had brought us to it from downwind.

That part I did not say aloud. He had received enough encouragement.

We eased upward until the ridge opened over a ravine. At its bottom, across a broad stretch of broken ground, a cave mouth cut into the hillside. It was an ugly black interruption in the rock, too deep for my eyes to settle immediately upon anything beyond the entrance. The smoke seemed to gather there before thinning into the air.

A boulder stood outside the cave, far enough from the opening to command the approach. Behind it, a bugbear hunkered low and kept watch.

Only one that we could see.

I held still. Loose grit pressed through the sole of my boot, and the blue candle remained wrapped and dark among my things. This was no place to give the shadows a helpful little beacon. Beside me, the others settled without a clatter or whispered curse. Even our breathing seemed conspicuous against the dry hush of the ridge.

The bugbear did not turn.

Zenvon had found the camp, found the better path, and delivered us to the edge of it unseen.

I looked from the sentry to him, then back to the cave. There was no need to repeat myself.

“Well done” would have to sustain him.
