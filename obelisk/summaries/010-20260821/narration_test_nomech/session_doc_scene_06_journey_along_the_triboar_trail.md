We took the direct road northeast.

Conyberry remained somewhere beyond our route, a ruin we had discussed and then did not visit. Toblen’s warning had changed the price of that detour. Nobody made a speech about it. We simply left Phandalin behind and kept Old Owl Well in front of us.

I led.

A road gives you three things to count: where someone can watch it, where someone can cross it, and how far you must run before either becomes difficult. The Triboar Trail was generous with the first two and poor with the third. Trees pressed near in places. Low ground swallowed sight. Every bend offered enough cover for trouble and no reason for trouble to announce itself.

Veyra and Sister Maela could see into darkness better than Pip and me. During the day, that did not matter. At night, it meant I was responsible for choosing ground before the light was gone. Leadership is sometimes a grand word for deciding where everyone sleeps.

We covered about thirty miles on the first day.

No wagons passed us. No riders. No merchants complaining about wheels or prices. The trail was empty in both directions, but it was not unused. The road remained worn beneath our feet. Old marks crossed newer ones in the dirt. People were still travelling this way.

They were simply not travelling while we were there.

That distinction mattered.

We found what remained of a caravan beside the trail. It had been attacked and left there. Whatever business had brought it east or west had ended in the road, and whatever could not be taken had been abandoned with it.

I stopped before the others reached it.

An attacked caravan is not only wreckage. It is a question about whether the attackers have finished. I studied the road ahead, then the ground around us, then the places overlooking both. Nothing moved. No voice carried from the trees. There was no second group waiting for someone curious enough to approach the first.

Only the caravan.

There was not enough to name who had done it. That was inconvenient. Wyvern Tor had marauders. Goblins worked these roads. Redbrands had discovered that wearing the same dress could pass for authority. There were other possibilities, because there are always other people who want the same money.

The evidence gave us a transaction without a name. Someone had come through. Someone else had stopped them. Goods, lives, or both had been taken, and the trail had kept the account.

It confirmed one thing only: the warnings were not being sold too high.

Wyvern Tor might have been responsible. It might not. I do not charge a man for a crime because he has committed similar crimes elsewhere. I also do not turn my back on him.

We moved on.

The High Road carried the serious traffic north and south. It connected places that still expected caravans to arrive. The Triboar Trail ran east and west into country where arrival was becoming less certain. By then I understood that Neverwinter and the land immediately around it were not simply another settled part of the map. They were the practical edge of civilization.

Beyond that edge, civilization did not end cleanly. It narrowed.

First there were fewer patrols. Then fewer farms. Then an empty road with proof that somebody still used it and better proof that somebody was hunting those who did.

The hinterlands had been worked over repeatedly: barbarians, orcs, goblins, wars, the Spellplague, gods interfering, and wizards who should have known better. It was a long list. The important part was that every item left something behind, and none of those things had paid for repairs.

Day passed without an attack.

Night did as well.

I did not find that comforting. An empty road can mean safety. It can also mean everyone else has already calculated the risk.

In the morning we continued northeast. I kept ahead far enough to see trouble before it reached the others, but not so far that Pip could not reach me if seeing it became the smaller problem. Veyra and Sister Maela carried what we would need when the light failed. Pip carried the strength for anything that did not care about negotiation.

I carried the direction.

By the following day, the land had risen into rugged hills around Old Owl Well. The trail no longer looked like something laid across the country. It looked like something the country had permitted, temporarily.

I checked the slopes, the broken ground, and the road behind us.

Nothing moved.

So we kept going.
