The orchard was Pip's idea, if anyone ever asks. Not the stopping—the stopping was Zenvon's, announced with all the gravity of a general plotting a campaign. No, the orchard itself. The way out of Phandalin runs past Daran Edermath's place, and I figured a retired adventurer with a reputation for knowing things would be a better use of our morning than another round of Toblen's porridge.

I was right, and I was wrong, and both were a pleasure to watch.

We found him clearing underbrush beside his cottage, bent over with a sickle and a back that had clearly filed formal complaints about the whole arrangement. He straightened when he saw us, and his face did that thing old men's faces do when they decide you're worth their time.

"Well, well, well, well, well. I… the heroes of Phandalin!"

I could have lived without the six "wells." The rest of it—the pride in his voice, the way he said *heroes* like it was a word he'd been saving—that part I could get used to.

"We'll, I have to say, I'm very proud to hear that you dealt with the Redbrands."

Zenvon, to his credit, didn't inflate the story. "Yeah, we walked… it happened as an accident. We didn't intend to, but it was, I guess, God's plan."

God's plan. I've heard better theology from a drunk dwarf, but the man was being modest and that counted for something. Daran didn't press. He just nodded, pleased, and moved to what was clearly his actual concern.

"That is the best news I have. Now, I just have to ask — have you been able to deal with the Old Owl Well problem?"

And there it was. Zenvon launched into the pitch—heading out tomorrow, wanted any intelligence before we went—and Daran's eyes lit up like a man who'd been waiting weeks for someone to ask.

"Fabulous, fabulous, fabulous."

Three times. The man was excited. That should have been my warning.

He sat back and launched into the eastern lands. Wars, demons, gods, elemental catastrophes. The more you go east, the less settled it gets. Conyberry's been a wreck for years. I'd heard some of it before, in snatches, but Daran told it like a man who'd walked through the wreckage himself, and I found myself listening closer than I'd planned.

Zenvon asked about routes—road or hills—and Daran gave the answer I'd have given: road's safer, hills have bandits. He hedged on the details, waving a hand. "Well, he's been saying there have been several attacks near Wyvern Tor, of caravans."

Harbin's information. Which meant it was worth approximately what we paid for it, but I'd take what I could get.

Then Zenvon did something I didn't expect. He invited the old man along.

"Would you like to join us?"

Daran laughed—a dry, rattling sound. His back hurts, he said, and he's got all the— he stopped, reconsidered, and gave the best answer I'd heard all day. "One of the things about being an old adventurer is: you're an old adventurer because you choose to stop adventuring."

I filed that away. It sounded like the kind of thing I'd want to remember in forty years, assuming I made it that long.

Zenvon, undeterred, asked if he had anything useful. Daran considered, then delivered his wisdom with the solemnity of a temple oracle: "Keep your eyes up. Make sure your sword is sharpened."

Zenvon blinked. "Wait, what? Eyes up?"

"Keep your back straight, keep your eye out, head on a swivel."

Useless. Gloriously, perfectly useless. And Zenvon—bless his halfling heart—pulled out his notes and started writing it down.

"Yeah, I was going to make notes of it, actually."

I watched the pen move. I watched Daran's face transform. The old man had found an audience, and by the gods, he was going to *perform.*

He went on for ten minutes. Ten. Minutes. Old adventuring stories, bits of advice, the same three pieces of wisdom dressed up in seven different coats. Zenvon's hand was cramping by the end, and his face had gone from polite interest to quiet desperation.

"Okay, no."

I couldn't help it. The laugh came up my throat and I bit down on it so hard my jaw ached. I was looking at Zenvon—the same halfling who'd conned a room full of bandits into believing their own man was a traitor—trapped in a field by an old man's enthusiasm, forced to write down *keep your eyes up* as if it were a revelation. I wanted to say something. I wanted to rescue him. But my ribs were too busy trying not to shake, and honestly? He'd walked into this one himself.

But Daran was happy. That was the thing. He was delighted, absolutely delighted, that someone was taking his advice seriously. And I caught myself thinking—that's the whole game, isn't it? Making an old man feel like his words mattered. Zenvon had done that without even trying, just by being too polite to stop.

"Cool. Alright, then, we'll head back."

I almost felt sorry for him. Almost.

But then he dug into his notes—the same notes that had just cost him ten minutes—and found something. "Oh, Daran, I just recalled a question I wanted to ask you. Tell me more about Netheril. What kind of magic should we expect over there?"

Daran's face went blank. "Did you say Netheril? N-E-T-H-E-R-I-L?"

"Yes, that's right."

"Why would you be asking that?"

"Because you mentioned it earlier."

"He never mentioned it. No, he didn't. He never."

There was a pause. The kind of pause where a man checks his own memory and finds it wanting. Zenvon consulted his notes, calm as still water. "No, actually—the note says Daran called it an old watchtower of Netheril, so…"

Daran blinked. Then he laughed at himself. "Oh, sorry—did my brain… my brain farted. Sorry. Yes, yes, yes. Yes, alright, my bad. Sorry. Thank you—I think that's why we have the notes."

My jaw ached again. The man's own words had been used against him, gently, and he'd taken it well enough to smile. Zenvon had gotten his answer AND an apology in one move. I made a mental note never to bring him to a negotiation I wasn't prepared to lose.

Then Daran leaned back, and the tone shifted. "Well! Well, you have to understand, this is an old story."

He told it well. I'll give him that. An ancient empire with ships that flew, magic beyond anything mortals can manage now. Ninth level spells, he said—modern wizardry tops out at nine. Netheril reached twelve. I don't know what that means, not really, but I know what it sounds like: power so far beyond us they might as well have been gods.

One of their leaders, Karsus, did something that broke the Weave itself. Magic almost died. The empire fell apart.

I watched Zenvon's face as he listened, and I saw the thing I'd come to recognize—the moment when a piece of a puzzle clicks into place. He was connecting dots I couldn't see yet. Karsus. A broken Weave. An empire scattered to the winds, leaving powerful trinkets all across the North.

Daran himself seemed uneasy with the story. "Those are stories. Right? It's like a big ancient empire that dies because of hubris. I mean, come on. It sounds like a morality play."

Zenvon didn't blink. "Well, I believe all the stories are built on some real-life incidents, so…"

Daran nodded slowly, and I could see him weighing the words. "Yeah—well, you know, Zenvon, that's a good… that's not something to… I've got to give you credit."

Credit. From a man who'd been alive for most of those stories' telling. I'd have taken that and stopped, but Zenvon pressed on, asking what happens to the trinkets, whether they could be cursed.

Daran mulled it over. The word *cursed*, he said, was a strong word. He dismissed the old wives' tales—the corrupting influence of Netheril was just a story meant to scare people. But then he paused, and his voice got quieter.

"With any powerful magical thing, you never really know why, who created it, for what reason, and to what purpose."

Zenvon agreed. "Can't agree more with you."

"And so I would say that those old wives' tales about the corrupting influence of Netheril and magic are just that—old wives' tales. Meant to scare people and keep them away from those kinds of magical things—which," he paused, "isn't a bad thing. Isn't a bad thing."

I thought about the black stone in my pack. The one I'd won in a dice game near Neverwinter's docks, the one I'd sold to a peddler headed for Phandalin, the one that had been whispering my name in my dreams ever since. I thought about how careful I'd been not to hold it too long. How I'd told the party to kill the thing that had seen it in my head.

Old wives' tales, Daran said. Meant to scare people.

Fine. Then why was my hand sweating?

"There's a lot of magical things out there that, in the wrong hands—or even worse, in hands that are not capable of handling those magical things—bad things can happen."

Wisely said, Zenvon told him. And he meant it. I could tell.

Daran admitted he had more questions than answers—"there's a lot of stories about the North"—and then, mercifully, the conversation wound down. Zenvon took his leave, promising to stop by when he was back from the Old Owl Well. Daran beamed. "Oh, excellent, excellent."

We walked back toward the Stonehill Inn, and I let the silence stretch for a bit before I said anything.

Ten minutes of notes. For *keep your eyes up.* I was going to be insufferable about this for at least the rest of the day.

But I was also thinking about Netheril. About an empire that fell because one man reached too far. About trinkets scattered across the North, and what happens when people find them.

About the word *cursed,* and how Daran had danced around it like it was a live coal.

I kept my eyes up. The road ahead was long.
