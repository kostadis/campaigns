We'd been walking for an hour. The hills around Wyvern Tor were all scree and stubborn scrub, the kind of ground that looks the same from every angle and wants to stay that way. I'd given up trying to read — the road had been making my handwriting worse by the mile, and the saddle of the pack mule was no desk. So I watched Zenvon instead.

He'd been quiet since we left Old Owl Well. Head down, eyes moving, the way he gets when he's working a problem with his whole body. I'd seen him do it a dozen times. I'd also seen him do it wrong.

"You go an hour," I finally said, "and Veyra says: I don't think you know where you're going, buddy."

It came out teasing. It came out more honest than I meant. An hour of watching him circle the same ridgeline twice had worn through my patience, and I'd already started mentally pricing how many more hours of daylight we had.

Zenvon didn't answer. He just looked at me, and I saw the corner of his mouth twitch.

Then he turned and started up a slope I'd written off as a dead end — a narrow goat track that hugged the cliff and vanished around a shoulder of rock. I opened my mouth to say something, and closed it. He moved like a man who'd just remembered exactly where he was going.

The ground fell away and there it was.

"I don't know how he did it," I said later, when we were crouched in the lee of an outcrop and the camp was spread out below us. "But he'd found a shortcut. A real one. The kind of path that doesn't exist unless someone who knows the country put it there, or someone who's looked at the country hard enough to see through it."

Zenvon looked at me. Didn't say a word. Just pointed.

*Trust me this time.*

I conceded. "Well done."

It cost me nothing. It cost him more — an hour of my needling, my fingers drumming on my thigh, my loudly expressed opinion that we were walking in circles. He'd taken it all and held his tongue, and then he'd found the camp and a way to it that saved us hours. I'd have been a poorer sport about it, in his boots.

The smell of smoke hit me before I placed the source — faint, woodsmoke with something animal in it, the smell of a fire that's been burning a long time at a low temperature. I climbed the last few feet of the ridge on my hands and knees, keeping low, and looked down.

Fifty yards away, a cave mouth opened at the bottom of the ravine. It was a dark, hungry slit in the rock, the kind of opening that swallows light and sound and gives back nothing but the smell of things that live underground. No torches. No banner. Nothing to mark it as anything but another hole in the hillside — except for the smoke, and except for the thing hunkered behind a boulder twenty yards outside the entrance.

A bugbear. One bugbear, alone, shoulder to the rock, head swivelling in a slow, patient sweep of the approach. It was doing its job properly. It just wasn't expecting anyone on this side of the hill.

Maela was already looking at me, one hand on her hammer, waiting for me to do the thing I do. Pip had gone very still beside her, and Zenvon was watching the sentry with the same flat attention he'd spent on the ridge, only sharper now.

We held our position. The sentry had no idea we were there.

*Trust me this time*, his pointing had said. And I had. And here we were, the camp found and the guard blind, and the whole party crouched in the quiet with an hour of daylight left and a decision to make.

I pulled out my notebook and wrote down what I could see. The bugbear, the boulder, the shape of the cave mouth. The smell of the smoke. The way the watch was set.

I didn't know, yet, who lived in that cave or what they'd done to earn Harbin's offer of a hundred and twenty gold. But I knew I'd be coming down that slope sooner than the sentry expected, and I knew Zenvon had bought us the right to choose the ground.

I closed the notebook and looked at him — the small halfling who'd earned his "well done" the hard way, one wrong hour at a time.

He was already looking at the cave.
