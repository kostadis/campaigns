We took the direct road. The detour through Conybury got discussed the night before, weighed like everything gets weighed in that inn — Conybury meant Conybury's ruins, and Conybury's ruins meant trouble we hadn't been paid for. The Old Owl Well had boots in it. So we went the straight way, and the straight way was thirty miles of Triboar Trail with the sun on our necks.

Nobody hit us the first day. That felt like luck. It felt like that the second day too, right up until the moment it didn't.

The caravan came into view around noon. I saw the wheels first — one of them off its axle and lying flat in the grass, the other three still standing like a table that had lost a leg. The wagon bed had been picked clean. Whatever the bandits hadn't taken, they'd smashed, and whatever they'd smashed, they'd done out of spite more than profit. Barrels cracked open for the look of it. A chest with its lid stove in and nothing inside to show for the effort.

I walked the perimeter before I let the others come close. Four sets of boot prints, all heading east after the work was done. Two of them had been dragging something heavy between them. I did not follow the tracks. I have learned that following tracks is how you find the people who made them, and we were not here to find those people yet.

Veyra climbed down and went through the wreckage with a professional's distaste, the way you'd sort through the remains of someone else's meal to see what it contained. Pip stood guard with his hand on Talon's pommel, watching the tree line. Maela stood at the road's edge with her holy symbol in her fist, not looking at the wagon at all. I did not ask her what she was looking at. I did not need to.

The evidence sat there in the dirt like an accounting problem with the numbers already solved. Traffic on this road has been targeted. Whoever did this — Wyvern Tor marauders, goblins, Redbrands, some faction we haven't met yet — they knew the route and they knew the timing, and they took what they wanted and left the rest to rot. I could not have told you which of them it was. I could tell you that it didn't much matter. They were all the same kind of problem wearing different clothes.

We saw no other wagons that day. No travelers heading the other way, no merchants, no pilgrims. Just the road, and the wagon, and the flies working the wheel that had come off its axle.

But there were signs people still used the trail — wheel ruts worn into the earth that the grass had not yet reclaimed, the ashes of a campfire in a hollow short of the road, the stink of an old midden a hundred yards off. People were still passing through, taking their chances, trading what they carried against the probability that they'd get it through. That's the arithmetic of this country. Neverwinter and its walls are the edge of things. Past that, the High Road runs north-south with the commerce, but no one builds a wall around a trail. The trail just lies there, and you take it or you don't.

The frontier doesn't end at the city gates. It ends where the road stops being a road and becomes a rumor — and we were well past that point, walking a trail that still had the teeth marks of whatever had eaten the last wagon to come through.

I thought about the hinterland as we walked. The barbarians who'd swept through here, the orc bands, the goblins, the wars that had been fought over this ground, the Spellplague that had broken the land itself, the divine meddling that had rearranged the map. And the wizards. The wizards who should have known better, the ones who cracked things open because they could and left the pieces for people like us to walk through. Everyone knows the list. Everyone walks the ground and tells the list like a litany, because a litany is a shape you can hold onto out here.

The second day we crossed into the hills surrounding the Old Owl Well. The land began to climb. The wagon had been the warning; the hills were the place where you started paying attention again, where the sound of your own boots on the stone was the loudest thing for miles.

I kept my hand near the hilt of the sword I'd taken from the Redbrand armory. It was a fine sword. It was not fine enough to stop thinking about the wagon.

Nobody asked me what I thought we should do if we found more than boots when we got there. Nobody asked, and I did not volunteer. The road had told me everything I needed to know about the country ahead, and the country ahead was the kind that makes you count your exits before you count your coins.

I counted the exits. There were two, and one of them was the way we'd come.
