The colorful tent was the first thing I saw, and it told me more than any watchtower could. A wizard who camps in the open, who doesn't bother hiding his presence, is either a fool or a man with nothing to fear. The ruins of the old watchtower hunched behind it, rubble spilling into a courtyard that had once been a proper yard. No one in sight. Just that tent, bright as a banner, and the hills breathing quiet around us.

I didn't trust the quiet. Quiet like that usually means something's holding its breath.

Zenvon wanted us to tiptoe. Fine. Let the halfling have his creeping—I've learned to let him set the pace on approach, since he's the one who usually sees trouble first. We moved slow, boots finding the soft ground, nothing clicking against stone. The well sat there, old and dry, and I kept my eyes on that tent because whatever was inside it, I could feel it the way you feel a storm coming, that pressure behind the sternum that has nothing to do with weather.

Then the tower gave them up.

They came out of the ruined base like they'd been waiting for an excuse—shambling, gray-skinned, eyes that didn't track. A lot of them. More than twelve, or twelve, I didn't count, because counting matters less than the fact that they were *there*, between us and anything we wanted. Zenvon asked if they'd talk.

I knew they wouldn't. I could feel it in the slack way they moved, the dumb animal patience of things that don't think and don't want and don't stop.

"Zombies are essentially mindless monsters," I said, and watched Zenvon's face do the thing it does when he's trying to fit something new into an old map. I looked at the tent, because the dead don't get up on their own. "There's probably a necromancer."

He was still confused. I've seen that look enough by now to know when to keep talking.

"A magic user who takes the dead corpses and reanimates them."

The zombies kept coming, more of them, filling the courtyard with that shuffling sound like wet cloth dragging over stone. And I did the math that nobody else in this party was doing, because I'm the one who's been trained to recognize evil when it wears a robe and a patient smile.

"Very evil magic. And very dangerous magic, and very powerful magic, given the number…"

Pip shifted his weight, hand hovering near a weapon he'd have to be mad to draw. "Boss, I don't know if we want to fight all these zombies."

He wasn't wrong. Fighting twelve dead things at once, with whatever raised them somewhere close—that's how you end up joining their number. Zenvon got that look he gets, the one where he's already moved past the fight to the plan. "Let's see if we can deceive them, or… I'm hoping there is a central control for all of these monsters—if we can find the necromancer."

Veyra pointed at the tent, because of course she'd already worked it out. "Well, if there's going to be a necromancer, he's probably in that tent."

And Zenvon, bless his clever little heart, decided that the way to deal with a man who commands an army of corpses is to insult him.

So he shouted. Called him *Zombie Lord*, of all things, taunting him out into the open. I bit down on about six different objections because, at that point, he'd already committed, and pulling him back would've looked like doubt.

The tent flap moved.

The man who stepped out was bald, clean-shaven, and wearing the kind of robes that cost more than our whole party's gear put together. Veyra went still beside me, and I heard her breath catch. "Yup—he's a wizard. He's a wizard. Do you see those tattoos on his head?"

I did. They crawled across his scalp, dark ink against bare skin, and they weren't decoration. Veyra pulled out her notebook and pointed, and her voice had that edge it gets when she's sure of something terrible. "They're the symbols of a necromancer!"

He stared at us. We stared at him. His zombies kept shuffling out of the tower until the courtyard had more dead than we had living, and they didn't spread out—they closed around him like a bodyguard, an army of flesh standing at attention behind a man who hadn't raised a finger.

"WHAT IS THE MEANING OF THIS?"

His voice carried. Not loud, exactly—it didn't need to be. It had the weight of someone who's used to being answered.

Zenvon talked. He's good at that, I'll give him that much—talked us from "we're just here to talk" to Hamun Kost introducing himself, to learning he was a Red Wizard of Thay, which made Veyra's eyes go wide and made me want to take a step back and drag the whole party with me. But Zenvon held his ground with a question, calm as if he were ordering at the Stonehill. Asked Hamun why the wizard was here, at the well.

"I'm a wizard," Hamun said, like that explained everything. "And it's not polite to ask what a wizard is doing here."

It didn't answer the question. It didn't have to, and we all knew it.

The negotiation went back and forth—Hamun telling us he wasn't interested in harming anyone, that he wanted to be a positive member of the community, that he could offer information. And then the real offer came: the location of Cragmaw Castle, in exchange for a favor.

The first option was marauders at Wyvern Tor, a bunch of bugbears and an ogre scouting his camp, causing trouble. The second was a banshee named Agatha—he wanted to know the name of the wizard who built this tower.

"Why don't you ask her yourself?" I asked.

Hamun didn't even blink. "Well, I mean—it's a banshee. And I don't want to get killed by a banshee."

That honest. A man who commands the dead, admitting he's afraid of a dead woman's scream. I decided I respected that, a little. At least he knew what he was, and what he wasn't.

Zenvon pulled us aside. "It looks like going to Agatha seems a bit of a suicide mission, so… it's better to deal with the bugbears and the ogres."

I should've let it go. But there was something in me that wouldn't let him pick the dangerous one out of fear of the unknown one, so I said, "Well, if we were trying to kill her, I couldn't agree with you more. But our goal isn't to kill the banshee. So if we go there and are very respectful, we could avoid getting killed by it."

Zenvon shook his head. "Still, that seems much riskier than fighting with the bugbears, which we're already familiar with."

And that's when I said what I meant, what I'd been holding since those dead things started walking. "Look—if the choice is killing bugbears and ogres, or going and chatting with a banshee, I'm for dealing with ogres and bugbears Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday… and twice on the tenth day."

Zenvon looked at me, and something in his face shifted. Acceptance. "Okay, then we have our answer. We'll tell Hamun that we'll head to Wyvern Tor and deal with the bugbears."

So we told him. And Hamun Kost smiled, a thin, knowing smile, and said, "Wonderful, wonderful. And when you're back, I'll make sure that I have that information for you. And maybe I'll even have a nice, fine meal for us to eat."

Zenvon asked about safe passage on the return—whether the zombies would try to kill us. Hamun's smile didn't waver. "Well—my advice to you is, you don't walk into somebody's house without shouting something."

Zenvon, because he can't help himself, pointed at the tent. "Well, technically your house is the tent, and we haven't walked into it yet."

"Yeah, well—my zombies are always very protective. You just give me a shout before you come, and I'll come out."

We left. The dead watched us go—all those gray faces turned toward us, no expression in any of them, just the dumb hunger of things that follow orders. I walked backward for the first few steps, watching them, waiting for the order that would send them shambling after us.

It never came.

I thought about that tent, and the man inside it, and the twelve corpses he kept at his back like a shield he didn't have to ask. I thought about what it meant that he was afraid of a banshee—afraid enough to send strangers to face her in his place.

And I decided, as we crested the ridge away from the well, that I understood Zenvon's choice better than I'd let on. Give me the monsters I know—the ones with teeth and muscles and rage I can read in their posture. There's a clean shape to killing something that wants to kill you back.

But a woman dead so long she's become a scream wearing a shape? Something that knows your name before you speak it?

I'd rather fight the ogres.

Every day of the week, and twice on the tenth.
