Toblen met us at the door like we were returning soldiers, not a party that had spent the afternoon running from bugbears with our tails between our legs. "The heroes! The heroes of Phandalin." He was beaming. It had not been two days, so nobody was asking what we'd done for them lately — that grace period was still intact, and I intended to enjoy it while it lasted.

"Elsa, why don't you get him a beer?" The barkeep's voice carried across the common room, and Elsa was already moving before the words finished leaving his mouth. Zenvon accepted the mug with the ease of a man who had earned it, and I watched the foam settle while he made small talk about what a pleasure the stay had been. He has a way of being charming that I have decided to stop resenting and simply admire from a distance. It saves time.

"Thank you. So, I wanted to tell you that my friends and I are leaving for Conyberry tomorrow, first thing in the morning."

Toblen paused. "And why would you want to go there?"

There it was. The question I had been asking myself since Sister Garaele handed us that comb. But Zenvon gave the clean answer: "We have a friend who needs some help, at the Old Owl Well." A friend. That was generous. We had a contract, a promise, and a debt — but out loud, a friend sounded better, and I let it stand.

"Yeah, but why would you go through Conyberry? It's an abandoned town."

Zenvon shrugged. "Well, that's the closest to the Old Owl Well. Do you know a better route — one that's kind of safe?"

Toblen went quiet for a beat, and I saw the calculation in his face — the same calculation every tavern keeper in every town makes when adventurers announce a destination. He could help us, or he could warn us, or he could do both badly. He chose the third.

"Conyberry's been abandoned for years. But I guess it's a place you could go." He paused. "I don't know about you, but going into abandoned towns doesn't feel safe." Another pause. "But you're powerful adventurers. Heroes of Phandalin. So I'm sure there's nothing… I'm sure I'm just a barkeep — I'm just an owner of a tavern. So maybe this is about me more than it is about you."

I tucked that away. There was something in the way he kept circling back to his own station — *I'm just a barkeep* — that sounded less like humility and more like a man rehearsing a permission slip he was giving himself not to know. Zenvon pressed him anyway. "Well, that's a fair point — but tell us anything that you know about Old Owl Well or Conyberry, because we have made up our mind to start early in the morning. As much as I like adventures, I'd like to avoid any surprises if possible."

"The town of Conyberry has been abandoned for years and lies in complete ruins. So you shouldn't find anything of value there. But any time you have a place that's ruins and is on the road — that's where, well, how shall I put it? People who are dangerous to the unwary might be."

Zenvon leaned in. "Generous to what?"

Toblen blinked. "Dangerous to the unwary. People who aren't paying attention."

"Paying attention. Okay."

I watched the exchange and let my own mind drift to what I knew — which was more than Toblen, I suspected, had ever bothered to learn. Conyberry was a Spellplague casualty. The Year of Blue Fire had torn through it like a hand through wet paper, and what the magical storm had left standing, the Uthgardt tribes had finished off afterward. There was a poetry to that which Professor Voss would have appreciated: first the gods played their games with mortals, and then mortals played theirs with each other. The ruins were not just empty stone — they were a palimpsest of two disasters layered on top of each other.

Toblen was still talking. "Right — so what I would do, if you're heading in there, is just be careful, keep your eyes peeled. But if I were you, I would avoid that town. Now, the only other thing: to get to Agatha's, you have to go through there. That's what Sister Garaele tells you."

"Yep," Zenvon said.

I did the maths in my head. The banshee's lair sat on the far side of Conyberry — that was the oldest, most reliable geography. Whatever we were walking into, we were walking through it either way, and there was something almost comfortable in that. At least the route wasn't in question.

The thing I noticed, standing there with my beer going warm, was that Toblen's warnings all had the shape of fear rather than memory. A man who had walked those streets could have told us which roofs had fallen, which wells had gone bad, which corners still held cold patches where the Spellplague had bitten deepest. Toblen told us to be careful, to keep our eyes peeled, to avoid the town entirely. He had never been there. He was not warning us away from a place he had seen — he was warning us away from the *idea* of a place, and I found I could not fault him for it. The idea was bad enough.

"Okay, so we'll rest. Alright. Toblen — it's been such a wonderful stay."

Zenvon was already moving toward the stairs, and the rest of us followed. The inn had never felt as good as it did that night — four walls, a locked door, and a bed that did not have a goblin under it. I lay awake a while, listening to Toblen's voice repeat in my head: *dangerous to the unwary*. I decided, before sleep took me, that the trick was simply to pay attention.

"Alright. So… next morning, you wake up."

Morning arrived the way mornings do — too soon, and with the taste of last night's ale still on my tongue.
