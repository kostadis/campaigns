We reached Old Owl Well near noon, after enough rugged hills to make every loose stone feel personally placed. The ridge gave us our first clear view: a crumbling watchtower, heaps of fallen masonry enclosing a courtyard, and a bright tent planted in the middle of it all.

No smoke. No workers. No welcoming committee.

“The colorful tent indicates that there can be somebody,” Zenvon said. “It’s the old watchtower. And the well is the… Old Owl Well, I suppose. So it looks like it’s pretty empty — but I sense some eeriness in the air.”

“I suggest my team tiptoe and walk slowly. Towards the well.”

So we entered slowly. Zenvon tiptoed.

The dead began coming out of the tower.

There were twelve of them, though the first impression was simply *too many*. Boots dragged over stone. Limbs hung at poor angles. Dry throats rasped without breath enough to speak. They spread across the courtyard between us and the tent, a wall built from stolen bodies.

“Are they not going to talk?” Zenvon asked.

“Wow,” he said when I shook my head.

No. Zombies do not talk. They do not negotiate, reconsider, or remember that the hands raised against you once held tools, cups, children. Someone had stripped all of that away and left only obedience.

I looked at the tent. “There’s probably a necromancer.”

Zenvon’s face did not improve.

“A magic user who takes the dead corpses and reanimates them.”

I tightened my hand around my holy symbol. “Very evil magic.”

The dead continued shuffling into place.

“And very dangerous magic, and very powerful magic, given the number…”

“Boss, I don’t know if we want to fight all these zombies,” Pip said.

A sound judgment. I can return the undead to stillness, but twelve corpses raised and held by one will meant the greater danger had not yet shown its face.

“Let’s see if we can deceive them, or…” Zenvon said. “I’m hoping there is a central control for all of these monsters — if we can find the necromancer.”

“Well, if there’s going to be a necromancer, he’s probably in that tent,” Veyra said.

Zenvon filled his lungs.

“So I’m going to shout: hey, Zombie Lord.”

Then, louder: “I call him Zombie Lord, because he’s controlling them. *Hey, Zombie Lord — are you afraid to face us? Is that why you’re sending the zombies?*”

The tent flap opened.

A man with a shaved head stepped into the courtyard, his temper arriving ahead of the rest of him.

“WHAT IS THE MEANING OF THIS?”

“Yup — he’s a wizard. He’s a wizard,” Veyra said. Her eyes fixed on the markings covering his scalp. “Do you see those tattoos on his head?”

She pulled out her notebook, found what she wanted, and stabbed a finger at the page.

“They’re the symbols of a necromancer!”

Zenvon raised his hands. “Yeah, so — we are just here to talk to you, nothing else. We don’t want to fight unnecessarily. My aim here is just to talk to you, and head back.”

The wizard asked our names.

“My name is Zenvon. These are my friends — Pip, Sister Maela, and Veyra. How do you introduce yourself?”

“As Hamun Kost.”

“Hamun Kost. Hamun Kost.”

There was a brief pause while Zenvon inspected the name as though Hamun might have palmed it from someone else.

“Cut right to the point, Hamun. I just wanted to know — what are you doing here in the lost town of Conyberry, and at this Old Owl Well?”

“Well, I’m a wizard,” Hamun said. “And it’s not polite to ask what a wizard is doing here. But this is an archaeological dig that I am interested in.”

“Well, what’s so special about this place that drew your interest?”

“Look — I am not interested. I’m just interested in this tower. And quite frankly, I don’t think I should have to answer why I’m interested in this tower. So why don’t we just keep it—”

He stopped himself.

“I’m a wizard, and I have the things I’m interested in.”

“Well, Hamun, you seem like a nice wizard, and I know you’ve been doing your own thing, but…” Zenvon indicated the courtyard full of corpses. “Whatever you’re doing — your presence here is kind of alarming to the neighboring lands of Neverwinter and Phandalin and other places. So I want to keep my citizens safe, and that’s why I just want to ensure that.”

“My — my dear boy! Zenvon, is that your name again?”

“Yeah.”

“I can assure you that I am not interested in harming anyone in this area.”

“Well, that’s reassuring — but it would help us be a little calmer if you tell me what you’re doing. And I swear your secret does not go out; it stays with us.”

“My dear boy — if I wanted my secrets to be safe, I would kill you. Now, I would like you to know that I am a Red Wizard of Thay. And a Red Wizard of Thay is not someone you should be—”

The title settled matters the zombies had not. I knew enough of Thay. Hamun was not boasting about a scholarly society.

“Understood. Just thinking…” Zenvon said. His eyes moved across the dead. “It’s tricky, actually. I don’t know what to do.”

“Look — I don’t want to hurt your community. But I want to show that I want to be a positive member of the community.”

“Well, how exactly, Mr. Hamun? How can you help the community?”

“Well, I can give you information.”

“Go on. I’m all ears.”

“What is the one thing you want to know? Where Cragmaw Castle is, for example?”

Then they stared at one another.

Meanwhile, the remaining zombies finished their slow progress from the tower and gathered around Hamun. He stood among them with no weapon drawn and no need to draw one. Twelve dead faces turned toward us. He had arranged an answer while Zenvon was still deciding on the question.

“Now… Zenvon. What is it that you would like to know?”

“Well, Hamun, there are a lot of things I would like to know — but the one thing that stands on top is where Cragmaw Castle is located.”

“Of course I know where that is!”

“Interesting. Wow. Where can we find the castle?”

“You know, I think I will be happy to exchange that information — if you were to do a small little thing for me.”

“I thought you were a Red Wizard. I’m surprised that I could do something for you.”

“Look, my friend. Look, Zenvon. There are these marauders at Wyvern Tor. They’ve scouted out my camp, and they seem inclined to cause trouble. I’d rather you deal with that trouble than I deal with that trouble.”

Hamun glanced at his silent servants.

“So if you were to go deal with those… with those rough-ins, I would be more than happy to provide you the location of Cragmaw Castle.”

“Okay — elaborate a little bit more on how the marauders have hurt you. How many of them were there, from your knowledge? And what happened to you with them?”

“Well, there is a bugbear. Named…” Hamun abandoned the name. “There’s a bunch of bugbears and an ogre.”

“Old girl?”

“Ogre,” Hamun said.

“Yeah, okay.”

“Not that I couldn’t deal with it myself — but I don’t want to have to raise more zombies to do my business.”

That made the bargain plain. Hamun was offering us a chance to prevent his workforce from expanding.

“But if that’s not what you’re interested in — I have another quest for you.”

“Go on. Let me see if I can do that for you, at least.”

“You may have heard there’s a banshee named Agatha.”

“Yes, I quite certainly have heard of her.”

“Right. I want to know the name of the wizard who built the tower at the Old Owl Well.”

“Why don’t you ask her yourself?”

Hamun hesitated. I watched his eyes and asked again.

“Why don’t you ask her yourself?”

“Well, I mean — it’s a banshee. And I don’t want to get killed by a banshee.”

There it was. A Red Wizard of Thay, master of twelve corpses, surrounded by enough evil magic to sour the stones beneath my boots—and he wanted us to speak with Agatha because *he* feared she would kill him.

“Either deal with the Wyvern Tor lot, or get me the name from Agatha. The information of where Cragmaw Castle is, is yours.”

Zenvon drew us aside.

“It looks like going to Agatha seems a bit of a suicide mission, so… It’s better to deal with the bugbears and the ogres.”

“Well, if we were trying to kill her, I couldn’t agree with you more,” I said. “But our goal isn’t to kill the banshee. So if we go there and are very respectful, we could avoid getting killed by it.”

That was the reasonable assessment. It was also not my vote.

“Still, that seems much riskier than fighting with the bugbears, which we’re already familiar with.”

Familiar mattered. Bugbears had weight, reach, tempers, and bones. Ogres were larger applications of the same problems. A banshee could tear the life from you with a cry, and respect would be cold comfort if she disliked the presentation of our comb.

“Look — if the choice is killing bugbears and ogres, or going and chatting with a banshee, I’m for dealing with ogres and bugbears Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday… and twice on the tenth day.”

“Okay, then we have our answer. We’ll tell Hamun that we’ll head to Wyvern Tor and deal with the bugbears.”

“Wonderful, wonderful,” Hamun said. “And when you’re back, I’ll make sure that I have that information for you. And maybe I’ll even have a nice, fine meal for us to eat.”

“Okay, that sounds promising. I appreciate your hospitality, Hamun. But when we return, I hope your zombies don’t try to attack us.”

“Well — my advice to you is, you don’t walk into somebody’s house without shouting something.”

“Well, technically your house is the tent, and we haven’t walked into it yet.”

“Yeah, well — my zombies are always very protective. You just give me a shout before you come, and I’ll come out.”

“Sure. I’ll keep that in mind, thank you.”

Twelve corpses watched us depart without speaking.

I watched Hamun Kost.
