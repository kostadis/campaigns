Old Owl Well fell behind us, and the hills began their slow campaign against the ankles.

The country around Wyvern Tor had been folded, broken, and folded again. Ridges concealed gullies; gullies narrowed into stony cuts that looked promising until they ended against bare rock. Loose shale shifted under my boots. Thorny scrub caught at my coat and tried to keep portions of it. I kept the blue candle wrapped, though now and then I touched the bundle to reassure myself it was still there.

Zenvon led.

For a while, this meant we followed him up a slope, down the other side, around an outcrop, and into a stretch of ground that looked remarkably like the one we had just left. I began marking distinctive stones in my notebook. When I found one of my marks ahead of us, Zenvon studied the hills with increased seriousness.

There are tactful ways to tell someone they are lost. They preserve dignity, encourage renewed concentration, and avoid damaging the trust necessary between scout and scholar.

“I don't think you know where you're going, buddy.”

He did not answer. That was sensible. The wind moved through the dry grass, and Pip shifted his weight while Maela examined the sky with an expression that suggested she had no intention of involving herself.

We searched for the better part of an hour. Zenvon checked the shallow gullies, climbed rocks for a clearer view, and crouched over patches of earth that contained tracks belonging chiefly to goats. I followed, increasingly certain that Wyvern Tor had either moved or developed a personal objection to being found.

It was not entirely fair. Broken ground hides things. Professor Voss once made me spend an afternoon trying to locate the entrance to a ruin after he had already found it. I accused him of moving the marker stones. He said suspicion was a useful scholarly instinct, but less useful when directed at one’s instructor.

He had moved the marker stones.

Zenvon stopped.

Nothing dramatic announced it. His shoulders settled, and his attention fixed on a narrow cleft between two ridges that I had dismissed as another crease in the hills. He slipped through it, found firm ground beyond, and motioned us after him. The cleft opened onto a rising path that cut cleanly past the worst of the slopes.

Not luck. Not a hopeful guess. He had read the terrain correctly and found the route as though the hills had finally agreed to show him their notes.

He turned to me.

Zenvon said nothing. He stared, held my gaze, and pointed ahead. The message was perfectly clear. I considered reminding him that discovering the correct direction after exhausting several incorrect ones was still a mixed result.

The path before him made that argument difficult.

“Well done.”

The concession cost me very little. I made certain it sounded as though it had cost considerably more.

We took his shortcut.

The ground rose beneath us in a rugged ridge along the hill’s lower slopes. Wind scraped over the stone, carrying the dry smell of dust and sun-warmed grass. Beneath it was something fainter.

Smoke.

I raised a hand. We slowed without speaking. The scent came and went, too thin for a wildfire and too steady to be imagined. Somewhere ahead, someone had built a fire and expected to remain beside it.

Near the crest, I lowered myself behind the ridge and looked through a gap in the scrub. A ravine opened below us, its sides steep with broken stone. At the bottom, several bowshots away, darkness cut into the rock: a cave mouth, low and broad enough to swallow the daylight around its edges.

A boulder stood outside it.

At first, that was all I saw. Then the mass beside the stone shifted. A heavy shoulder rose above the boulder’s edge, followed by the blunt outline of a head. The bugbear had tucked itself into the scant shade some distance from the entrance. It watched the ravine while the smoke from within the cave thinned into the air.

It had not seen us.

I eased back until stone hid the cave again. The others gathered close, careful with boots, buckles, and breath. Zenvon looked from me to the ravine, waiting.

I resisted the urge to congratulate him twice. There are limits. But I opened my notebook on my knee, and when I drew the cave, the sentry, and the line of approach along the ridge, I used the route he had found.

For the moment, the bugbear remained behind its boulder, watching an empty ravine.

We remained above it, deciding how long that illusion should last.
