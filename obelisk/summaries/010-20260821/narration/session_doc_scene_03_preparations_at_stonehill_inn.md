Stonehill Inn was warm, loud, and pleased with us.

Toblen’s face opened the moment we entered. Phandalin had apparently not yet exhausted its gratitude, and we remained the toast of the town. Adoration has a shelf life. For the moment, ours was still fresh.

Zenvon approached the bar. “Hey, Toblen. It's been a pleasure, you know.”

“The heroes! The heroes of Phandalin.” Toblen waved toward Elsa. “Elsa, why don't you get him a beer?”

Elsa poured. The foam rose above the rim, and Zenvon accepted the mug with both the beer and the title intact.

“Thank you. So, I wanted to tell you that my friends and I are leaving for Conyberry tomorrow, first thing in the morning.”

Toblen paused. The delight left his face by degrees. “And why would you want to go there?”

“We have a friend who needs some help, at the Old Owl Well.”

Zenvon took a drink.

“So we are going to the Old Owl Well to help him.”

“Yeah, but why would you go through Conyberry? It's an abandoned town.”

The word *abandoned* did considerable work for him. There are ruins emptied by plague, ruins emptied by war, and ruins emptied because something beneath them has begun answering questions. Treating them as one category is emotionally efficient and academically useless.

“Well, that's the closest to the Old Owl Well. Do you know a better route — one that's kind of safe?”

“Conyberry's been abandoned for years.”

Toblen rubbed at the bar with a cloth, though the wood beneath it was already clean.

“But I guess it's a place you could go.”

He stopped rubbing.

“I don't know about you, but going into abandoned towns doesn't feel safe.”

Another pause. His eyes moved across us, perhaps taking inventory of the people he was warning and finding the inventory discouraging.

“But you're powerful adventurers. Heroes of Phandalin. So I'm sure there's nothing… I'm sure I'm just a barkeep — I'm just an owner of a tavern.”

The qualification arrived after the warning, where it could do no harm to the warning.

“So maybe this is about me more than it is about you.”

That, at least, sounded true.

“Well, that's a fair point — but tell us anything that you know about Old Owl Well or Conyberry, because we have made up our mind to start early in the morning,” Zenvon said. “As much as I like adventures, I'd like to avoid any surprises if possible.”

I opened my notebook. Avoiding surprises is not always possible, but giving them proper names afterward is the foundation of scholarship.

“The town of Conyberry has been abandoned for years and lies in complete ruins,” Toblen said. “So you shouldn't find anything of value there. But any time you have a place that's ruins and is on the road — that's where, well, how shall I put it?”

He folded the cloth and set it down.

“People who are dangerous to the unwary might be.”

“Generous to what?”

Zenvon frowned at him over the beer. He had plainly misheard, but Toblen’s next words rescued us from having to consider the charitable practices of roadside murderers.

“Dangerous to the unwary. People who aren't paying attention.”

“Paying attention. Okay.”

“Right — so what I would do, if you're heading in there, is just be careful, keep your eyes peeled. But if I were you, I would avoid that town.”

It was advice composed entirely of caution and containing no information about what required caution. Toblen had never seen Conyberry. I became certain of that as he spoke: no remembered street, no landmark, no particular sound or scar. Only the idea of ruined walls beside a road, populated by whatever his fear supplied.

“Now, the only other thing: to get to Agatha's, you have to go through there. That's what Sister Garaele tells you.”

“Yep.”

That part mattered. Conyberry was not merely an obstacle on the road to Old Owl Well. Sister Garaele’s errand led through it as well, which meant avoidance would only postpone the question.

The rest of Toblen’s knowledge was old, but sound. Conyberry had been devastated during the Spellplague. Most of it had fallen to magical catastrophe; the inhabitants who remained were later killed by Uthgardt raiders.

I knew the histories. The Spellplague was politely described in surviving texts as an era of divine upheaval. Professor Voss preferred plainer language: the gods played games with mortals, and mortals supplied the pieces, the board, and the graves.

A ruin born from that age was not dangerous merely because brigands might sleep in it. Magic remembers badly. Stone remembers longer.

Zenvon put down his mug. “Okay, so we'll rest. Alright. Toblen — it's been such a wonderful stay.”

I closed my notebook, but left the blue candle wrapped.

Sleep came with clean sheets, a full roof, and the peculiar comfort of knowing precisely how incomplete our information was. By morning, the inn had quieted, pale light lay across the room, and Conyberry remained ruined whether Toblen had seen it or not.
