I managed not to laugh until his back was turned.

“So, we're going to head back to the Stonehill Inn,” Zenvon said.

The inn was warm in the particular way that makes exhaustion declare itself all at once. Firelight shone on the tables, voices filled the rafters, and the smell of beer and cooked onions caught in my clothes. I had been carrying my fatigue carefully until then. Inside the door, I nearly dropped it.

“You know — spend the night here, then start in the morning,” Zenvon said. “So I'm going to tell Toblen that…”

Toblen Stonehill brightened when he saw us. Phandalin had not yet exhausted its gratitude; we were still its heroes, and our welcome had not acquired conditions.

“Hey, Toblen. It's been a pleasure, you know.”

“The heroes! The heroes of Phandalin.” Toblen lifted a hand toward the bar. “Elsa, why don't you get him a beer?”

Elsa poured. Zenvon accepted the mug with both courtesy and purpose.

“Thank you. So, I wanted to tell you that my friends and I are leaving for Conyberry tomorrow, first thing in the morning.”

Toblen stopped smiling for a moment.

“And why would you want to go there?”

“We have a friend who needs some help, at the Old Owl Well.”

That description was generous. It was also efficient.

“So we are going to the Old Owl Well to help him.”

“Yeah, but why would you go through Conyberry? It's an abandoned town.”

I had been setting my books on the table. At *abandoned town*, I stopped and left one in my hands.

“Well, that's the closest to the Old Owl Well. Do you know a better route — one that's kind of safe?”

“Conyberry's been abandoned for years.”

Toblen rubbed one thumb along the edge of the bar. His gaze moved from Zenvon to the rest of us, then back.

“But I guess it's a place you could go.”

He paused again.

“I don't know about you, but going into abandoned towns doesn't feel safe.”

On that point, scholarship and tavernkeeping were in complete agreement.

“But you're powerful adventurers. Heroes of Phandalin. So I'm sure there's nothing… I'm sure I'm just a barkeep — I'm just an owner of a tavern.”

His voice had lost some of its welcome. Not because he wanted us gone. Because he imagined us failing to return.

“So maybe this is about me more than it is about you.”

“Well, that's a fair point — but tell us anything that you know about Old Owl Well or Conyberry, because we have made up our mind to start early in the morning.” Zenvon raised his mug but did not drink. “As much as I like adventures, I'd like to avoid any surprises if possible.”

“The town of Conyberry has been abandoned for years and lies in complete ruins.”

I opened my notebook.

“So you shouldn't find anything of value there. But any time you have a place that's ruins and is on the road — that's where, well, how shall I put it?”

His hesitation put more danger into Conyberry than certainty would have.

“People who are dangerous to the unwary might be.”

“Generous to what?”

“Dangerous to the unwary. People who aren't paying attention.”

“Paying attention. Okay.”

I wrote *dangerous to the unwary*, although it was not useful. Ruins were dangerous to the unwary. So were roads, inns, cellars, employers, sealed letters, and interesting stones. The difficulty was determining where attention should be placed before something demonstrated the answer.

“Right — so what I would do, if you're heading in there, is just be careful, keep your eyes peeled. But if I were you, I would avoid that town.”

Toblen drew a breath.

“Now, the only other thing: to get to Agatha's, you have to go through there. That's what Sister Garaele tells you.”

“Yep.”

Zenvon watched him closely. I did too. Toblen possessed no remembered dust from Conyberry’s road, no turn of phrase polished by repetition, no particular doorway or broken wall that troubled him. He had never been there. His warning came from fear, not witness.

The history beneath it was true.

The Spellplague had broken Conyberry first. Magic had swept through and destroyed most of the town. Later, Uthgardt raiders came for those who remained and finished what catastrophe had begun.

I stared at the words in my notebook.

First the gods played their games with mortal lives. Then armed hands arrived among the ruins.

There was something obscene in the order of it. Magic destroyed the walls; ordinary cruelty killed whoever had survived them. Years later, the road still passed through, and travelers were advised to keep their eyes peeled—as if vigilance were sufficient protection against history repeating itself.

Professor Orryn Voss used to say a ruin is only dead if nothing remembers it. I had thought that was beautiful.

Lately, I had started to think it was a warning.

“Okay, so we'll rest. Alright. Toblen — it's been such a wonderful stay.”

The room softened as the night deepened. My candle burned blue beside the notebook while voices faded, mugs emptied, and chairs scraped back from tables. I read my notes once more before sleep: Spellplague. Uthgardt. Agatha beyond the ruins. Old Owl Well farther on.

Morning found me awake beneath the last thin curl of blue smoke.
