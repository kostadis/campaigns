We took the direct road northeast toward Old Owl Well. Conyberry remained off our route.

I had no objection. The ruins offered Sister Garaele’s business, but Old Owl Well offered Daran Edermath’s, and we had already given our word on both. The difference was distance. One obligation could be reached without spending another day pretending the road was safe.

The Triboar Trail carried us farther than I expected before the light began to fail. I measured our progress against bends in the road, patches of woodland, and the rising ground ahead. It came to roughly what I remembered a party could cover in a day.

That made sense.

Nothing attacked us.

This was good, but it was not evidence of safety. It meant only that nothing had chosen that place and that time. We set our watches, kept the light controlled, and passed the night without hearing feet approach through the dark.

Morning gave us the evidence the night had withheld.

The caravan had been pulled apart beside the trail. Ruts left the road and ended in churned earth. Broken fittings lay among the grass, and discarded pieces had been stripped of anything worth carrying. There were no owners waiting to reclaim them and no attackers foolish enough to leave their names.

I studied what remained.

The Wyvern Tor marauders had been attacking travellers. That was the report. This could have been their work, which was not the same as proof. Goblins used these roads. Redbrands had operated outside Phandalin. There could be another group nobody had thought to warn us about, which would have been inconvenient but not surprising.

The useful fact was simpler: someone had attacked traffic on the Triboar Trail and abandoned the caravan here.

The road itself was empty. I looked in both directions and saw no wagon canvas, no horses, no travellers moving between us and the horizon. There were wheel marks, hoofprints, and old boot tracks enough to show that people still used it. Just not while we were watching.

An empty road with fresh tracks is not abandoned. It is avoided carefully.

The High Road was the real trade route, running north and south through Neverwinter. This east-west trail was a narrower proposition. Fewer caravans. Fewer witnesses. Fewer guards arriving because somebody had failed to reach an inn before dark.

I had thought of Neverwinter as a city with wilderness around it. The distinction was more severe than that. Neverwinter and the land close enough for its authority to matter were civilization. Beyond that boundary, roads continued, settlements continued, and promises continued. Protection did not.

The hinterlands had been worked over thoroughly. Barbarians, orcs, goblins, wars, the Spellplague, meddling gods. Wizards who should have known better.

That last category seemed unnecessarily broad until I considered Veyra’s account of her missing teacher, the black stone I had carried without mentioning, and how often magical problems began with someone explaining that they understood the risks.

Right, okay.

I kept us moving.

Leading had been easier when it meant walking ahead and checking for an ambush. There was a price to that work. You found the danger before everyone else did.

Now they also watched where I turned.

Pip could take the front when steel was required. Sister Maela could keep us standing after steel had been required. Veyra could identify the thing that should not exist and, frequently, set it on fire. But the road still narrowed beneath my feet, and I was the one measuring how far we had come, how much daylight remained, and which obligation we would reach first.

Nothing attacked us that night either.

By the following day, the ground had begun to rise and fold around the trail. Hills crowded the route, rough enough to hide movement and too numerous to treat as one destination. Old Owl Well was not all of them. It was a particular place somewhere among them.

That was useful. A marked destination can be approached.

The hills around it were another matter.
