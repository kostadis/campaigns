Zenvon had learned new tricks without gaining the good sense to rest before trying them. His invisible hand and borrowed sounds proved useful almost immediately. Beneath Tresendar Manor, four bugbears remained in their barracks, the nothic still prowled the crevasse, and the Dendrars were waiting behind a door they had barricaded against the dead.

“Hey guys, it's me, I'm back for you, it's all safe now, you can let go of the door, let me help you,” Zenvon called.

Wood scraped over stone. The door opened cautiously, and Mirna Dendrar stood behind it with Nars and Nilsa, all three hollow-eyed with hunger. Outside, the skeletons studied our red cloaks, stepped aside, and returned silently to their places.

Zenvon offered food and water first. Sensible. Starving people have little use for speeches.

Mirna told us the Redbrands had murdered her husband, Thel, and meant to kill the rest of them. She had invented wealthy relatives in Neverwinter and promised ransom, buying each day with a lie she hoped would live longer than her captors’ patience.

Then she asked where Thel was.

Zenvon tried to answer. His eyes shifted, his words slowed, and Mirna’s face tightened. He looked to me.

There are truths that must be spoken plainly, and truths that become cruelty when every detail is included. I told her Thel was dead. I did not tell her about the feeding sounds from the crevasse or what might remain when Ssarnak finished. She had endured enough without carrying that picture for the rest of her life.

In return for honesty she gave us another burden. Her family’s old alchemy shop stood in southeastern Thundertree, with an emerald necklace hidden behind the storage shelves. We could take it if we ever passed that way.

She also asked us to bring Thel’s body home.

I said we would try. Promises are light when spoken. They acquire their weight later.

We escorted Mirna and her children out through the ruined manor and left them safely at the edge of Phandalin. Then we went back below.

The apothecary smelled of dust and old reagents. Beyond it came the wet tearing sounds from the cavern. Zenvon conjured the noise of Iarno “Glasstaff” Albrek working in his workshop. The feeding stopped. Something crept away through the dark to investigate.

Zenvon climbed down into the fissure and found a concealed chest. He opened it from a distance with his spectral hand. Traps, mimics, and hungry things under lids all teach the same lesson: do not touch. Adventurers remain poor students.

Inside were silver and gold, five malachites, potions for healing, climbing, and growth, and a finely made magical sword. We did not know it was Talon, once carried by Aldith Tresendar. We knew only that it was valuable and sharp.

Pip received it. No debate was necessary. He held the blade with open delight, and for a moment the cellar seemed less foul.

Then we approached the bugbear barracks.

I pushed past Zenvon at the door and pressed my ear to the wood. Several gruff voices were barking commands at one miserable goblin. Zenvon eased the door open with his invisible hand, and we entered in our stolen cloaks.

Droop saw us and fainted.

Nosk did not. He looked Zenvon over and sneered, “leave it to a Redbrand to think he's in charge.”

Zenvon heard the important part beneath the insult. These brutes did not serve Glasstaff. They answered directly to Nezznar the Spider.

He began asking what Nezznar would think of Nosk’s conduct. Each question was placed precisely where pride was already tender. Nosk’s temper broke, and he blurted out that Nezznar was at Cragmaw Castle.

The triumph lasted only until Nosk understood what he had revealed.

“It don't matter that now you know, because now I kill you!”

The room erupted.

Zenvon released a wave of thunder that struck two bugbears and shook the walls. Nosk answered with his morning star. The blow folded Zenvon nearly double and left him wavering on his feet.

“Hold on — I have you.”

Divine warmth answered my hand, flowing into him just before his legs gave way. It was enough to keep him upright.

It was not enough to make the fight winnable.

Another bugbear struck him, and I felt the cold certainty of it: the next clean blow would finish him. Courage is not remaining in a room until everyone is dead. Sometimes courage is admitting which side that will be.

We ran.

Pip slammed the barracks door behind us. Zenvon emptied flask after flask of oil across the passage, then sent his invisible hand back with a flame. The door splintered as the bugbears broke through. Fire rolled across the slick stones, scorching them and tangling their pursuit in smoke, heat, and treacherous footing.

Veyra stumbled as we raced through the tunnels. I caught her arm and hauled her onward.

At the crevasse, something unfolded from the shadows. The nothic watched us with its single swollen eye and gave us its name.

Ssarnak.

It leaned close to Veyra as she passed. Her teacher was not dead, it whispered. He was “not in a place where dead is easy.”

Veyra stopped so suddenly my grip nearly slipped. All the color left her face. Then she twisted toward the crevasse, trying to wrench herself free and return to it.

I could not persuade her. There was no time to find words sharp enough to cut through hope.

So I locked both arms around her and dragged her away.

She fought me. I held on. Whatever answer Ssarnak possessed, it was offering that answer from a cavern filling with smoke while bugbears pursued us. Orryn Voss might be alive. Veyra had to remain alive to find him.

We reached the manor entrance, slammed the door, and locked it. The first impact from the other side shuddered through the wood before my breathing had steadied.

Outside, in the sudden quiet, Veyra asked Pip about the black stone he had once sold. She wanted to know how it had felt in his hand.

Zenvon reached into his pocket.

He had carried a black stone since the road and told none of us. When he produced it, Veyra’s blue crystal flared brighter. The light sharpened every exhausted face around it.

Veyra spoke of Orryn Voss and his belief that some device was controlling people. The stone, Pip’s shard, the altered goblins, the corpses whose strained faces matched Sildar Hallwinter’s account, and Ssarnak’s promise about her teacher gathered into one ugly possibility.

They watched the light.

I watched what the light did to them.

I said nothing. Suspicion is not proof, and fear dressed as certainty has sent too many good souls marching in the wrong direction. My expression soured despite me. When they looked for an explanation, I gave them none.

At the Stonehill Inn, Toblen offered us rooms without charge for freeing Phandalin from the Redbrands. Zenvon paid to have food brought upstairs. We arrived bloodied, smoke-stained, and praised as heroes while bugbears hammered on a locked door beneath the hill.

We slept at last.

Afterward we brought Sildar Hallwinter the letter from Glasstaff’s desk. It proved that Iarno Albrek had served Nezznar all along. Sildar paid the balance he owed us and placed a bounty on Iarno, preferring him alive for trial in Neverwinter but admitting that a corpse would not trouble him greatly.

Zenvon argued that Nezznar mattered more. Sildar agreed that Iarno might have fled to his master, allowing us to catch “two birds with one stone.”

I had heard enough about stones.

At the Shrine of Luck, Sister Garaele called us the new heroes of Phandalin. She entrusted us with a jeweled silver comb for Agatha, a banshee who might know the location of Bowgentle’s spellbook. Garaele offered healing potions in return. Veyra found Bowgentle’s name in her notes, and Zenvon accepted while warning that more urgent matters might delay us.

Our final visit was to Halia Thornton at the Phandalin Miner’s Exchange. Her congratulations were warm until she learned Glasstaff had escaped. Then the warmth left her eyes.

She asked whether we had found correspondence among his belongings and offered gold for it, claiming concern for local trade. Zenvon told her the information was worth far more.

They parted politely.

Nothing about it felt polite.
