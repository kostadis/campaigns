The hills beyond Old Owl Well were excellent at impersonating one another.

The same shoulders of grey stone rose out of the same coarse grass. The same narrow cuts promised passage and ended in thorn or loose shale. Wyvern Tor remained somewhere ahead of us, which was useful in much the same sense that knowing Neverwinter lay somewhere north was useful.

Zenvon led.

I followed with my books secured against the climb and my blue candle wrapped tightly enough that no stray pulse could announce us to every marauder in the hills. The ground kept shifting under my boots. Pebbles clicked down the slopes whenever Pip or Maela put a foot wrong, while Zenvon ranged ahead, paused, examined some mark I could not see, and chose another promising stretch of indistinguishable rock.

The better part of an hour passed.

We crossed one stony rise, descended into a shallow gully, and climbed out again. At the top, I looked back and saw our earlier tracks crossing below us.

“I don't think you know where you're going, buddy.”

It was not a scholarly assessment. Professor Voss had taught me to qualify uncertain conclusions, distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence, and never mock a colleague before checking the map.

Professor Voss had not spent the morning climbing the same hill twice.

Zenvon gave me nothing. No argument, no wounded dignity, not even the courtesy of pretending the second search was based on information unavailable during the first. He turned back toward the slopes and studied them again.

This time, something clicked.

I saw it happen in the sudden stillness of his shoulders. His attention moved from a scuff in the dirt to a break in the brush, then upward along a fold in the ridge I had dismissed as another dead end. He slipped through the rocks, found firm ground where I had seen only scrub, and led us into a narrow passage that cut cleanly through the worst of the climb.

It was not merely the correct trail. It was a shortcut so direct that the surrounding terrain began to look less accidental, as though the hills had been hiding a seam and Zenvon had put a knife into it.

He stopped where the passage opened, turned, and stared at me.

Then he pointed ahead.

The stare continued.

The point remained.

*Trust me this time.*

There are moments when a person may preserve dignity, and moments when the evidence has become tiresomely complete.

“well done.”

I kept my voice low. Approval does not improve through volume.

The first trace of smoke reached me as we climbed the lower ridge. It was faint and stale, caught close to the ground rather than rising in any visible column. Woodsmoke, certainly, though underneath it sat the greasy suggestion of something cooked over an ill-kept fire. The breeze carried it away, returned it, and took it again.

At the crest, I lowered myself beside the others and looked down.

The ravine split the hillside below us. Roughly fifty yards away, a cave mouth opened in its base: dark stone around deeper darkness, broad enough to admit creatures who had no need to duck. There were no banners, no stacked supplies, no helpful sign declaring that marauders had taken up residence. Only smoke, disturbed ground, and the dark.

Then the boulder moved.

Not the boulder itself. Something hunkered behind it shifted its weight, and a broad, furred shoulder came into view. A bugbear crouched there, perhaps twenty yards outside the cave, using the stone as cover while it watched the approaches.

I went still.

The sentry had not seen us. Its head turned across the ravine, slow and watchful, but never lifted toward our ridge. Zenvon checked the line between us and the boulder, then checked it again. We remained outside the creature’s sight, tucked behind broken stone with the cave plainly visible below.

A short while earlier, I had doubted Zenvon could find the camp.

Now we were close enough to smell its fire, and its guard did not know we existed.

I drew my notebook out by inches, careful that the clasp made no sound. Doubt was useful. So was revising a conclusion when presented with superior evidence.

Reluctant approval is still approval.
