By noon the hills had become an argument between my boots and the ground. The ground was winning.

We crested a low ridge and found the Old Owl Well below us: a crumbling watchtower, a courtyard hemmed in by mounds of fallen stone, and one colorful tent pitched amid the ruin. No smoke. No voices. No movement.

“The colorful tent indicates that there can be somebody,” Zenvon said. “And what's the circular thing? Is that the watchtower?”

“It’s the old watchtower. And the well is the… Old Owl Well, I suppose.” He studied the empty courtyard. “So it looks like it's pretty empty — but I sense some eeriness in the air.”

The feeling was real enough. Old stone keeps memories. Some are only damp and darkness. Some wait.

“I suggest my team tiptoe and walk slowly. Towards the well.”

Zenvon proceeded to tiptoe. Deliberately. Across rubble.

I have heard quieter crockery dropped down a stairwell.

We passed through the main entrance, and the dead began coming out of the tower.

There were too many to count comfortably. Feet dragged across stone. Slack arms swung. Heads lolled with each uneven step as corpse after corpse shambled into the courtyard.

“Are they not going to talk?” Zenvon asked.

No. They were not.

Zombies have no speech because there is no one left inside them to speak. Whatever thoughts, prayers, grudges, and small kindnesses once belonged to those bodies had been stripped away. What remained moved because another will had reached into dead flesh and pulled.

I looked from the corpses to the bright tent.

“There's probably a necromancer.”

Zenvon’s confusion lasted long enough that I had to continue.

“A magic user who takes the dead corpses and reanimates them.”

The words tasted foul. There are proper rites for the dead: clean hands, steady prayers, names spoken clearly. Necromancy makes tools of what should have been entrusted to silence.

“Very evil magic.”

More of them emerged.

“And very dangerous magic, and very powerful magic, given the number…”

“Boss, I don't know if we want to fight all these zombies,” Pip said.

Sensibly put. My holy symbol was warm against my chest, answering the wrongness before us, but faith is not permission to mistake suicide for duty.

“Let's see if we can deceive them, or…” Zenvon watched the dead spread through the courtyard. “I'm hoping there is a central control for all of these monsters — if we can find the necromancer.”

“Well, if there's going to be a necromancer, he's probably in that tent,” Veyra said.

Zenvon drew breath.

“So I'm going to shout: hey, Zombie Lord.” Then, louder: “Hey, Zombie Lord — are you afraid to face us? Is that why you're sending the zombies?”

It was not the title I would have chosen for a powerful practitioner of evil magic while surrounded by his servants.

It worked.

A man stepped from the tent, his head shaved and marked with tattoos.

“WHAT IS THE MEANING OF THIS?”

Veyra stared at the symbols, then pulled out her notebook and flicked through the pages.

“Yup — he's a wizard. He's a wizard. Do you see those tattoos on his head?”

I saw them. Curved marks laid into the skin like ownership brands.

“They're the symbols of a necromancer!”

Our recollection went no further. It did not need to. The walking corpses had already supplied the missing scholarship.

Zenvon raised his empty hands. “Yeah, so — we are just here to talk to you, nothing else. We don't want to fight unnecessarily. My aim here is just to talk to you, and head back.”

The stranger demanded a name.

“My name is Zenvon. These are my friends — Pip, Sister Maela, and Veyra. How do you introduce yourself?”

“As Hamun Kost.”

“Hamun Kost. Hamun Kost.” Zenvon took a breath. “Cut right to the point, Hamun. I just wanted to know — what are you doing here in the lost town of Conyberry, and at this Old Owl Well?”

“Well, I'm a wizard,” Hamun said. “And it's not polite to ask what a wizard is doing here. But this is an archaeological dig that I am interested in.”

“Well, what's so special about this place that drew your interest?”

“Look — I am not interested. I'm just interested in this tower. And quite frankly, I don't think I should have to answer why I'm interested in this tower. So why don't we just keep it—” He stopped himself. “I'm a wizard, and I have the things I'm interested in.”

That explanation would have been inadequate even without the corpses.

“Well, Hamun, you seem like a nice wizard, and I know you've been doing your own thing, but…” Zenvon gestured at the dead surrounding us. “Whatever you're doing — your presence here is kind of alarming to the neighboring lands of Neverwinter and Phandalin and other places. So I want to keep my citizens safe, and that's why I just want to ensure that.”

“My — my dear boy! Zenvon, is that your name again?”

“Yeah.”

“I can assure you that I am not interested in harming anyone in this area.”

“Well, that's reassuring — but it would help us be a little calmer if you tell me what you're doing. And I swear your secret does not go out; it stays with us.”

Hamun smiled.

“My dear boy — if I wanted my secrets to be safe, I would kill you. Now, I would like you to know that I am a Red Wizard of Thay. And a Red Wizard of Thay is not someone you should be—”

“So… hold on, hold on.”

I knew enough. Thay had made power into government and cruelty into policy. Hamun had just told us that murdering us would be an administrative solution, not a moral difficulty.

The dead continued gathering around him.

“Look — I don't want to hurt your community,” Hamun said. “But I want to show that I want to be a positive member of the community.”

I looked at the nearest corpse. Its mouth hung open.

“Well, how exactly, Mr. Hamun? How can you help the community?” Zenvon asked.

“Well, I can give you information.”

“Go on. I'm all ears.”

“What is the one thing you want to know? Where Cragmaw Castle is, for example?”

Silence followed. Zenvon waited for Hamun to continue. Hamun waited for Zenvon to ask. Meanwhile, the last of the zombies settled around the wizard in a wall of dead flesh.

The message was plain. We were negotiating because Hamun permitted it.

“Now… Zenvon. What is it that you would like to know?”

“Well, Hamun, there are a lot of things I would like to know — but the one thing that stands on top is where Cragmaw Castle is located.”

“Of course I know where that is!”

“Interesting. Wow. Where can we find the castle?”

“You know, I think I will be happy to exchange that information — if you were to do a small little thing for me.”

“I thought you were a Red Wizard. I'm surprised that I could do something for you.”

“Look, my friend. Look, Zenvon. There are these marauders at Wyvern Tor. They've scouted out my camp, and they seem inclined to cause trouble. I'd rather you deal with that trouble than I deal with that trouble.”

Convenient. He could command a courtyard full of corpses but preferred living strangers to absorb the danger.

“So if you were to go deal with those… with those marauders, I would be more than happy to provide you the location of Cragmaw Castle.”

Zenvon pressed him for particulars. Hamun admitted there were bugbears and an ogre.

“Old girl?” Zenvon asked.

“Ogre.”

“Yeah, okay.”

“Not that I couldn't deal with it myself — but I don't want to have to raise more zombies to do my business.”

There it was. More bodies, if required. More graves emptied of dignity. I kept my hand away from my holy symbol. Revulsion is useful when it tells you where evil stands. It is less useful when evil has surrounded you and still possesses information that may save the living.

“But if that's not what you're interested in — I have another quest for you,” Hamun said.

“Go on. Let me see if I can do that for you, at least.”

“You may have heard there's a banshee named Agatha.”

“Yes, I quite certainly have heard of her.”

“Right. I want to know the name of the wizard who built the tower at the Old Owl Well.”

“Why don't you ask her yourself?”

Hamun hesitated. I did not let him escape it.

“Why don't you ask her yourself?”

“Well, I mean — it's a banshee. And I don't want to get killed by a banshee.”

Honesty, at last.

“Either deal with the Wyvern Tor lot, or get me the name from Agatha. The information of where Cragmaw Castle is, is yours.”

Zenvon drew us aside. “It looks like going to Agatha seems a bit of a suicide mission, so… It's better to deal with the bugbears and the ogres.”

“Well, if we were trying to kill her, I couldn't agree with you more. But our goal isn't to kill the banshee. So if we go there and are very respectful, we could avoid getting killed by it.”

I heard my own argument and disliked every word of it. Respect is proper. Respect is not armor.

“Still, that seems much riskier than fighting with the bugbears, which we're already familiar with,” Zenvon said.

He was right. I wished he were not.

“Look — if the choice is killing bugbears and ogres, or going and chatting with a banshee, I'm for dealing with ogres and bugbears Firstday, Secondday, Thirdday… and twice on the tenth day.”

“Okay, then we have our answer. We'll tell Hamun that we'll head to Wyvern Tor and deal with the bugbears.”

“Wonderful, wonderful,” Hamun said. “And when you're back, I'll make sure that I have that information for you. And maybe I'll even have a nice, fine meal for us to eat.”

“Okay, that sounds promising. I appreciate your hospitality, Hamun. But when we return, I hope your zombies don't try to attack us.”

“Well — my advice to you is, you don't walk into somebody's house without shouting something.”

“Well, technically your house is the tent, and we haven't walked into it yet.”

“Yeah, well — my zombies are always very protective. You just give me a shout before you come, and I'll come out.”

“Sure. I'll keep that in mind, thank you.”

Hamun remained among his dead servants as we prepared to leave the courtyard. I had come ready to oppose evil. Instead, I had bargained with it and chosen which danger I preferred.

Ogres, certainly.

Twice on the tenth day.
