## Zinnia — The Gargoyle Ambush

The gargoyles came out of the dark ceiling before I could name what they were: stone wings, grey skin, the ambush already sprung by the time the shapes resolved. That is the problem with things that fly. There is no floor to read them off of.

One came for me. It hit hard, a raking blow that got through everything I had set against it, then a second that opened the first wider, and I knew I had less left than I'd thought. Then it beat its wings and climbed out of reach, back up into the dark where I could not follow.

*Oh, good.* "Goodbye."

Around Calmer the air had gone wrong: a shimmering zone that made anything crossing into it move like it was pushing through deep water. A second gargoyle blundered into it, slowed to a wading crawl, and swung at him anyway. It missed. A third came at him, missed, and fled the way it had come. Whatever he was holding in his mind, he kept holding it through the hit.

"Gentlemen," Calmer called out, cheerful as a man ordering wine, "don't wish to be rude, but perhaps some protection might be in order?" A beat. "Don't forget, I am the Chief Cleric. If I die, bad things will happen to all of you."

"Okay," Zephyr said. "Let's protect Calmer."

They were flying. That was the whole of it. Two of them out of reach in the black, and me on the ground with nothing that reaches the black. For half a breath I weighed stepping through space to meet one. And set it aside. Too tidy. Too little violence in it.

So I ran instead. Everything unnecessary fell away and the body took over: a burst of speed, ground eaten in a straight line, and then the last stride wasn't a stride at all, it was up. Every step a decision made in the body before the mind caught up. I left the floor. My first strike was a joke, all air, my hand finding nothing, and then I was above where I'd started, past the point where I should have begun falling, and instead of falling I caught the thing. Both hands into cold stone hide. It tried to turn its claws on me for the insult of holding on. I didn't let it. I clung and let it carry my weight instead of the floor.

Somewhere below, another one of us had gone up the same way. Sequoia, muttering a word over himself and springing after a gargoyle of his own, missing his cut but airborne, which he had not been a moment ago.

"I can't tell if you're—" Sequoia was saying to someone. "I don't know if you're an idiot or not, but I'm kind of on top of something right now."

"Oh," said Zephyr, "we were allowed to jump up?"

"No. I've got a working that throws me up there."

"Can't jump out."

Zephyr's pistol cracked somewhere beneath us, a bark of powder at a shape in the air. Then Calmer let go of whatever he'd been saving: a lance of force that split and washed over two of them at once, stone flinching under it.

My moment came, and I was still riding mine.

I had one hand's worth of purchase and one clean line into the seam where wing met back, and I put everything into it. The body knew the target before I did. Stone cracked, and then it wasn't holding itself up anymore, dead weight, all of it, mine and its together, and the floor coming up fast.

I did not fall so much as I steered the corpse into the fall ahead of me. It hit first. I hit it. A twist at the last instant, my weight rolling off dead stone the way you roll off a springboard, and the ground took the gargoyle and the gargoyle took me, and I came up on my feet with nothing worse to show for the drop than dust.

That should have been the end of the accounting. It wasn't.

Something large at the edge of the room, something I had filed as furniture, as an idol, as scenery, moved. Not stepped. Uncoiled. Projections came off it, several at once, reaching out into the space we'd just cleared, testing the air where the gargoyles had been.

I got my footing under me and watched it come.

## Zephyr — The Juggernaut's Rampage

The idol woke up.

I had been watching it the whole fight: a slab of stone that had no business moving, and then it moved. Projections came out of it. Pseudopods, reaching, unfolding like a thing deciding it was alive after all. It rolled. Stone that rolls. *I've heard of worse in the Abyss, but this is bad.*

Behind me Calmer was getting swarmed, gargoyles peeling at his plate like they meant to open a tin. "Gentlemen," he said, in the voice he uses when he wants everyone to know he's still the smartest man in the room, "don't wish to be rude, but perhaps some protection might be in order?" And then, because one line is never enough for him: "Don't forget, I am the Chief Cleric. If I die, bad things will happen to all of you."

He's not wrong. The healer is the one asset you don't spend. I sighed. "Okay. Let's protect Calmer."

Sequoia was riding a gargoyle. Actually on its back, legs around it, telling anyone who'd listen he was *kind of on top of something right now* and couldn't tell if the rest of us were idiots or not. Zinnia went up off the altar, came down on a gargoyle, killed it, and landed light on the corpse like it was a springboard. The dance was going fine.

Then the idol reached out and hit Calmer with three of those stone limbs at once, a blow like a wall falling on him, and he sagged, and held, and did not go down. The working he was holding never slipped. Good man. Louder-than-necessary man. Held.

That was when I understood the idol was the whole problem. A Juggernaut. Everything else in the room was noise.

I put a bolt into it from the pistol and slid back toward the altar before it could answer, keeping myself near Calmer. Sequoia's gargoyle dropped under him and he stepped off the falling stone clean, like a man walking off a gangplank onto solid ground. The room was almost ours.

Then the thing came for me.

It rolled, the entire weight of it, a mountain deciding to lie down where I was standing, and I moved before I thought about moving. The stone passed and I wasn't under it. *Come and get it.* It missed completely.

So I tried to take it apart the way I take things apart. Trip it: nothing, it's a wheel, you can't topple a wheel. Poison the blade: nothing, the venom found nothing in it to kill. Every tool in the box and none of them fit the lock. *This again.* It rolled through the altar behind us and turned it to rubble, a whole sacred slab reduced to gravel, and some cold accountant's part of me noted that the stone had probably meant something to the Water Temple and meant nothing at all to me. Move on.

Sequoia and I traded positions, keeping each other's angles clean, and his dagger bit deep. We were making progress. We were.

Then it came back for me and this time it did not roll. It just hit. Three times, stone on flesh, and then there was no floor.

Dark. No pain, exactly: pain is for the living and I was somewhere adjacent to that. Somewhere far off there was a tug, a hook set in whatever's left of a man when the body quits, pulling, and something in me pulled back and held. The first pull, resisted, and I knew somehow there'd be others. And through the dark, dimly, a hand at my collar, my heels dragging over rubble, someone hauling me like a sack of grain through a doorway. *The assassin. Cargo now.* The blow that put me down should have been worse than it was, later I'd learn Sequoia had bent something so it landed softer, but not soft enough. I stayed under. The wraith had promised one of us would die today, and for a black stretch I wondered, with no drama left in me, whether this was the debt coming due.

Then warmth. Calmer's warmth, holy fire poured into a corpse until it remembers it isn't one, and I was back on my feet with the taste of stone in my mouth and a very short list of grievances.

I looked at the gargoyle still worrying the door. "Now, I will exact my revenge on the juggernaut." Wrong target, right sentiment. Sequoia was at my shoulder, and I put my blade through the gargoyle, and it died.

The Juggernaut was in bad shape; the whole party had been carving at it while I was drooling on the floor. Calmer was at the door asking nobody in particular, "do you think the doors are gonna stop him?" and answering himself: "maybe it will, maybe it won't, but if I slow him down, he can't race as fast after us." Fine. Slow it. Everyone had a job.

Then Sequoia turned to me with that flat halfling honesty. "Would you mind being the sacrificial lamb again?"

Me. The one who sends other people into the water. Stand still, bleed, draw the mountain.

"Yes, yes."

I stood in front of it. It came, and Calmer walked into the fight and finished it, and the great rolling weight went still.

## Sequoia — The Fall of the Juggernaut

The Juggernaut couldn't roll over anyone, so it did the next thing. It turned on Zephyr and swung three times.

The first two he took. The third dropped him. A hit like that doesn't hurt so much as unmake you for a second: one moment standing, the next a heap of tiefling on brown marble, badge and all.

*That's not good.*

I had a window. Not a good one. Whatever attacked him, I could make it swing again, hurried and clumsy. So I did. The stone arm came back around and went wide of where it had been, the air itself flinching, and the blow landed softer.

Softer wasn't enough. He was still down. I'd shaved the edge off a thing that had already cut him open. The margin I'd bought was the difference between bad and worse, and he was still lying in it.

So I moved. Not through the Juggernaut. You don't dash through the Juggernaut, that's a lesson you only need once. Around. I got a fist in Zephyr's collar and dragged him back toward the others, dead weight scraping stone the whole way. Good thing I am small. Nobody clocks the halfling hauling the body.

Calmer looked at Zinnia. "Let's heal this guy. First things first." The light went into Zephyr and he came back up, not whole, but breathing, blinking at the ceiling like a man surfacing from deep water, not sure which way was up. He said something that didn't mean anything. That's what coming back from dead sounds like.

Then Calmer got up. "Gentlemen, let me do something." He opened the door on the thing and put divine weight into it: a slow, grinding cold that settled over the stone and dragged at it.

"Do you think the doors are gonna stop him?" he asked, no one in particular.

"Weird," Zinnia said.

"Maybe it will, maybe it won't," Calmer said, "but if I slow him down, he can't race as fast after us." Fair. The stone thing took the blow and its grinding got heavier. It was looking bad now.

Then the last gargoyle started banging at the far door.

Zinnia and I threw our shoulders against it. He's got the reach and the muscle; I've got neither. This isn't my kind of work. The door bucked, held for a breath on his strength, and then the thing on the other side put its weight in and the door gave anyway. Two of us and it still opened. *This again.*

But it didn't get its swing in, not yet. Zinnia hit it once, clean, then pulled back. "I'll go behind Calmer." Sensible. You don't stand next to a thing made of rock if you don't have to.

The Juggernaut stopped moving. Just stopped, mid-chamber, gathering itself. That was the opening.

Zephyr took the gargoyle. I was right on top of it, which is all he needs: one blade in the right place and the thing came apart on the stone. He'd wanted his revenge. He got a gargoyle instead. It counts.

Then me. The Juggernaut had almost nothing left in it: a couple of good hits and it was scrap.

"Zephyr, could you stay there," I said. Then, because it needed saying plainly: could he stand nearby so I could get in behind the guard. The sacrificial lamb again. He knew the word before I finished it.

"Yes, yes," he said. He always says yes.

I moved him into place and put my sword where the plates didn't meet. Serious hit. Not a kill, the thing was still grinding. So I peeled off clean, giving nothing away, and took Zephyr back out with me, hauling him clear before it could swing after him.

It swung anyway. The stone came around after him, slow, off-balance from the cold Calmer had laid on it, and I spent the last of what I had. The blow went wide, the air bending it away from him. Second time I'd put my body between Zephyr and a thing that wanted him dead. Both times it was a swing I'd invited by using him as bait. Put him in harm's way, then get him clear. Same move, both halves. That's the job.

Whatever I had, it was gone now. Nothing left in the bag but knives.

Calmer walked into the ring of light he'd called up, calm as a man crossing a room, and hit the thing where it counted. The Juggernaut stopped moving. This time for good: the grind died out of it and the stone just stood there, done.

"Whoops!" Calmer said. "That was a bit of a scrap."

## Sequoia — Looting the Water Temple

The fighting was done and the room had gone quiet the way rooms do after: the smell of it still hanging, water pooling somewhere it hadn't been before. The others sat down where they stood and let the wounds knit, breath coming back into them by degrees. Zinnia dropped against a pillar. "Hell yeah." A little later, "Beautiful." Then, "I'm done." He looked better standing up than he had going down.

Calmer didn't sit. He stayed on his feet, plate and all, and didn't rest. I noted it and didn't ask.

*My turn to work.*

The thing we'd killed, the big one, the Juggernaut, was crusted over with stones. Small ones, dull, the kind you'd pass in a streambed. I went over it with my hands and counted them off as they came loose. Agates. A pocketful, no more than that, each one worth about what a decent meal costs in a town that isn't at war.

Calmer watched the pile grow and got warm about it. "See? I told you gentlemen, that we would get lots of treasure. Don't you agree with me that I made the right call?"

"Didn't we all agree with you?"

He let that go. He was happy. Let him be happy.

I already knew what I was carrying. This didn't move the number much. But money is money and you don't leave it on a corpse. I kept counting.

Then the real question, the one that had been sitting under all of it. "Is there any, like — magical items that come with being the leader?"

We ran this place now. Nobody voted us in. You don't get put in charge of a temple by being liked. So it followed that the chair came with things. "Is there, like, a royal stash anywhere?"

There was, and I'd walked past it. Belsornig's room. We'd killed him and gone through it fast, too fast, in a hurry to be somewhere else, and we'd never really looked. Never looked at the body. Money on the table, left there for weeks because we'd been moving.

"You wanna go over there?" Then I answered myself. "Alright, let's go."

The clerics' room first: the two that dropped in the first breath of the fight, before they'd finished being surprised. Two gold rings, shaped like sea creatures, curled things with too many arms. Worth real coin, the pair of them. In this room they were the cheap thing.

"Pocket change, the both of them," I said. Which was my way of saying I'd already stopped being impressed.

Belsornig's chamber I took slow. This was the one that mattered, so I did it right: hands over every surface, floor to shelf, the way you go through a room when you know it's hiding. My fingers found the scrolls before I'd decided where to look, one seam, then the next, clean, no fumbling. One to shrug off venom. One that stripped away illusion, that let you see a thing as it truly was. A third, tucked where a lazier search would've missed it. It was one of those searches where everything lines up and the room just gives itself to you. Doesn't happen often. I let it.

Then the body.

He'd been sitting on a temple's worth of things. An ivory tray. Bowls with serpent inlay, decanters, stemmed cups, all in sixes. Lanterns that burned without oil. Braziers, a censer gone green with age, blocks of incense stacked like firewood. Silk robes, green and gold, stones sewn into them. Books: a book of the water rites, and more, a shelf of lore. A trident. A little coral box, and inside it a spill of seed pearls, small as grain.

And under the ceremony, the useful part. A ring that meant the wearer would never be held, bound, or slowed. Half-armor, worked fine. A mace, a good one, the kind that hits harder than the arm swinging it. A belt of gold that was worth more by itself than the whole moathouse job had ever paid us.

I watched the hands as much as the hoard. That's the part that tells you things: who reaches, and for what.

Calmer reached first. "Oh — Calmer says, I will take —" and he had the scroll that calls down a pillar of fire before he'd finished the sentence. Took it clean off the pile, no discussion. He knew what he wanted. Fine. It was the one thing in the room I'd have expected him to want.

That left the robe. The Chief cleric's robe: heavy, ceremonial, the kind of thing you wear when you want a room to kneel. Nobody wanted to put it on. Somebody might, later: a face we didn't have yet, a recruit who'd need to look the part.

"Yeah, keep it around."

We left it out of the split and moved on. The pile was counted. I knew what everyone had, and I knew what I had.

*By peaceful meat.*

## Zinnia — An Unexpected Petitioner

We were dividing the treasure when the knock came. North door. One rap, then two more, unhurried: the sound of a man who wanted to be heard and had already decided we wouldn't kill whoever we found on the other side.

My attention went to it before I moved. Nobody knocks on a door in the middle of a temple war unless the knock is the strategy.

Sequoia crossed to it. "Yeah, I'll open."

He pulled it back, and I read the man on the threshold before he said a word. Soft, damp, cowled in green, a curled circle stitched front and back, plain mail glinting under the robe, water beaded at his hairline and his cuffs, dripping onto stone that had never been his. A cleric of the Water Temple who had walked here alone, unarmed for practical purposes, to hand himself over. The stench of standing water came in with him. He surrendered the moment the door moved. That was the whole play, and I could see it: he'd rather be a mouse in our house than a corpse in his own.

"Hello! Hello."

"Hi," I said. "Would you all—"

He didn't let me finish. He went straight past me to Calmer. "Oh, Calmer, the great and now glorious leader of the temple, the Upper Temple, I, as a lowly member of the water temple, wish to beseech you." Then, quick, the pivot: "Then before you decide, and should you decide slowly — you strike me as the people who decide too fast, let me tell you what's in my head, because it is worth more to you than my blood is worth on that nice floor."

Worth more than his blood. He'd rehearsed that. A man who leads with the value of his own life is a man who knows exactly how little of it he has left.

"We'll hear you out," I said.

Calmer looked at me. "Zinnia, why don't you come ahead? You're much better at these negotiations."

"Okay, I'll go, I'll go ahead."

I stepped forward and the room came with me: the silence I bring into a space instead of words. He felt it. Good.

"Belsornig is dead," he said. "I wept. The regulation amount." Then the offer, fast and clean, the only thing he'd actually walked in here to sell: "Now, the Treasury — under the treasure of the fourth basin doesn't flow because it drains to a sump only I can find, and the trolls he was buying. That contract isn't signed yet. Kill me, and you go looking for both. Keep me, and I hand them to you by supper."

"Okay," I said.

The trolls. Barkinar's trolls: the same contract we'd been handed to resolve, arriving now wrapped in a dead man's ledger and a live man's fear. *Gotcha.* He didn't know he was selling us something we already owed elsewhere. I didn't tell him.

"How do I know I can trust you?" I said. And before he could answer his own way out of it: "I'll send someone to go with you."

"No, no, I have never been happy, but—" He steadied. "I pledge, I do not pledge my loyalty only here, I pledge my loyalty to Calmer and the new leadership of the Earth Temple and of the Lower Temple."

Zephyr made a low sound. "Hmm."

"You're talking to them," I said.

I let the pledge sit and watched what he did with the rest of the room. He turned to me next. "Zinnia, that is your name? The masked one. The killer... the seer of all."

A stranger knew my name and my reputation before he crossed the threshold. Watched, then. Briefed, or he'd been listening at the walls of his own temple while it burned. Either way, someone had told him who to flatter and in what order. He wasn't finished. He swung to Zephyr: "Oh, be so blessed, Calmer, to have one of the Abyssal's creatures own as an ally!"

That was the whole man in one motion. He blessed Calmer, then me, then Zephyr: every corner of the room in turn, hedging every exit, in case he'd misjudged which of us held the knife. A man who doesn't know where the power sits flatters all of it. I knew, then, exactly how far to trust him, which was not far.

"I don't completely trust him," I said. "But I'll let him do it. I won't let him do it alone."

Sequoia had heard enough. "Yeah, we accept your recruit."

"I'm so gracious!" He pointed at the pile of robes we hadn't sorted. "And now, as the Chief Cleric, may I have the robes?"

"Oh, you're not the chief cleric," Sequoia said. "Oh — of the water temple."

"Yes, yes. I would not — I would not presume it would be beyond me to become the chief cleric, because after all, is not Calmer the head cleric? Is it not his turn to lead us to glory? Do we trust him yet to be?"

There it was: the whole ambition, folded inside a question about someone else's greatness. He wanted the green robes and the title, and he wanted us to hand them over as if it had been our idea.

"Do we trust him yet?" Sequoia said.

"To become head?" I said. "I'll ask him to prove himself before becoming head."

"Yeah, yeah."

"Prove myself!"

"Yes."

"We need to go through the full hiring process," Sequoia said, "and frankly, we need—"

"Hello, no, no — I am loyal. I'm extremely loyal."

"No, we know — but we want to see what you can do."

"Well, I'll see you in action," I said.

"Well, I mean — shall we start with the most obvious? The treasure."

"You're offering bribery?" Sequoia said.

"No, I'm not offering bribery! I'm offering you access to the treasure and gold that was hidden by Belsornig, and he was planning to abscond with—"

"Deal, yup, no, we're good." Sequoia glanced around. "Guys, is that good?"

"But that — I don't want you to think that is all that I could offer. I can offer so much more, as long as you were to make me—"

"Comfortable, but—" Sequoia cut him off. "Everybody, we think we're—" He turned to us. "Are we good to accept him?"

"Yeah," Zephyr said.

Good. A mouse who'd walked into our house and asked to stay. We'd keep him: the treasury, the trolls, the sump only he could find were worth the risk of one damp cleric at our backs. And we'd watch him. He'd blessed everyone in the room in case he'd guessed wrong about who to fear. A man like that doesn't stop guessing. Neither do I.

## Zephyr — Belsornig's Hidden Cache

The basin sat cracked open where the Juggernaut had gone through it: brown marble split to its black veins, the yellow triangle inlays chipped and scattered, steam still ghosting up from somewhere below. Eelrich led us to it like a man showing off a house he was proud of. The defector. The Water cleric who'd decided his prospects looked brighter on our side of the ledger, and who wasn't wrong.

"Alright," Sequoia said. "Let's go through this treasure you were talking about."

Eelrich stopped at the ruined basin and studied it, all reverence and regret. "Creature, that creature broke the basin," he said. "Nevertheless — nevertheless." He gestured at the rubble. "If one of you would be so kind as to move the rubble."

*This again.* The oldest trick in a very old book. Kind stranger asks the newcomers to put their hands somewhere he won't put his own.

"Yes, I will do that," I said, and had one foot toward it before my body finished the arithmetic my mouth had started.

"Hey, hold on," Calmer said. "It might be a trap."

Zinnia didn't bother with the theory. "Why don't you do yourself," he said to Eelrich. Flat. Not a question.

The reckoning resolved itself, cold and quick. If the cache was real, it was his to earn. If it was a trap, better his fingers than mine.

He sighed the sigh of a man who'd hoped we were stupider. "Oh, very well, very well." And then he went to work: kneeling, threading his hands through the cracked stone, feeling for the mechanisms he already knew were there. He pulled one loose. Then another.

"Oh, come on," I said. Every trap he disarmed was one he'd have watched me trigger.

Then his hands came up full. "Here is Belsornig's getaway cash," he announced, "the money he was planning to abscond with and hold on to for all time."

*Belsornig.* I'd taken the killing blow on that one myself: the Dagger of Venom out of Sequoia's hand and driven home, because it was the right tool and I'm the one who decides how a job ends. And here was the money he'd salted away against a rainy day that had, in point of fact, arrived.

"Okay," I said. "Yes."

Eelrich wasn't finished. He held the cache out and he held out something else with it. "With this gift, I hope I will have proven my loyalty enough to be considered to be — hold on to the robes. I understand it's a provisional thing, I understand it's only temporary, but it would be so glorious to hold those robes, and just for a moment, imagine myself as the head of the Water Cleric underneath the all-powerful Earth Temple Cleric, Calmer."

*There it is.* A man doesn't hand you his master's escape fund out of the goodness of a heart he plainly doesn't have. He wants the robes. He wants the title. He's paying the tribute up front so the ask lands soft.

"I feel like he's trying to…" I started, watching him work the room the way I work rooms. He wanted to be told he was already what he was asking to become. Fine. The cash was real, and the ask was cheap. "Fair enough."

"Yep," said Sequoia. "Looking pretty good."

"So — you good? Zinnia, what do you think?"

"Allowed," he said.

"Might be tricking us," I said, "but it's okay." A man angling for a promotion is a man you understand completely.

"It's okay," Calmer said. "I mean, he's, after all, only one cleric — how much harm…" He let it trail, then decided against trusting it all the way. "We're gonna keep an eye on him."

The chest opened on gems, jewelry, and whatnot: a good weight of it, the kind that makes a corridor feel warmer than it is.

"A lot of gold," I said.

"You should have a lot of gold," Calmer said, twice, like a blessing. "You should have a lot of gold."

Sequoia was already tallying against his own pile. "I'm at almost four thousand."

"Remember five to twelve," Zinnia said.

We'd need Nulb to turn any of it into something spendable. "Yep, yep, we have to," I said.

Then the part that mattered. Dren would want his cut, and Dren had earned it. "Absolutely. Okay." A tenth off the top, that felt right. "Oh, no — ten percent."

"Yeah, ten percent," said Sequoia.

"Yep." A tenth of the haul, carved off before anyone touched their share.

Then Sequoia, mild as anything: "Personally, I think we should even it out — some people have more gold than others."

And there it was: four of us standing over a dead man's escape fund, negotiating the fairness of our shares like clerks balancing a temple's books. Something had taken root in us, and it hadn't asked permission.

I'd spent my whole life levying interest on a world that charged me for existing. So it surprised me a little to hear myself do the opposite. "No, since I'm the leader, I'll pay." The bigger share of Dren's tribute, off my own stack. "The bigger share."

"Go ahead," Zinnia said.

Calmer went quiet. I could see him turning it over, that over-eager conscience of his hunting for the objection.

"No, I never—" I started.

"Anything you want to say?" Sequoia prompted him.

Calmer landed on the far side of whatever he was wrestling. "I think that's fantastic. I think that's an excellent idea."

"He could pay more, too," Zinnia added. "The proof is, it's loose."

"Zinnia," Calmer said, looking at him with something like admiration crossing his face, "that is a masterful stroke."

## Calmer — The Chamber of the Pool

Zephyr had finished dividing the treasure, he always finished dividing the treasure, and then he turned to the door on the south wall, the one Brother Eelrich had been standing in front of the whole time like a man guarding a relative's grave.

I asked what was behind it. That is the sort of thing the Prophet of Stone asks, and I have learned the cadence of it.

"Behind the door to the south," Eelrich said, and his voice did a small reverent thing. "That is where the high priest retained all of its powers. Inside is a creature of unparalleled power."

I nodded like a man being told where the good wine was kept.

"A creature of unparalleled power," he said again, warming to it. "One that we worshipped, and one that we served, and one that served us."

*Worshipped.* The word sat wrong in my chest. I have stood in a lot of rooms in this place and swallowed a lot of words. I have watched Romag toast me as a convert and clinked my cup against his. But *worshipped and served and served us* is a particular kind of sentence, and something in me that has been quiet for weeks stirred at it and did not go back to sleep.

"Can we see it?" Sequoia said.

"You wish to see it?" Eelrich asked.

"Yeah."

"Sure," said Zinnia, easy, the way he says everything, and Eelrich went, "well," and opened the door.

The room past it was carved from great blocks of polished serpentine, green-black and wet-looking though it was dry, and the ceiling above was a dome of crystals cut to throw everything back at you crooked: my own plate armor smeared across a hundred facets, a hundred small broken Calmers. All of that reflected light bent down toward the center, toward the pool.

The pool gave off a blue-green glow. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that it also took light in: I watched the pale flames from the cressets lean toward it and vanish somewhere under the surface, swallowed, held. Eight bronze cressets, four along the east wall and four along the west, all burning a sick green fire that shed light and no heat at all. I have blessed enough fires to know when one is a lie.

The tapestries I did not look at long. The mosaic on the far wall I could not stop looking at. Ten arms of dark purple tile, reddish along the edges, two green orbs for eyes, and in each of the ten tentacles a human figure held up like an offering. I did not have to guess what I was seeing. I have read enough scripture, real scripture, to know a sacrificial tableau when a temple puts one on its wall to be proud of. This was not a warning. It was a menu.

And this was the thing they worshipped. This was what St. Cuthbert—

I closed my mouth on the name. I felt it come up my throat the way it had in the Air Temple corridor, the way it does when the wrongness in front of me is total and old and pleased with itself, and I held it behind my teeth and let it go back down. Not here. The mercenaries were not in the room, but Eelrich was, and a man's cover does not survive his god's name spoken over an altar to a kraken.

The pool was long as a great hall, and as broad, set in a basin of small blue lapis tiles, and the water was so clear it lied about its depth. The edges came up shallow. Then the floor fell away toward the center, down and down to where the water went dark, and at the very bottom, chained where the light finally died, there was a figure. Humanoid. Bound. Alive.

Then it spoke inside my head.

Not to me alone: I saw Zinnia's chin come up, saw Sequoia go still. A Triton, the voice said, pleading, fast, frightened in the way that only true things are frightened. *Release me. When the Kraken shown on the wall materializes, it will come for you, and it will come at any moment. As many of you as can, jump into the pool. Help me break these chains. Soon it will be too late.*

I stood there in my looted plate, the Prophet of Stone, the man who runs this temple now whether I meant to or not, and I looked at a good creature chained at the bottom of a shrine to a monster, begging.

Something in me wanted to be in the water already. The same thing that has always wanted to charge the wrong thing alone.

And something colder and more careful stood behind it and said this is a shrine, and shrines set traps in the shape of exactly what you cannot walk away from.

The Kraken had not come yet. The Triton was still chained. And I had not yet decided which of the two men I have become was going to move first.
