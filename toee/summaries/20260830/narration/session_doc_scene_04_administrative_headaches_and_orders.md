Captain Dren Halveth arrived clutching a little scroll in one fist. His uniform sat crooked, his eyes were red, and the scroll had been worried soft around the edges. He looked at me, glanced across the others, then returned to me as if confirming where the burden belonged.

“Hey, boss!”

“Yes?” Calmer said.

Dren barely turned toward him. “Yeah, Zephyr, I need to tell you something.”

There are moments when authority feels like a fine coat: fitted, expensive, and visible from across the room. This was not one of them. Brown marble veined with black stretched beneath our boots, and somewhere beyond the walls something heavy struck stone. Water dripped from a ceiling that had not been dripping before. The Temple was losing a war, winning a war, and eating itself, depending on which corridor one inspected.

“I've been doing the supply check. The Minotaur has food for 3 days. The men have pay for 4. The dead have started leaving without permission.”

He consulted the scroll after each disaster, as if hoping the next line might be wine.

“I can solve any one of these. I cannot solve all three while pretending this is a normal command.”

Fair.

The dead deserting was the most honest assessment of our administration so far. We had taken power by assassination, manipulated the surviving factions into war, and inherited a Minotaur with a meal plan. Now a captain stood before me waiting for priorities because apparently ruling an evil temple involved inventory control.

*This again.*

“How do we usually get food and pay?”

“Well, so from what I gathered, there's a supply chain from Nulb. So somebody's gotta go to Nulb to go deal with that.”

“Ugh.”

Of course it was Nulb. Every structure in the world, however grand, eventually rested on someone hauling sacks from a town I did not want to visit. The Abyss had rivers of poison and screaming walls, but at least nobody pretended the screaming walls would remain operational without procurement.

“Guys, we're going on a trip to Nulb.”

“Yeah, that's what I said earlier,” Sequoia said.

He had. This did not improve Nulb.

Dren lifted the scroll. “Alright, so there's a bunch of things you guys have. What do you want them to work on? You want to work on the Minotaur problem? Do you want to work him on the dead?”

The Chest of Rao also remained unresolved, and Barkinar was still waiting for our decision. Authority had arrived all at once: soldiers, stores, sacred property, wandering corpses. None of it came with an instruction manual. Even the chest seemed less like treasure now and more like correspondence I had neglected to answer.

“We should probably fix the supply chain,” Sequoia said.

Dren waited. The supply chain was ours. He needed his own assignment.

“Undead?” Sequoia offered.

Dren turned to all of us. “What do you want me to do?”

“No, let's just do the Minotaur, fine.”

A pause.

“Find bodies.”

Dren looked down at his scroll. “I can do more than one thing, but what would you like me to do?”

Then his eyes settled on me again.

“Boss. Zephyr, what do you want me to do?”

The title landed more heavily the second time. Men were hungry. Pay was running out. The dead had discovered freedom of movement. Somewhere below us, the war we had designed continued to grind flesh against stone, and none of the survivors cared whether our authority was legitimate. They cared whether orders arrived.

So I gave them.

“Protection of the armor to the Water Temple.”

Let them guard what could equip our soldiers. It gave them responsibility, exposed them if they stole, and kept them occupied around a resource everyone wanted.

“Alright. Firewood to the Air Temple.”

Let them guard what burned. The arrangement possessed symmetry, if not wisdom.

“Sounds good,” Dren said.

He marked the scroll. Ink made it government.

“And what do you want me to do about the dead that have started leaving?”

“Why?” Sequoia asked.

“Incinerate it,” I said.

Sequoia pressed for the cause before the order became policy. “Figure out why, and then—”

“Oh, that's easy, man! There's some necromancer raising them from the dead.”

“Then kill it! Then kill him.”

Dren did not mark that down yet. “Do you want me to kill him — or, Zephyr, do you want me to deal with the body supply problem? After all, the bodies are starting to stink up the place.”

The smell had already reached us beneath the damp stone and old smoke. Sweet, heavy, impossible to negotiate with.

“Yeah, stink up the place.”

The tiefling considers the command structure. The tiefling considers the available labor. The tiefling breathes through his mouth.

“Burn all the bodies.”

“Alright, burn all the bodies. Can I use the Fire Temple salamander to help with the burning?”

“Yeah. Wait — use the bodies to feed the un— the Minotaur.”

I looked at Sequoia. He had found the efficient solution halfway through saying the wrong one. Waste disposal became food supply. Two failing systems joined hands and briefly resembled competence.

“So, usable bodies, give to Minotaur. But just to be clear, boss — you're going to Nulb to sort out our food supply problems, right?”

“Yeah,” Sequoia said.

“Alright.”

Dren finally lowered the scroll. His shoulders remained crooked, but the panic had somewhere to go now.

“Yeah, that sounds good. I think we've got a handle on it. So you're gonna leave now, or are you gonna leave tomorrow morning?” He looked around.

“Tomorrow morning.”

Morning was theoretical inside the Temple. Bronze crescent sconces burned until someone replaced them. Battles began without sunrise. Corpses walked without permission. Still, tomorrow morning sounded like an institution, and we were apparently building those now.

“Guys, we'll have some work ahead of us.”
