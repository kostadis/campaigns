## Scene 1: Patrons of the Common Chord

### Vukradin

Aldus Hern still has not given us an exact time for dinner with Lord Neverember. There is only so much preparation a person can do for an appointment without an hour attached, and we have another appointment, beneath the city, with rather less flexibility about the conditions of attendance. The lord can wait.

“Is it Neverember or Neverwinter? I'm kidding.”

Soma takes up the matter of his name. “He's never flaming. He keeps it on the DL.”

“Yeah, I think Soma has it exactly right.”

The Common Chord gives us somewhere to wait before our late-night business. It used to give me somewhere to play and then throw me out unpaid. Now it is the hardest ticket in Neverwinter. I look around the room while we discuss the lord’s household, trying to reconcile the place I remember with the place we are sitting in.

“Okay, and Lord Neverember… Lord Neverflaming has a new drow advisor.”

“Great, isn't that Valphine?” Soma asks.

“No.”

“Oh, right.”

“It's a rumor, and I suspect Valphine will know this advisor at dinner, but we will see.”

Valphine has lost the thread, so I bring her back to the rumored advisor.

“Oh, right, right, right. Which we assume to be one of my siblings, in fact.”

“That is my guess too, yes.”

“Everyone's related to everyone,” Soma says.

It remains a guess. A drow advisor is not necessarily a Sotorra, and a rumor is not an introduction. Dinner may settle it. Meanwhile, Valphine regards House Margaster as competition, which is a considerably more comfortable relationship with House Margaster than I have managed.

“Have we secured the capital for Brewbarry's bathrobe business, or is that still in progress?”

“I believe it's in progress,” Soma says.

We straighten that out: the money is secured. Brewbarry has his capital. It is the expense of bringing things into the city that remains.

“But, oh, but the cost of import is very high.”

“Right, because their shipping lanes are still…” Soma corrects herself. “Shipping portals, whatever, are still hosed.”

And House Margaster controls the available logistics. They deliver on time and on schedule, which I acknowledge, because an objection ought to survive an accurate description of the people one objects to. But Brewbarry is trying to make bathrobes, he has secured the money to make bathrobes, and still his ability to bring softness to people depends upon what House Margaster charges to move the materials. Punctuality does not make that expense disappear.

“Alright, let's head out to Lim's Food Deliveries and get there by 3 AM,” I say. “And then we can cast whatever spells we need in order to be ready.”

There is time before we need to go. Across that time, from within easy earshot, comes a complaint about me.

A local is explaining that the Chord has become unaffordable, and that Vukradin is to blame. He does not realize Vukradin is standing nearby.

For a moment I simply look at him. I remember wanting people to come here. I remember playing to a room that could have accommodated a great many more listeners, and I cannot see how the proper resolution of that difficulty is a room that listeners cannot afford to enter. Somewhere between those two rooms, my name has become an explanation for the price.

But he wants affordable music. I want to provide affordable music. We are already much closer to agreement than he knows.

I approach and tell him about the independent music studio I am planning, and the charitable fund that will help open it.

“That will speed the opening of a low-cost, nonprofit way to hear Lathander's Death on a regular basis.”

I want regular performances. I want a place where people can learn, where the money goes into the music and the room, where someone who likes what we do can come back without having to make a special financial occasion of it. The studio in Phandalin is still ahead of me, but here is a person describing precisely why it needs to exist.

“And spread the word.”

While we talk, conversation at the bar supplies information about the night’s other business. Water from the river affects the sewers. There is a tide down there.

“There's a tide in the sewers? A river has a tide?”

Neverwinter is beside the sea. I know this, of course, but knowing where the sea is and remembering that it can follow you underground are different stages of preparation. I listen more closely. We are going to be beneath the streets, and the difference between a convenient entrance and an increasingly wet predicament is worth establishing before we enter.

“How many moons does this planet have?”

“That's an easy question to answer,” Valphine says.

“Just the one.”

“It just rotates—it revolves really fast,” Soma offers.

“It has one moon named Selûne,” Valphine says.

“Oh, Selûne. Of course. Yeah.”

The Tears of Selûne complicate the discussion briefly. Asteroids, rather than another moon. I work through the intervals while the conversation around us continues, until the useful facts emerge: two high tides and two low tides each day.

“There you go. Not one and one: two high and two low.”

“That's murder on trade,” Soma says.

“It should take effect underground, too, right?” Valphine asks.

Underground is the part that concerns me. I revise my arithmetic and settle it.

“High to low is 6 hours.”

The low tide coincides with our intended arrival.

“We want to be in the sewer for… at low tide.”

“3 AM,” Soma says.

“We have about 3 hours, maybe 4, before things start to get really uncomfortable.”

“I mean, I can hang out underwater indefinitely.”

That is excellent for Soma. For the rest of us, three or four useful hours is a better basis for the plan. Done.

The local I approached is on the drunk side, and he does not have much money. Nevertheless, he hands me ten gold pieces.

“Wow, that's pretty generous, actually.”

“That's a hell of a donation,” Soma says.

The coins have weight in my hand. A complaint about the price of music has become money freely given to make music affordable. I keep it separate from our ordinary funds; this money has a purpose, and the person giving it ought to know that I understand the purpose.

“Thank you very much for contributing. That's awesome.”

He pauses. “Do I get on… Is there some kind of signage for people who contributed?”

“Absolutely. Yes.”

Recognition is entirely reasonable. The studio will exist because people helped it exist. Their names belong in the program.

“There are different tiers of giving levels, actually. 10 gold pieces puts you at the,”

“Premier or the Plus, which one?” Soma asks.

“At the plus level, yeah, you're not quite at Premier yet.”

He is happy with that. Plus is a substantial contribution, and ten gold deserves to be treated as substantial regardless of whether somebody else can give more. I arrange for his name to be recorded for the program notes. We establish that the credit lasts a year.

“A year. If you want to be in next year's, you'll have to donate again, but one year is a long time.”

“Yeah, we call this Patreon,” Valphine says.

“It's my Patreon, exactly.”

“This is where it comes from, patronage,” Soma says.

Yes. People supporting the work because they want the work to happen. I have spent so much time explaining why I will not take one purse or another that it is a pleasure to stand here holding money I can accept, from somebody who wants the same room I want.

“Before we go, should I try to convince other people to contribute to the Studio Fund? How much time do we have?”

Enough. We still have time before three.

“Alright, let's do… let's hit up a few more people, let's see how it goes here.”

I approach five more patrons. The first recognizes me, the actual Vukradin, which saves a certain amount of introduction. He has already completed his charitable giving for the year.

“Okay, that's fine. I'm not here to pressure anybody.”

His donation is not an entrance fee to being a fan. If his giving is finished, it is finished, and we can still have a perfectly good conversation about music.

“The benefits are all on you. If you can be at the Plus or Premier level, I'm cool. I just like fans.”

“Biggest fan, biggest fan,” he says.

“Oh, Bigger Than Brewbarry?”

There is a practical difficulty with that claim. Brewbarry is here, and the comparison is available to anyone who wishes to make it. I am content to leave the man his enthusiasm and move on without his money.

Wick is enthusiastic about patronage of the arts. He has no idea who Lathander’s Death is, and he does not believe I am Vukradin. He does, however, think opening a music school is an excellent idea.

“School?” Soma asks.

“School, studio—they're all the same, right? If you have a studio, it's for training people. That's just how it goes.”

I cannot imagine building a place for music and then objecting when someone wishes to learn music in it. Wick’s understanding of the project gives him a reason to help. My identity can remain unsettled for him; the usefulness of the studio does not depend on his recognizing my face.

Sabbatha contributes too, although her attention travels to Brewbarry. She looks him over and admires his commitment to the Brewbarry look. The dedication. The appearance.

“Yes, he is quite bulging, isn't he?”

“Rippling,” Soma says.

“Exactly.”

Brewbarry’s appearance evidently has an audience of its own. I am pleased she appreciates him, even if she is appreciating his dedication to looking like himself. He has been a great supporter of my music. There is room for some appreciation to come back in his direction.

Ondrelle Kass also joins the supporters. I make sure the names are kept for the future program: Wick, Sabbatha, Ondrelle Kass. There is one more conversation, with Old Hesp, who recognizes me before I recognize him.

“I remember when you used to play to an empty room.”

“Yeah?”

I look at him again. This is a different recognition from the people who know the name or doubt the face. He remembers the room.

“Did you come? Did you come? Did I give you your money back at the end of.”

I did. He remembers the guarantee: if he did not rate the performance ten out of ten, I would not keep his money.

“I know, that's how honest I am. Well, what do you think now?”

He pauses.

“Now this is a friggin' expensive place to be. Everybody's gotta look cool.”

I asked what he thought now, and he answers about the place. That is fair. The place is part of hearing the music. A person has to get through the door, has to sit somewhere, has to feel that he can be there, and it is no use telling him the performance is wonderful if the expense has already decided whether he gets to hear it.

“Well, come to the new studio, when it's up and running.”

He misses the old feel, the grit of it. He struggles a little to describe what is gone, but I remember enough of this room to follow him.

“We're gonna get it back. We're gonna get it back.”

“This is too commercial.”

“Yeah, yeah, I agree.”

Old Hesp credits Petra with turning the Common Chord into a money-making operation. He respects what she has accomplished even while he complains about what it costs him. Then his attention goes to Brewbarry’s table.

I follow it.

Leather. Shine. A plaque.

He remembers when the table had none of that. So do I. The finish gives the table a presence it never needed when it was simply where Brewbarry sat. Now somebody can come here to see Brewbarry’s table, and the room around it can charge prices that send someone who actually came to our performances looking elsewhere.

Scandalous.

Old Hesp points out the fancy food as well. That was not here before. I look from the food to the table, and there is the expensive venue in material form, quite separate from the empty room I remember and still using the people who played in that room to tell its story.

I do want people to come. I do want the work to succeed. I want enough money to build the studio, and I have no intention of pretending that walls and instruments will appear because my intentions are excellent. But Old Hesp came when there was scarcely anyone here, and now he stands in front of me explaining that the successful version has become difficult to afford. I can offer him somewhere else. I can make that invitation part of what the studio is for.

His name goes into the program credits too.

The evening brings twenty gold pieces into the studio fund. Petra’s separate five hundred for my likeness remains pending until the performance two days from now. I keep the amounts distinct. Twenty gold received, freely contributed, for the room I have promised these people.

Then it is time to attend to the room beneath the city, which has no leather finish to consider and a tide we cannot negotiate with.

“Alright, let's go, do some… spelunking.”

## Scene 2: The Ninth Crate Disappears

### Valphine Sotorra

The steps descend toward the sewer grille in shallow, aqueduct-like courses, wet stone offering very little purchase and the darkness below offering rather more concealment than I would prefer to leave entirely to our quarry. Two carriers bring down the crates. From where we watch, the work is visible; its authority is less so. Eight of the boxes lack the Commission Harbor mark, which tells us something about how they entered Neverwinter, although nothing yet about whose protection made the omission profitable.

Vukradin has already offered himself for whatever requires silence.

“I have a very high stealth, if we need me to…”

“I mean, you could tag along with a familiar…” Soma suggests.

His Boots of Elvenkind make him the sensible choice. The rest of us possess other virtues, few of which recommend us for following someone down wet steps without announcing our interest. Soma casts *Find Familiar*, and a rat supplies a smaller alternative, one through whose eyes and ears she can perceive so long as it remains within a hundred feet. A useful reach for watching a delivery. A rather limited one for discovering where the delivery goes.

The carriers lower their burdens beside the grille.

“All 9 of them?” Soma asks.

Nine. I count them as they are deposited, watching the disposition of the boxes as closely as the men who carry them. Eight belong together. The ninth requires separate attention.

“So we want one person to track down the gnome, right? Crate 9,” Vukradin says. “We also want to track the people who dropped them. So we want to do two things.”

Soma’s interest is less evenly distributed.

“Well, I'm tracking the crate.”

“Which crate?”

“The ninth crate, the one that doesn't go into Lim, because I'm more concerned about my friend's shady business.”

Friendship does complicate an investigation, although in this case it has the useful effect of keeping Soma attentive to the portion of the business Lim does not receive. Eight crates constitute an arrangement. The ninth may explain who else benefits from it.

“Okay, so you want me to follow them back into the sewers?” Vukradin asks. “That seems pretty dangerous. One party member in the sewers.”

“I see. Alright, we can send the rat that way, then. You can follow the crate.”

For the moment, there is no crate to follow. There are boxes being left for collection and two men whose work is almost finished. One stacks the eight together. The other puts the ninth on a ledge, then pauses and looks down into the dark.

I watch that pause.

He appears to be checking for something below, and the distinction matters: the placement of the box tells him where his responsibility ends, but his glance suggests an interest in what happens after it leaves his hands. Then he walks away.

“The rat's gonna go whichever way leads to the sewer,” Soma says.

Vukradin makes his intended position clear. He will remain here, wait for whoever collects the ninth crate, and follow the collector.

“So everyone other than Vukradin is gonna trail these guys, and Vukradin's gonna trail the crate,” I say. “Is that right?”

They agree. An adequate division, provided the men remain together.

They do not.

The carriers depart in different directions, conspicuously declining to acknowledge each other. Their work has just placed them side by side with nine crates; now they act as though acquaintance would be an unreasonable accusation.

“That nonchalant whistling,” Soma says.

I appreciate the diagnosis. Their studied independence attracts more attention than a farewell would have done, although whether it reflects instructions, habit, or their own estimation of subtlety remains uncertain.

“So if they split up, that's gonna make them hard to follow,” Vukradin says. “You're gonna have to choose one or split again.”

I return to the glance into the sewer. At first I cannot confidently distinguish seniority from a difference in assigned labor. A man may know where a box goes without having any authority over the man who stacks the others. Vukradin’s earlier encouragement steadies my concentration, and I separate the question we need answered from the more ornamental question of rank.

The man at the ledge is the better choice. Whatever his standing, he has shown an interest in the next participant. The other has shown us how to stack boxes.

“I'd follow the guy who paid attention to the ledge,” Soma says. “He knows something. He knows something's coming out of that thing.”

That is also my reading, with the small reservation that expectation is not yet knowledge. We have enough to choose our man.

“I could handle this myself, guys, you don't trust me?”

Before the matter can acquire the weight of a personal grievance, something emerges from below.

It is not a gnome.

I am surprised. I had allowed the anticipated collector to remain a person in my calculations; what comes up to the ledge is a small construct, cat-sized, brass and leather arranged into a quick, purposeful body. Its movements have the economy of instructions being fulfilled. It takes the ninth crate and returns beneath the grille.

For a moment, brass, leather, and the separate ledge explain one another. The carrier was looking toward the point from which this thing would come. They do not explain who sent it.

Vukradin moves to pursue.

“This is my concern,” Soma says. “Vukradin ends up going into the sewers now, and…”

“I'll be fine.”

“One person in the sewers? Alright.”

The same objection sounded quite reasonable when he raised it himself. The arrival of something worth following has apparently revised his assessment.

“Tell me the odds,” Soma says.

“Only a 1 in 400 chance of disaster. It'll be fine.”

I would prefer to know how many forms of disaster his calculation includes. Silent boots address the noise of his passage; they do not make the passage known to us. Still, the construct is leaving, and the man who expected it is leaving by another route. We cannot preserve both opportunities by remaining on the steps.

Vukradin goes below. Soma, Brewbarry, and I take the carrier.

Soma and Vukradin have the paired Sending Stones, so the division does not entirely sever our means of communication. It does, however, leave stone and distance between the bard and the people who could intervene on his behalf. I keep my attention on the man ahead. There is little utility in accepting a separation and then allowing concern over one pursuit to spoil the other.

Our carrier stops at a cook shop.

At the fourth hour, the choice is a narrow one; few places are open, and this one offers him breakfast. He sits alone and eats with apparent satisfaction. The distinction between the man who checked a dark sewer for a collector and the man enjoying his meal is chiefly a change of occupation. I watch for what the meal may interrupt or precede.

Soma has a more immediate concern.

“He's not eating turtle soup or anything at this hour, is he?”

We remain outside his breakfast. The shop is not the Board Laid Bare, and whatever connection brought his crates to Lim has not brought him to her table. Here he is simply eating alone.

We could approach him. We could turn his comfortable pause into a very different interval, and perhaps obtain an answer before the food cooled. But we promised Lim not to spook her connections, and a frightened intermediary could close several routes while opening only his mouth.

Soma reaches the same practical conclusion.

“I'm not trying to put her out of business. I want to see where he goes next. Stopping for a snack doesn't tell us anything, and spooking him doesn't seem kosher.”

So we wait.

Brewbarry remains with us, the carrier has his breakfast, and the ninth crate is beyond my sight beneath the city. Our chosen man may yet lead us somewhere useful. For now, I give him the time to finish eating.

## Scene 3: Lost Among Unexpected Fans

### Vukradin

I remember the crate. I remember that I am following it, and that following it matters, and then the passage I mean to take seems scarcely worth the trouble. There is no argument behind this. Nothing has happened to make the pursuit less important. I simply look that way and cannot summon any interest in going there.

So I go somewhere else.

The sewer carries on around me, damp stone and branching passages, each turn offering somewhere to put my feet without offering much help in deciding where that should be. By the time I want to ask someone about it, my friends are no longer beside me.

There is, however, a man standing in front of several other men.

He looks at me oddly.

“How the hell did you get over here?”

“I don't know.”

This is a difficult beginning to a conversation because it is both true and insufficient. I look past him at the people gathered behind him, then back at the tunnel.

“Where am I?”

He looks at me. Apparently my presence requires an explanation before my location can be supplied.

“Because I'm not over there.”

That accounts for the geography as well as I can presently manage. I try to understand what he wants from me, but his expression gives me very little. He seems confused rather than angry. Beyond that, I cannot tell, and standing silently in front of one another will not improve either of our situations.

“Yeah, there you go. I'm lost. I don't know what… I don't know how I got here, I just…”

I remember what I was doing. It is the route between doing that and standing here that I cannot explain usefully, and I am certainly not going to open with a crate pursuit when the immediate problem is that I need directions.

“Yeah. Wrong turn. Should have taken that left at Albuquerque.”

He appears to accept that I am lost. He looks at me and licks his finger.

“Well, this is my territory.”

“This is your territory.”

“Yes, this is my territory. Wow.”

I look at the tunnel again. Territory. All right. We have moved from a geographical question to a question of management.

“Did you want me to go?”

“I'd want to know why you're in my territory.”

“Oh, I'm sorry that I got lost. I'm happy to leave. Could you point me in the direction of Lim's? Cafe, that's… that's where I want to be headed.”

“Lim's Cafe?”

“We're friends.”

This does not produce the reassuring effect I expect. His attention sharpens. He looks at me more suspiciously now, which is an unfortunate response to the mention of a friend, particularly when that friend has a cafe and the entire purpose of naming the cafe is to establish somewhere else I can be.

“That's interesting. Why are you…”

He stops. His scrutiny changes again.

“Are you that singer?”

“Exactly. And Lim's a fan.”

There. Something we can work with.

“What are you doing down here, man?”

“I told you, I got lost!”

“Vukradin… you're Vukradin, right?”

One of the men beside him is smiling now, openly delighted, and the sight does more to make this stretch of sewer comprehensible than anything the tunnel manager has said.

“This is… is this really Vukradin?”

“Do you guys want autographs?”

They would love some autographs. Of course I can do that. A person can spend a great deal of time wondering whether the music reaches anyone beyond the room where he plays it, and then turn the wrong way underground and find someone smiling because he knows your name. I cannot begrudge him a signature merely because neither of us expected the occasion.

“Sure, we can do some autographs, but then I gotta be going.”

“Sure… Maybe you can…”

“Point me in the right direction.”

“You know what, we're gonna do one better, we're gonna take you there.”

“Alright, sounds good.”

“We don't want you to get lost again, Mr. Vukradin.”

This is thoughtful. We have already established that directions alone may not be my strongest protection today.

“Lim will be thrilled.”

The man pauses before we can get on with leaving.

“It's very dangerous down here.”

“Is it?”

“Yeah, it's, it's, it's…”

“Yeah, it's dangerous in your territory? I would think you'd keep it very safe. You seem like a safe individual. I'm sure you keep the place shipshape.”

He has offered to escort me personally. That is a reasonable beginning for someone responsible for a place, and if there is a problem beyond his ability to manage, then we should discuss the problem. He need not struggle through a general warning when a specific one would help us both.

“Mr. Vukradin, I agree with you. But I have to tell you why I was out here. I was concerned when I saw you, and I'm very glad I recognized you. One of my guys tells me you have a deal with a friend of mine.”

“At the Common Chord?”

“No, no, at the Toe mine.”

“Mountain's Toe Mine. Oh, yeah…”

“Zeleen…”

“Oh, I didn't know you knew him.”

Rsolk knows Zeleen Varnaster. This unexpected connection reaches all the way from these tunnels to our business at Mountain’s Toe Gold Mine, and I find myself looking at him with fresh interest too. We have rather more to talk about than which turn I missed.

“Yeah, we spent some time in Phandalin, yeah, yeah.”

“Yeah, he said that he had a business relationship with you, I said that he was full of shit.”

“No, no, that's true, that's true. Yeah, they haven't produced very much yet, but they say they're working hard.”

Rsolk laughs.

“That's how I feel about it, too.”

He laughs again. I am glad the business relationship can at least produce this. I would appreciate it producing more money as well. The studio needs actual funds, and assurances of hard work, however encouraging, cannot become a room in Phandalin where we can make music.

Still laughing, he says, “Look, I manage the tunnels here.”

“Oh, you're the tunnel manager.”

“Yeah, a tunnel manager.”

“So this is your territory, and you have all the tunnels.”

“Not all the tunnels, but the tunnels in this area.”

A useful distinction. I can now understand why an unfamiliar person appearing here attracts his attention without granting him responsibility for everything beneath Neverwinter.

“Two months ago, things started coming up the floodline.”

“Floodline.”

He describes a tunnel that floods regularly. I try to follow the explanation, holding onto the location and the fact that the water comes back, though I have no useful expertise to contribute about what a tunnel ought to do when it floods.

“I don't know anything about tunnels, man, I'm a musician.”

“I've been having this problem with the tunnels. I've lost men, so we don't go there anymore.”

The laughter is finished. I look down the passage as he speaks. Somewhere in this network is a stretch of tunnel his people have abandoned because men went there and did not come back. His warning has a reason now, and his concern at finding me wandering makes considerably more sense.

I cannot repair a floodline. But Brewbarry, Soma, Valphine and I have dealt with a substantial number of things that make ordinary work impossible, and if that is what is happening here, then there may be a way to help these people and earn money honestly in the same undertaking. No stolen chest. No argument over who a dead person’s possessions belong to. Work that someone needs done.

“Do you need help with that? My friends and I have some abilities, and we're looking for ways to make money. Maybe we can help you.”

Rsolk needs very little encouragement. His readiness to tell me more is answer enough that the offer interests him.

“One of the guys who escaped said there was some kind of creature they'd never seen before. It looked like…”

“Creature stuff.”

This is much closer to our area of competence.

“It looked like a spider, but it had all the wrong colors.”

Then there are the webs. The survivor described webbing visibly wrong, something off about it beyond merely belonging to a dangerous spider. I remember the strange webs we encountered before. That recollection comes readily enough, unlike any useful explanation for my arrival in this tunnel.

I have not seen what is down by Rsolk’s floodline. A survivor’s description does not make it the same creature. But I know enough to take the wrongness seriously.

“Yeah, we have experience with that.”

There is a job here. We can return with my friends, find the affected passage, and see what can be done about the creatures. Rsolk can tell me how to find him again. We have reached the practical part of the conversation, where I expect to discuss what he can offer for clearing the tunnel.

“Mr. Vukradin, I know this is too much to ask, but could you get me some tickets to the Common Chord?”

“I can ask.”

I am pleased he wants to hear music. I am less certain that the Common Chord will cooperate, and it would be unfair to let him mistake my willingness for control over the venue.

“we have kind of an arm's length relationship with the Common Chord, but I will ask for you.”

“That's all I could ask for.”

That much I can promise. The payment for the spider work remains to be settled, but the need is real, he wants our help, and I have what I need to bring us back here. A possible contribution to the studio, earned by making this place safer. Worth pursuing.

“Alright, let's do some autographs, and then we gotta be on our way.”

I sign for them. There is room for this before we leave, room to give the smiling man what I offered and let an encounter that began with a demand become something he can be happy about.

Then Rsolk and his associates escort me through the tunnels toward familiar ground. I follow their turns, carrying the directions for finding them again and the account of the spiders by the floodline. The way is considerably easier with someone who knows where he is going.
