### Soma

The delivery man eats alone. Valphine, Brewbarry, and I watch him finish an early breakfast at a cook shop. Four in the morning, and he can afford to have somebody else cook for him.

“And dinner at 4 a.m.,” I say.

Breakfast, apparently. He has been working all night. I doubt his stomach cares what we call it.

Valphine’s assessment is that he really is a delivery man. No disguised leader. No mastermind carrying crates to keep his hands busy. So we follow the carrier.

He takes us into the Bluelake District, to a building divided into single rooms. Many tenants under one roof. Little room wasted on any of them.

“High efficiency flat,” I say.

He goes to his room and sleeps. My familiar goes in after him. A rat needs considerably less space than I do.

I look through what the rat can find. Some sign of who pays him would help. A receipt. An ordinary pay stub. A mark on his belongings. At first there is nothing. No factional insignia, no identifying marks. Just a sleeping man and possessions that suggest he is doing better than an ordinary carrier should.

The meal alone might mean nothing. A person can spend too much on breakfast. Taken with his belongings, it begins to look less like one indulgence.

“Yeah, he’s a little overcompensated for his job.”

Still no badge. No convenient declaration of criminal allegiance for the rat to inspect. He appears to have found good work.

“Oh shit, I want that gig.”

Valphine considers the building. “So, is he in a house, or is he in an apartment?”

There are enough rooms here to make the arrangement clear.

“Okay, so he has a landlord,” she says. “Who we could potentially ask questions about him of.”

That is more promising than waiting for the sleeper to turn over and reveal a name stitched into his pillow.

“But it’s middle of the night, huh?”

We wait until morning. By seven, the man who runs the place is available, and our delivery man is still asleep. We can ask about the building without knocking on his door.

“Yeah, no. We can’t spook him indirectly, so we’ll just talk to the landlord.”

I approach with Valphine and Brewbarry beside me. Meril’s staff rests in my hand.

“Alright. We just had some questions. We wanted to know…” I settle on the practical one. “What’s it cost to live here? I think it’s the first one we need to know.”

The landlord looks at me.

“I don’t see many turtles around here.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

He keeps looking at my shell. There is a whole building full of rented rooms behind him. I have asked about one of them. Somehow this requires an examination of my back.

“I thought turtles carried their house on their shoulders, so they didn’t need a place to stay.”

“I see what you think of us. I can’t live in this thing. It merely protects me.”

“Oh!”

A discovery before breakfast. Good for him.

“I actually have a nice cabin out in the woods, out by, oh god, where did we start? Neverwinter.” I correct the useful part. “Well, outside of it. I was not in Neverwinter proper.”

“Oh, well, I learned a new thing. I didn’t know turtles didn’t live in their shells.”

I wait.

“I guess you’re not like… I guess you’re not like turtles.”

“No, no, we have… Very… that’s kind of slanderous to us.”

His attention moves past me at last. He notices Valphine. Then Brewbarry. His interest in my ancestry becomes less comfortable.

“I’m just asking a question.”

“Oh, oh, I’m just asking questions, I see.” I look at him. “Just asking questions guy has entered the chat.”

“Just asking questions.”

“Look.” I still need the rent. “I’ll pretend I didn’t hear some of that. I just… I want to know what it costs to live here, and, you know, what kind of people normally stay in a place like this.”

He has mistaken us for prospective tenants. I give him something to do with that.

“You could say I’m looking for a place for my, you know, my shell sprouts in the future.”

“Look, first of all, we don’t care. We’re an equal-opportunity housing establishment.”

“It’s so progressive.”

“It isn’t about being progressive. Money’s money, man. I don’t care who’s got it.”

There. An answer I can use. Briefly, we have reached the subject of money.

“I ask every question,” he adds, and looks at Valphine. “You’re a drow.”

“Very astute.”

“I thought Drow didn’t like the sunlight.”

“Oh, I love the sunlight.”

Her eyes glow gold above her white armor. He has chosen an interesting person to explain sunlight to.

“I love the sunlight,” she repeats.

“It isn’t our duty to educate him,” I say. “He needs to learn on his own. He needs to put in the work.”

I bring him back again.

“So you just take money and don’t ask questions. You don’t know anything about your tenants?”

“How do you deal with the KYC laws in here?” Valphine asks.

“No problem,” I say.

The terms of the lodging finally emerge. He requires substantial cash up front. This is no place for a day laborer to arrive with the evening’s wages and hope they will cover a bed. Our carrier’s room takes money in advance. The same carrier who finishes his shift with a bought meal and sleeps among belongings better than his work would suggest.

“Yeah, it seems odd. You never do any sort of background check? Never make sure people have the gold they claim they’re gonna have after months?”

“I don’t scry on my customers.”

“I don’t believe that. Given the racism you presume, I’m sure you scry on certain ones of them, don’t you?”

His gaze catches on Brewbarry’s chest. The muscles move. The landlord watches them.

“Jiggling about,” I observe.

“Look, I don’t want any trouble.”

We have managed to frighten him without getting a price. My bale can be efficient when it chooses.

“If you want a place to stay here, I’ll give you a discount.”

“Really, we just want to know about one of your tenants.”

“Alright.”

I describe the man we followed home.

“You mean Denvar?”

“Denvar. Yeah. That’s more than we came with, we got a name.” I try it again. “Denvar!”

The landlord has supplied one useful fact. I should let him supply another before I wear it out.

“Denvar, Colorado.”

Then I return to the matter in front of us.

“Yes, yes, we mean Denvar. He dropped something last night.”

The man upstairs does not need to know that three armed strangers have followed him home. A lost pouch gives us a reason to ask after him. A helpful reason.

“We saw him skulking about in the, the alleyway super late at night, and he dropped a little pouch. We’d like to return it to him.”

“Making a delivery,” Valphine adds.

“Oh.”

Yes. That sounds better than skulking. We are being helpful.

While we talk, the rat’s search produces something useful at last. Delivery chits. They are kept in order, covering roughly seven to ten days. I hold my attention on them. This is what I sent the familiar to find.

Each carries the same instruction.

“9 crates, 3rd hour, 8 up, 1 down.”

I go over it under my breath.

“Third up, one down.”

No. I look again.

“Oh, eight up. Sorry. Okay.”

Nine crates. Eight up. One down. A third-hour delivery. The repetition matters more than my first attempt to read it.

There is a client reference and a house mark. No personal name anywhere. Those are the only identifying details, and they do not yet tell me who stands behind the work. The chits are reconciled through weekly settlements.

“And the… all his deliveries… 100% of his delivery chits are the same. They’re all this one thing. He literally has one job.”

I look through them again. Same quantities. Same instruction.

“Yeah. It is. Wow. Yeah, no, that’s not suspicious.”

The earlier chits have settlement marks. Payment stamps. Somebody has accounted for the work and paid him for it.

“They clearly seem to be related to this one contract.”

Then I reach the newest chit. Seven in the group. Six paid. The seventh has no settlement stamp.

That is better than an insignia. An insignia might tell us who he belongs to, if we recognize it. Unpaid work gives him somewhere to go.

“And so that was the one that he just, presumably delivered. Yeah, so I guess we have reason to follow him once he wakes up, don’t we?”

He has finished the delivery we watched. He has come home and gone to sleep. The payment is still outstanding.

“He needs to be able to afford a place like this. He needs the money pretty quickly.”

The landlord can tell us how quickly. I ask whether Denvar is settled up on his bills.

He owes money today.

“Yeah?”

Today. I hold onto that.

“Yeah, so he’s… he’s motivated.”

No need to wake him. No need to search every house mark in the city while he sleeps above us. If his next errand is to collect his pay, we can follow him to somebody who knows more.

“You know, he’s just sleeping in his room right now, right? Like…”

“He’s got until the end of the day, and he’s a good customer. He’s quiet. He doesn’t bother anybody.”

“I’ll bet.”

“Good guy, huh?” Valphine asks.

“Yeah, totally good guy.”

Quiet. Pays reliably. Does not trouble the other tenants. The landlord knows what he needs to know about Denvar. We need the part that happens before the money reaches him.

“You don’t think he’s a serial killer or anything?”

His eyes move over us again.

This time he takes inventory. My staff. Valphine’s white armor and glowing eyes. Brewbarry’s halberd. He looks back at me.

“Should I be worried?”

“You never know with these types. You never know.”

“I’d be worried about him,” Valphine says.

The landlord has his own unpaid bill to consider now. I leave him to it. We have a name, a sleeping man, a payment due, and an end-of-day deadline. Enough to make waiting useful.

Before we leave, he turns back to me.

“Soma, now that I know turtles are looking for housing, perhaps you could tell me what makes a good turtle room?”

I could give him a very expensive answer. He is listening properly now.

But I think of a hearth. Water close enough that fetching it does not become the work of the day. Somewhere to sit with the weight off my joints.

“Oh, a nice hearth, plenty of water. Being near a body of water is nice.”
