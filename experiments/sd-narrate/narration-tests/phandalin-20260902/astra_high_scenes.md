## Scene 1: Patrons of the Common Chord

### Vukradin

The Common Chord used to throw me out unpaid. Now it is the hardest ticket in Neverwinter, and Brewbarry’s table has leather, a shine, and a plaque. I look at the table, then at Brewbarry. There is something deeply encouraging about a room recognizing the value of the people who played in it, provided those people can still afford to enter the room.

We have a few hours before our business at Lim’s food deliveries. Aldus Hern has still not confirmed the exact time of our dinner with Dagult Neverember. A summons requires a time if one is expected to arrange an evening around it. Until then, his lordship can wait.

“Is it Neverember or Neverwinter? I’m kidding.”

The name keeps working at me.

“It is never, like, a fire ember.”

“He’s never flaming,” Soma says. “He keeps it on the DL.”

“Yeah, I think Soma has it exactly right.”

There. A perfectly serviceable way to remember our host.

“Okay, and Lord Neverember… Lord Neverflaming has a new drow advisor.”

“Great, isn’t that Valphine?”

“No.”

Soma remembers. I look toward Valphine.

“It’s a rumor, and I suspect Valphine will know this advisor at dinner, but we will see.”

“Oh, right, right, right,” Valphine says. “Which we assume to be one of my siblings, in fact.”

“That is my guess too, yes.”

“Everyone’s related to everyone,” Soma says.

A rumor is still a rumor. I would prefer to meet this advisor before deciding what their presence means, although dinner with a member of Valphine’s family would certainly give us something to discuss. We already have House Margaster to contend with. Valphine regards that house as competition, which is a different starting point from mine.

Brewbarry’s bathrobe business has its capital secured. I have to straighten that out in my head, because the money and the difficulty of using the money have become entangled. The loan is arranged. The imports are expensive.

“But, oh, but the cost of import is very high.”

“Right, because their shipping lanes are still…” Soma pauses over the terminology. “Shipping portals, whatever, are still hosed.”

And House Margaster controls the available logistics. They deliver on time and on schedule. I can acknowledge punctuality. I can also object to a situation in which Brewbarry finally has the means to bring softness to people, and the next thing between him and doing it is an expensive route controlled by House Margaster. The goods arriving promptly does not make the cost disappear.

“Alright, let’s head out to Lim’s Food Deliveries and get there by 3 AM,” I say. “And then we can cast whatever spells we need in order to be ready.”

That gives us our next fixed obligation. It also leaves time here, among the tables and the fancy food, where a local is explaining that the Common Chord has become unaffordable.

Because of Vukradin.

I am close enough to hear this accusation against myself, but he has not realized that the person responsible is standing nearby. I listen. He wants to come here. He wants what this place offered, and the price has put it out of his reach, and somehow my name has become the explanation for why he cannot have it.

I remember playing to empty rooms. I remember having to persuade people that the music was worth hearing at all. Now my name can apparently help fill a room while making someone who wants to listen resent me. That cannot be the resolution.

So I approach him and explain that I am planning an independent music studio in Phandalin. There is a charitable fund. He can contribute if he would like to help us open it sooner.

“That will speed the opening of a low-cost, nonprofit way to hear Lathander’s Death on a regular basis.”

This is something we can actually do about his complaint. A studio needs money to open, yes, but affordable music is the purpose of raising it. I want people to hear us regularly. I want a place where we can work, where music can be made and people can come to it without first calculating whether they can afford an evening.

“And spread the word.”

While we talk, conversation elsewhere in the bar reaches us. The sewers have a tide. Water comes through from the river, and the river is affected by the sea beside Neverwinter.

I turn back toward the others.

“There’s a tide in the sewers? A river has a tide?”

The coastal part makes sense once I stop thinking of the river as a thing that only runs in one direction. Still, if we are going to hide underground, I want the timing understood before the water arrives.

“How many moons does this planet have?”

“That’s an easy question to answer,” Valphine says.

“Just the one.”

“It just rotates—it revolves really fast,” Soma says.

“It has one moon named Selûne,” Valphine says.

“Oh, Selûne. Of course. Yeah.”

For a moment the Tears of Selûne complicate the calculation in my head, but they are asteroids, not another moon. The useful information comes out of the surrounding tavern conversation clearly enough: two high tides and two low tides each day. I work through the intervals, correct myself, and arrive at six hours from high to low.

“We want to be in the sewer for… at low tide.”

“3 AM,” Soma supplies.

“We have about 3 hours, maybe 4, before things start to get really uncomfortable.”

“I mean, I can hang out underwater indefinitely.”

A useful advantage for Soma. The rest of us need to respect the window. Three or four hours gives us time to work, provided we enter when intended and remember that the water will keep rising whether our business is finished or not.

The local I have approached is on the drunk side, but my explanation of the studio has reached him. He does not have much money. He hands me ten gold pieces.

I look down at them.

“Wow, that’s pretty generous, actually.”

“That’s a hell of a donation,” Soma says.

It is. This is someone complaining that a night of music costs too much, and he has just put ten gold toward helping make another place possible. I keep the coins separate from our ordinary money.

“That goes in the studio fund, not the regular coffers. Thank you very much for contributing. That’s awesome.”

He pauses.

“Do I get on… Is there some kind of signage for people who contributed?”

“Absolutely. Yes.”

Of course there should be recognition. He is helping build something. His name belongs in the program.

“There are different tiers of giving levels, actually. 10 gold pieces puts you at the,”

“Premier or the Plus, which one?” Soma asks.

“At the plus level, yeah, you’re not quite at Premier yet.”

He is happy with that. Plus is a good level. Ten gold is substantial, and an annual program credit gives us a way to acknowledge the people who made the music possible.

“A year. If you want to be in next year’s, you’ll have to donate again, but one year is a long time.”

“Yeah, we call this Patreon,” Valphine says.

“It’s my Patreon, exactly.”

“This is where it comes from, patronage,” Soma says.

Patronage. Yes. Someone hears what we want to build and helps us build it. There is room in that arrangement for gratitude, and names in a program, and music people can afford. I can explain that to more than one person.

We still have plenty of time before the low tide.

“Alright, let’s do… let’s hit up a few more people, let’s see how it goes here.”

I approach five more patrons. The first recognizes me. The real Vukradin, here in the Common Chord, discussing a studio fund. He has already completed his charitable giving for the year.

“Okay, that’s fine. I’m not here to pressure anybody.”

He has made his choices about where to help. I have no complaint with that. The opportunity to contribute is an opportunity, and if he wants to hear the music, then we already have something to talk about.

“The benefits are all on you. If you can be at the Plus or Premier level, I’m cool. I just like fans.”

“Biggest fan, biggest fan,” he says.

“Oh, Bigger Than Brewbarry?”

“Brewbarry’s very large,” Valphine says. “In fact, he’s huge in Japan sometimes.”

I look at Brewbarry. The claim was always going to be difficult to sustain in his presence.

There is no donation from this patron, and there does not need to be. I move on.

Wick is enthusiastic about patronage of the arts. He does not know Lathander’s Death. He also does not believe I am Vukradin, which proves less of an obstacle than one might expect. He thinks the music school is an excellent undertaking.

“School?” Soma says.

“School, studio—they’re all the same, right? If you have a studio, it’s for training people. That’s just how it goes.”

The studio in my mind has always contained people making music. People learn by making it, and by hearing what someone else can do, and by trying again with someone there to help. Wick’s understanding gives that part of the ambition more room. He wants to support it. I am glad to accept.

Sabbatha’s attention keeps returning to Brewbarry. She looks him over and admires how thoroughly he has committed to the Brewbarry look.

It is a considerable commitment.

“Yes, he is quite bulging, isn’t he?”

“Rippling,” Soma adds.

“Exactly.”

Brewbarry has brought his entire self to this evening. The table has a plaque, people are studying his appearance, and Sabbatha contributes to the studio. I am pleased for him. I am pleased for us.

Ondrelle Kass contributes as well. The program notes acquire names: Wick, Sabbatha, Ondrelle Kass. Then there is Old Hesp, the last patron I meet.

He recognizes me.

“I remember when you used to play to an empty room.”

“Yeah?”

That reaches me more directly than the other declarations of recognition. An empty room is a specific memory. There was room for every note, certainly. There was also room for the audience that had not come.

“Did you come? Did you come? Did I give you your money back at the end of.”

I did. He remembers the guarantee: if he did not rate the performance ten out of ten, I would not keep his money.

“I know, that’s how honest I am. Well, what do you think now?”

He pauses.

“Now this is a friggin’ expensive place to be. Everybody’s gotta look cool.”

I look around the Common Chord again. Here is someone who actually came when I was playing, who remembers the promise I made about his money, and what he has to tell me now is that being here costs too much. I cannot answer him with the popularity of the venue. He is standing in the popular venue. He knows.

“Well, come to the new studio, when it’s up and running.”

He misses the feel of the old place, the grit of it. I understand that better with him here, because my memory of this room includes being unwanted and unpaid, while his includes something he wants to recover. I do not need to wish for the empty audience to want him back in it.

“We’re gonna get it back. We’re gonna get it back.”

“This is too commercial.”

“Yeah, yeah, I agree.”

Old Hesp blames Petra, although he respects what she has accomplished. She has made the Common Chord a money-making operation. He points toward Brewbarry’s table and remembers when it did not have leather, when it was not shiny, when there was no plaque identifying it. Then he calls my attention to the food. They did not serve food like this before.

I follow his pointing hand. Leather. Plaque. Fancy food. My name attached to a room where a former listener has to worry about looking right.

Petra’s separate five hundred gold payment for my likeness is still pending until our performance two days from now. It will be a substantial sum. Here, tonight, the studio receives twenty gold from people I have spoken with among the tables. I keep the amounts distinct. The five hundred has not arrived, and these twenty gold already have a purpose.

Old Hesp goes into the program notes with Wick, Sabbatha, and Ondrelle Kass.

I want to see those names in a program someone can afford to sit down and read. Hesp can remember the old room without having to dress for the new one. Wick can hear what Lathander’s Death actually sounds like. There will be work before any of that happens, but the coins are here, the names are recorded, and the people have told me why they care.

For now, we have another appointment. Three in the morning. Low tide. Perhaps three or four useful hours underground.

“Alright, let’s go, do some… spelunking.”

## Scene 2: The Ninth Crate Disappears

### Valphine Sotorra

The stone steps descend towards the sewer grille in shallow, wet courses, an aqueduct’s practical geometry reduced to an entrance for goods whose owners prefer not to trouble the harbor authorities. From our position beside the approach, I can watch the carriers lower their crates without mistaking their labor for their purpose. There are two men. For the moment, both are carrying boxes.

Soma casts *Find Familiar*, and a rat appears, small enough to belong among wet stone and sewer openings without inviting examination. Through its eyes and ears she can extend her attention a hundred feet; useful here, though a hundred feet of intelligence is not the same as a companion who can intervene.

Vukradin offers himself for the less limited pursuit.

“We need someone to do something stealthily.”

“I mean, you could tag along with a familiar…” Soma says.

His Boots of Elvenkind give him an advantage that the rest of us cannot conveniently acquire by deciding to be quiet. I consider the steps, the grille, the distance a carrier might put between himself and us while we discuss who is best qualified to walk after him.

Below, the crates accumulate.

“All 9 of them?” Soma asks.

All nine. Eight have no official Commission Harbor mark, their bare surfaces confirming that this delivery entered Neverwinter outside its approved channels. The missing marks establish an evasion; they do not establish who possesses enough influence to sustain it. That is the more interesting distinction, and one unlikely to emerge from questioning the wood.

“So we want one person to track down the gnome, right? Crate 9,” Vukradin says. “We also want to track the people who dropped them. So we want to do two things.”

“Well, I’m tracking the crate.”

“Which crate?”

“The ninth crate, the one that doesn’t go into Lim, because I’m more concerned about my friend’s shady business.”

Soma’s concern has a precise boundary: she wants to understand Lim’s business without destroying it in the process. I approve of the distinction. A frightened connection can cease to be useful long before it ceases to be alive, and we promised Lim discretion.

“Okay, so you want me to follow them back into the sewers?” Vukradin asks. “That seems pretty dangerous. One party member in the sewers.”

“I see. Alright, we can send the rat that way, then. You can follow the crate.”

The carriers make the immediate arrangement clearer than our discussion does. Eight crates stand together for Lim. The ninth rests separately on a ledge, close to the darkness beyond the grille, where someone can collect it without disturbing the larger stack. The division is deliberate and economical. Two deliveries have shared the labor of one journey.

The man handling the separate crate pauses and looks down into the dark.

I watch him. The glance is brief, but it reaches beyond the work immediately before him; he appears to be checking for something below, something whose arrival belongs to his understanding of the delivery. Then he leaves.

Soma directs the rat towards the sewer.

“So, look, the… The crates got dropped off. Lim’s gonna come out and take 8 of the crates,” Vukradin says. “And she’s not gonna take a 9th crate.”

He intends to remain concealed here until the ninth crate’s collector appears, then follow it. We can pursue the men who brought it. There is finally an assignment attached to each question.

“So everyone other than Vukradin is gonna trail these guys, and Vukradin’s gonna trail the crate,” I say. “Is that right?”

Both agree.

The carriers promptly make that arrangement less convenient. They walk away in different directions, conspicuously avoiding so much as a look at one another, performing unfamiliarity after we have watched them complete the same delivery.

“That nonchalant whistling,” Soma says.

I appreciate the diagnosis. Their separation may be prudent, but the performance does not survive an audience that arrived before its beginning.

“So if they split up, that’s gonna make them hard to follow,” Vukradin says. “You’re gonna have to choose one or split again.”

I examine the division of labor again. At first, I cannot confidently place one man above the other; handling a separate box is not a title, and I have seen sufficient servants entrusted with dangerous objects to resist that easy inference. I draw on the last reserve of confidence Vukradin’s inspiration affords me and hold the useful detail apart from the rest: the look below.

Seniority is unnecessary. Knowledge is sufficient.

One man stacked boxes. The other placed the ninth where its collector would find it, then looked towards the place from which that collector might emerge. He is the better prospect, whether he gives orders or merely receives more complete ones.

“I’d follow the guy who paid attention to the ledge,” Soma says. “He knows something. He knows something’s coming out of that thing.”

I choose him. Brewbarry and Soma will come with me.

“I could handle this myself, guys, you don’t trust me?” Soma asks.

Before we can leave, something comes up from below.

It is not a gnome.

The construct is cat-sized, brass and leather fitted into a small, quick body, and its movements contain none of the carriers’ elaborate social precautions. It approaches the ninth crate, takes it, and returns beneath the grille, completing its task without hurry despite the speed of its limbs.

I am surprised. We expected a person, and a person might hesitate, bargain, betray an employer, or look towards an accomplice. This collector offers us a mechanism and a direction. Somewhere beyond it there may still be a mind worth questioning, but reaching that mind now requires following an obedient object underground.

Vukradin moves to pursue it.

“This is my concern,” Soma says. “Vukradin ends up going into the sewers now, and…”

“I’ll be fine.”

“One person in the sewers? Alright.” She allows the reversal to stand for a moment. “Tell me the odds.”

“Only a 1 in 400 chance of disaster. It’ll be fine.”

His numerical reassurance is remarkably exact for a tunnel we have not examined. I would prefer to know what he has included under *disaster*, but the construct is already going below, and our human lead is walking away. Neither pursuit will improve while we refine the other’s risks.

Vukradin follows, his elven boots serving the purpose for which he recommended himself. Soma and he carry the paired Sending Stones, giving our divided party a means of communication even after the sewer takes him from sight. It is a useful provision. It does not widen the passage or place Brewbarry beside him.

I turn after the carrier.

The fourth hour leaves us few open establishments to consider, and he chooses one of the cook shops still serving food. We follow him there, keeping our interest from becoming an introduction. The wet descent and the waiting crates give way to a place occupied with the ordinary business of feeding people at an inconvenient hour.

“Can’t be a lot of those,” Soma remarks.

The man settles to breakfast alone and eats with apparent enjoyment. The mouth that might explain the ledge is presently engaged elsewhere. I watch him without promoting the meal into a meeting merely because a meeting would be more useful to us.

“He’s not eating turtle soup or anything at this hour, is he?” Soma asks.

Her attention remains on the shop.

“It’s not the Board Laid Bare he’s at, of course, he’s somewhere else.”

That distinction matters to her. Lim’s business has brought us here, but this is another establishment, and the man’s appetite gives us no immediate reason to connect its proprietor to the unmarked crates. We have followed a carrier to breakfast. An investigation requires the discipline to endure that fact.

“He’s just eating alone. He isn’t talking to anybody.”

We could approach him. Between Brewbarry’s presence and my questions, we could make his breakfast difficult to enjoy, but the promised discretion would be gone at the first indication that we know what he left beside the grille. Lim would bear some portion of the consequences, and whatever route he follows after this might close to us.

Soma reaches the same practical conclusion.

“I’m not trying to put her out of business. I want to see where he goes next. Stopping for a snack doesn’t tell us anything, and spooking him doesn’t seem kosher.”

I remain where I can watch him. He has already shown us one useful distinction between his work and his companion’s; I can allow him the time required to show us another.

Across the intervening space, he continues eating his breakfast. We wait.

## Scene 3: Lost Among Unexpected Fans

### Vukradin

I remember the crate. I remember that I was following it, and that following it mattered, but the passage ahead no longer seems worth the trouble. The purpose is still there when I reach for it. The interest is gone.

There must be something more useful in another direction.

I take another direction.

The sewer carries on around me, damp underfoot, the confined air sitting against my face. By the time I stop, the others are no longer with me. I look back along the passage. I cannot account for why I chose this one. I can account for the crate, for the pursuit, for having companions, which ought to be enough to establish where a person is going, and yet here I am with none of those things in front of me.

A man stands ahead of a group of other men. He gives me a peculiar look.

“How the hell did you get over here?”

“I don't know.” I look past him, then back. “Where am I?”

He looks at me.

“Because I'm not over there.”

That answers how I come to be here in the broadest possible sense. It does very little for either of us in the narrower one. I try to understand what concerns him, what sort of men I have walked into, whether this is a misunderstanding I can resolve by explaining myself. I get nowhere. He seems confused rather than angry, but confusion is all we have in common so far.

I do know what I was doing before I arrived here. I do not think a detailed account of the crate will improve my request for directions. Being lost is the part that matters.

“Yeah, there you go. I'm lost. I don't know what… I don't know how I got here, I just…”

The words falter in exactly the place where an explanation should go. He accepts it. Whatever else he thinks of me, he believes that I do not know where I am.

“Yeah. Wrong turn. Should have taken that left at Albuquerque.”

He licks his finger.

“Well, this is my territory.”

“This is your territory.”

“Yes, this is my territory. Wow.”

I look at the tunnel again. I had not understood that I was crossing a boundary. A person can hardly respect a boundary he cannot locate.

“Did you want me to go?”

“I'd want to know why you're in my territory.”

“Oh, I'm sorry that I got lost. I'm happy to leave. Could you point me in the direction of Lim's? Cafe, that's… that's where I want to be headed.”

“Lim's Cafe?”

“We're friends.”

This makes things worse.

His attention sharpens. A moment ago I was an unexplained visitor; now the name of someone I know has given him a reason to examine me more closely. I cannot tell what Lim means to him, and I have no useful theory about why friendship should complicate directions to a cafe. Still, we have found a connection. We can work from a connection.

“Are you that singer?”

“Exactly. And Lim's a fan.”

“What are you doing down here, man?”

“I told you, I got lost!”

He looks at me again.

“Vukradin… you're Vukradin, right?”

One of the men beside him is smiling. Really smiling, openly pleased, and I feel the relief of it before I have quite caught up with the change in our situation.

“This is… is this really Vukradin?”

Here, too. Beneath Neverwinter, in a stretch of sewer I cannot name, somebody is happy to see me. I had been trying to explain my position in relation to several tunnels, when all along there was an entirely better way for us to understand each other.

“Do you guys want autographs?”

They would love autographs.

“Sure, we can do some autographs, but then I gotta be going.”

There are still friends to find. I am pleased to meet these men, and I want them to have something to remember the meeting by, but the fact that I have found fans does not mean I have found my way.

“Point me in the right direction.”

“You know what, we're gonna do one better, we're gonna take you there.”

“Alright, sounds good.”

“We don't want you to get lost again, Mr. Vukradin.”

“Lim will be thrilled.”

An escort is excellent. An escort means that the person who understands the tunnels will be responsible for choosing them, which seems a sensible division of abilities. I am ready to proceed on that basis when the leader pauses.

“It's very dangerous down here.”

“Is it?”

His answer begins uncertainly, and I try to make the question more useful.

“Yeah, it's dangerous in your territory? I would think you'd keep it very safe. You seem like a safe individual. I'm sure you keep the place shipshape.”

“Mr. Vukradin, I agree with you. But I have to tell you why I was out here. I was concerned when I saw you, and I'm very glad I recognized you. One of my guys tells me you have a deal with a friend of mine.”

“At the Common Chord?”

“No, no, at the Toe mine.”

“Mountain's Toe Mine. Oh, yeah…”

“Zeleen…”

“Oh, I didn't know you knew him.”

Zeleen Varnaster knows this man. The mine reaches farther than its production would suggest. I came down here following a crate and have somehow arrived at a business acquaintance.

“Yeah, we spent some time in Phandalin, yeah, yeah.”

“Yeah, he said that he had a business relationship with you, I said that he was full of shit.”

“No, no, that's true, that's true. Yeah, they haven't produced very much yet, but they say they're working hard.”

He laughs.

“That's how I feel about it, too.”

He laughs again. I am glad we can agree about this. Working hard is encouraging, but I would also appreciate something produced by the work. The studio in Phandalin needs actual money, and my ambitions do not become less sincere because I would like the mine to contribute some.

“Look, I manage the tunnels here,” he says, still laughing.

“Oh, you're the tunnel manager.”

“Yeah, a tunnel manager.”

“So this is your territory, and you have all the tunnels.”

“Not all the tunnels, but the tunnels in this area.”

That is a useful distinction. Rsolk manages a particular area, and something within it has become dangerous enough that he needs to warn an unexpected visitor. I listen while he explains.

“Two months ago, things started coming up the floodline.”

“Floodline.”

He describes a tunnel that floods regularly. I try to place it in relation to where we are, though the practical workings of this place remain well beyond me.

“I don't know anything about tunnels, man, I'm a musician.”

“I've been having this problem with the tunnels. I've lost men, so we don't go there anymore.”

The laughter is finished. I look at the men behind him. These are people who knew the way down here, people for whom this was familiar ground, and some of them went into a tunnel and did not come back. Knowing the route was not enough.

That is something my friends and I may be able to help with.

“Do you need help with that? My friends and I have some abilities, and we're looking for ways to make money. Maybe we can help you.”

He needs very little persuading. The offer has his attention immediately, and I can see a practical possibility opening between us: he needs a danger removed, we have experience removing dangers, and a payment for useful work could bring the studio closer without anybody having to pretend that finding somebody else's possessions makes them ours.

First, though, I need to understand what took his men.

“One of the guys who escaped said there was some kind of creature they'd never seen before.”

“Oh.”

“It looked like…”

“Creature stuff.”

“It looked like a spider, but it had all the wrong colors.”

That brings back something I do recognize. The strange webbing we encountered before, the wrongness of it, the difficulty of looking at something a spider had made and accepting that it belonged in front of us. Rsolk describes webs that were visibly wrong as well. I cannot identify a creature from a survivor's account alone, but this is more familiar territory than the floodline.

“Yeah, we have experience with that.”

I wish it were experience we had no further use for. Still, it is useful to Rsolk now. He has lost men, and we have faced something resembling what the survivor saw. We can come back with the others and investigate. I do not have to solve the tunnel by myself simply because I have wandered into it by myself.

With the possibility of help established, Rsolk has another request.

“Mr. Vukradin, I know this is too much to ask, but could you get me some tickets to the Common Chord?”

“I can ask.”

Tickets. I am glad he asks, though this is a different problem from spiders and requires its own accurate statement of our abilities.

“we have kind of an arm's length relationship with the Common Chord, but I will ask for you.”

“That's all I could ask for.”

We have not settled a price for clearing the tunnel. We have settled that he wants our help, that I will ask about tickets, and that there is work here worth bringing to my friends. That is enough to begin with. I can already imagine explaining the opportunity to them: unnatural spiders, men lost near a flooding tunnel, someone who knows how to get us there. A possible contribution to the studio, earned by making this place safer.

“Alright, let's do some autographs, and then we gotta be on our way.”

I give the men their autographs. The smiling associate is here in front of me, and for these few moments there is nothing difficult about understanding why we are all standing in this tunnel.

Then Rsolk and his associates escort me back toward familiar ground. I leave with the directions we will need to find him again, following people who know where they are going.
