### Vukradin

The Common Chord used to throw me out unpaid. Now it is the hardest ticket in Neverwinter, and Brewbarry’s table has leather, a shine, and a plaque. I look at the table, then at Brewbarry. There is something deeply encouraging about a room recognizing the value of the people who played in it, provided those people can still afford to enter the room.

We have a few hours before our business at Lim’s food deliveries. Aldus Hern has still not confirmed the exact time of our dinner with Dagult Neverember. A summons requires a time if one is expected to arrange an evening around it. Until then, his lordship can wait.

“Is it Neverember or Neverwinter? I’m kidding.”

The name keeps working at me.

“It is never, like, a fire ember.”

“He’s never flaming,” Soma says. “He keeps it on the DL.”

“Yeah, I think Soma has it exactly right.”

There. A perfectly serviceable way to remember our host.

“Okay, and Lord Neverember… Lord Neverflaming has a new drow advisor.”

“Great, isn’t that Valphine?”

“No.”

Soma remembers. I look toward Valphine.

“It’s a rumor, and I suspect Valphine will know this advisor at dinner, but we will see.”

“Oh, right, right, right,” Valphine says. “Which we assume to be one of my siblings, in fact.”

“That is my guess too, yes.”

“Everyone’s related to everyone,” Soma says.

A rumor is still a rumor. I would prefer to meet this advisor before deciding what their presence means, although dinner with a member of Valphine’s family would certainly give us something to discuss. We already have House Margaster to contend with. Valphine regards that house as competition, which is a different starting point from mine.

Brewbarry’s bathrobe business has its capital secured. I have to straighten that out in my head, because the money and the difficulty of using the money have become entangled. The loan is arranged. The imports are expensive.

“But, oh, but the cost of import is very high.”

“Right, because their shipping lanes are still…” Soma pauses over the terminology. “Shipping portals, whatever, are still hosed.”

And House Margaster controls the available logistics. They deliver on time and on schedule. I can acknowledge punctuality. I can also object to a situation in which Brewbarry finally has the means to bring softness to people, and the next thing between him and doing it is an expensive route controlled by House Margaster. The goods arriving promptly does not make the cost disappear.

“Alright, let’s head out to Lim’s Food Deliveries and get there by 3 AM,” I say. “And then we can cast whatever spells we need in order to be ready.”

That gives us our next fixed obligation. It also leaves time here, among the tables and the fancy food, where a local is explaining that the Common Chord has become unaffordable.

Because of Vukradin.

I am close enough to hear this accusation against myself, but he has not realized that the person responsible is standing nearby. I listen. He wants to come here. He wants what this place offered, and the price has put it out of his reach, and somehow my name has become the explanation for why he cannot have it.

I remember playing to empty rooms. I remember having to persuade people that the music was worth hearing at all. Now my name can apparently help fill a room while making someone who wants to listen resent me. That cannot be the resolution.

So I approach him and explain that I am planning an independent music studio in Phandalin. There is a charitable fund. He can contribute if he would like to help us open it sooner.

“That will speed the opening of a low-cost, nonprofit way to hear Lathander’s Death on a regular basis.”

This is something we can actually do about his complaint. A studio needs money to open, yes, but affordable music is the purpose of raising it. I want people to hear us regularly. I want a place where we can work, where music can be made and people can come to it without first calculating whether they can afford an evening.

“And spread the word.”

While we talk, conversation elsewhere in the bar reaches us. The sewers have a tide. Water comes through from the river, and the river is affected by the sea beside Neverwinter.

I turn back toward the others.

“There’s a tide in the sewers? A river has a tide?”

The coastal part makes sense once I stop thinking of the river as a thing that only runs in one direction. Still, if we are going to hide underground, I want the timing understood before the water arrives.

“How many moons does this planet have?”

“That’s an easy question to answer,” Valphine says.

“Just the one.”

“It just rotates—it revolves really fast,” Soma says.

“It has one moon named Selûne,” Valphine says.

“Oh, Selûne. Of course. Yeah.”

For a moment the Tears of Selûne complicate the calculation in my head, but they are asteroids, not another moon. The useful information comes out of the surrounding tavern conversation clearly enough: two high tides and two low tides each day. I work through the intervals, correct myself, and arrive at six hours from high to low.

“We want to be in the sewer for… at low tide.”

“3 AM,” Soma supplies.

“We have about 3 hours, maybe 4, before things start to get really uncomfortable.”

“I mean, I can hang out underwater indefinitely.”

A useful advantage for Soma. The rest of us need to respect the window. Three or four hours gives us time to work, provided we enter when intended and remember that the water will keep rising whether our business is finished or not.

The local I have approached is on the drunk side, but my explanation of the studio has reached him. He does not have much money. He hands me ten gold pieces.

I look down at them.

“Wow, that’s pretty generous, actually.”

“That’s a hell of a donation,” Soma says.

It is. This is someone complaining that a night of music costs too much, and he has just put ten gold toward helping make another place possible. I keep the coins separate from our ordinary money.

“That goes in the studio fund, not the regular coffers. Thank you very much for contributing. That’s awesome.”

He pauses.

“Do I get on… Is there some kind of signage for people who contributed?”

“Absolutely. Yes.”

Of course there should be recognition. He is helping build something. His name belongs in the program.

“There are different tiers of giving levels, actually. 10 gold pieces puts you at the,”

“Premier or the Plus, which one?” Soma asks.

“At the plus level, yeah, you’re not quite at Premier yet.”

He is happy with that. Plus is a good level. Ten gold is substantial, and an annual program credit gives us a way to acknowledge the people who made the music possible.

“A year. If you want to be in next year’s, you’ll have to donate again, but one year is a long time.”

“Yeah, we call this Patreon,” Valphine says.

“It’s my Patreon, exactly.”

“This is where it comes from, patronage,” Soma says.

Patronage. Yes. Someone hears what we want to build and helps us build it. There is room in that arrangement for gratitude, and names in a program, and music people can afford. I can explain that to more than one person.

We still have plenty of time before the low tide.

“Alright, let’s do… let’s hit up a few more people, let’s see how it goes here.”

I approach five more patrons. The first recognizes me. The real Vukradin, here in the Common Chord, discussing a studio fund. He has already completed his charitable giving for the year.

“Okay, that’s fine. I’m not here to pressure anybody.”

He has made his choices about where to help. I have no complaint with that. The opportunity to contribute is an opportunity, and if he wants to hear the music, then we already have something to talk about.

“The benefits are all on you. If you can be at the Plus or Premier level, I’m cool. I just like fans.”

“Biggest fan, biggest fan,” he says.

“Oh, Bigger Than Brewbarry?”

“Brewbarry’s very large,” Valphine says. “In fact, he’s huge in Japan sometimes.”

I look at Brewbarry. The claim was always going to be difficult to sustain in his presence.

There is no donation from this patron, and there does not need to be. I move on.

Wick is enthusiastic about patronage of the arts. He does not know Lathander’s Death. He also does not believe I am Vukradin, which proves less of an obstacle than one might expect. He thinks the music school is an excellent undertaking.

“School?” Soma says.

“School, studio—they’re all the same, right? If you have a studio, it’s for training people. That’s just how it goes.”

The studio in my mind has always contained people making music. People learn by making it, and by hearing what someone else can do, and by trying again with someone there to help. Wick’s understanding gives that part of the ambition more room. He wants to support it. I am glad to accept.

Sabbatha’s attention keeps returning to Brewbarry. She looks him over and admires how thoroughly he has committed to the Brewbarry look.

It is a considerable commitment.

“Yes, he is quite bulging, isn’t he?”

“Rippling,” Soma adds.

“Exactly.”

Brewbarry has brought his entire self to this evening. The table has a plaque, people are studying his appearance, and Sabbatha contributes to the studio. I am pleased for him. I am pleased for us.

Ondrelle Kass contributes as well. The program notes acquire names: Wick, Sabbatha, Ondrelle Kass. Then there is Old Hesp, the last patron I meet.

He recognizes me.

“I remember when you used to play to an empty room.”

“Yeah?”

That reaches me more directly than the other declarations of recognition. An empty room is a specific memory. There was room for every note, certainly. There was also room for the audience that had not come.

“Did you come? Did you come? Did I give you your money back at the end of.”

I did. He remembers the guarantee: if he did not rate the performance ten out of ten, I would not keep his money.

“I know, that’s how honest I am. Well, what do you think now?”

He pauses.

“Now this is a friggin’ expensive place to be. Everybody’s gotta look cool.”

I look around the Common Chord again. Here is someone who actually came when I was playing, who remembers the promise I made about his money, and what he has to tell me now is that being here costs too much. I cannot answer him with the popularity of the venue. He is standing in the popular venue. He knows.

“Well, come to the new studio, when it’s up and running.”

He misses the feel of the old place, the grit of it. I understand that better with him here, because my memory of this room includes being unwanted and unpaid, while his includes something he wants to recover. I do not need to wish for the empty audience to want him back in it.

“We’re gonna get it back. We’re gonna get it back.”

“This is too commercial.”

“Yeah, yeah, I agree.”

Old Hesp blames Petra, although he respects what she has accomplished. She has made the Common Chord a money-making operation. He points toward Brewbarry’s table and remembers when it did not have leather, when it was not shiny, when there was no plaque identifying it. Then he calls my attention to the food. They did not serve food like this before.

I follow his pointing hand. Leather. Plaque. Fancy food. My name attached to a room where a former listener has to worry about looking right.

Petra’s separate five hundred gold payment for my likeness is still pending until our performance two days from now. It will be a substantial sum. Here, tonight, the studio receives twenty gold from people I have spoken with among the tables. I keep the amounts distinct. The five hundred has not arrived, and these twenty gold already have a purpose.

Old Hesp goes into the program notes with Wick, Sabbatha, and Ondrelle Kass.

I want to see those names in a program someone can afford to sit down and read. Hesp can remember the old room without having to dress for the new one. Wick can hear what Lathander’s Death actually sounds like. There will be work before any of that happens, but the coins are here, the names are recorded, and the people have told me why they care.

For now, we have another appointment. Three in the morning. Low tide. Perhaps three or four useful hours underground.

“Alright, let’s go, do some… spelunking.”
