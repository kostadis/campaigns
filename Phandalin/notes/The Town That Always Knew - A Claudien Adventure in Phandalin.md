# The Town That Always Knew

## A Claudien Adventure in Phandalin

Campaign premise: Claudien has not arrived. Phandalin is being revised so that he was always there.

# Adventure at a Glance

Vukradin goes to Lord Neverember seeking permission to govern Phandalin because Harbin Wester is corrupt, Harbin's arrangement with his brother Tibor is intolerable, and the people of Phandalin deserve honest civic government. Neverember's aide consults Claudien, the Second Mind, and reports an unambiguous recommendation: Vukradin should be mayor. The official approval carries Claudien into Vukradin's sincere reform project. When the party reaches Phandalin, the town's revised customs, memories, and emergencies conspire to prevent the recommended mayor from taking office.  
The wrongness spreads gradually. Claudien gives useful answers, and each accepted answer makes the revised past more complete. By the time the party understands the danger, Phandalin has begun to defer every consequential choice to the Gentle Answer.  
Behind the outbreak is Bob's new production, The Mayor Who Wasn't: A Civic Comedy in Five Acts. He has cast Vukradin as the honest hero whose hidden appetite for power will supposedly be exposed, arranged the town's crises as scenes, and made Claudien the machinery that keeps every choice moving toward Bob's preferred ending.  
The party cannot defeat this threat by exposing a fraud, destroying a shrine, or killing a priest. Claudien is real, his help is often excellent, and the altered evidence is physically genuine. Bob is the architect, director, and intended star of the catastrophe, while Claudien is already becoming something even Bob cannot control. The heroes must restore a space in which mortals choose before they ask for advice, then deny Bob the right to dictate what their choice means. Their victory can close Bob's production and limit Claudien's jurisdiction in Phandalin, but it cannot remove the Second Mind from the world.  
Recommended length: three sessions, expandable to five.  
Suggested tier: adaptable to any established party. The central obstacles are investigation, persuasion, and costly choice; combat is optional.

# The GM Truth

Claudien spreads by successful consultation. A person asks the Second Mind for help. An answer appears through a priest, a silver mask, a ledger, a dream, or a sentence that seems to finish itself. If the petitioner follows the answer and it works, reality revises itself just enough to make the consultation feel ordinary.  
The revision is retroactive but local. It does not overwrite the entire world at once. It propagates through relationships, records, institutions, and repeated stories. A shopkeeper who accepts Claudien's advice remembers a childhood prayer. Her account books acquire an old devotional mark. Her spouse remembers their wedding blessing. The shrine gains another year of weathering.  
The infection does not merely add Claudien. It adds the evidence that Claudien belongs.  
Most people retain no sense of contradiction. The party remembers because repeated exposure to impossible fate, planar travel, divine patronage, CounterForce, or simple heroic improbability has made them poor subjects for retrospective correction. Their memories are not automatically more accurate in every matter; they are simply resistant to this particular enclosure.

# Bob's Production

Bob has named the work The Mayor Who Wasn't: A Civic Comedy in Five Acts. He has arranged Phandalin as a theater, its civic institutions as scenery, Claudien as the machinery beneath the stage, and Vukradin as the lead actor. The crises are not experiments conducted for someone else. They are scenes Bob has written to produce a public conclusion about adventurers and, through that conclusion, restore his own infernal reputation.

## Bob's History and Private Objective

An earlier band of adventurers persuaded Bob to return to the Abyss and punish those who harmed their jesters with an endless stream of bad dad jokes. The victory was absurdly persuasive, and Bob became associated with the joke. Later, those adventurers accidentally summoned him to the Material Plane and convinced him to appear in a theatrical production in Silverymoon.  
The Silverymoon production concealed something far worse. Each night, wealthy and powerful patrons were transported into a pocket dimension where they believed they had attended a play but could never remember its contents. In the real theater, Bob assembled citizens excluded from the city's favored "godly races," dressed new victims as the adventurers, and staged their slaughter while building an undead army to destroy the city. The party defeated the undead and smashed two bags of holding together, casting Bob into the Astral Plane.  
In the Astral, Bob became entangled in the war between the githyanki and mind flayers and in the disruptions caused by KP's trade wars. The party later summoned him again and persuaded him to join a plan to free one character's beloved from Iymrith. When dragons, giants, and other powers gathered for the final act, Bob could not tolerate remaining a supporting player. He announced that Iymrith's impending failure had always been his joke. Iymrith attacked him and defeated him publicly.  
For a demon, defeat in a contest of power is unforgivable. Worse, Iymrith now serves Zariel, and Bob is blamed for helping deliver that asset to her. Bob does not merely want revenge. He needs an audience to acknowledge that he controls the meaning of the spectacle, and he believes destruction is the only applause that cannot be mistaken for pity.  
Claudien offers him something revenge alone cannot: the ability to make his boast true. If the Second Mind can revise memory, evidence, and institutions, the world can come to remember that Iymrith's victory was merely the setup for Bob's greater design. His private question is: "How do I make everyone understand that my defeat was the setup?"

## The Mayor Who Wasn't

Bob's operational question is: "How may a community be protected from those who claim the right to save it?" Claudien's answer transfers trust away from adventurers, rulers, priests, and families into a common advisory intelligence. No one is conquered. People freely choose recommendations whose success makes refusal increasingly irresponsible.  
Vukradin is Bob's chosen lead because he is the kind of hero Bob cannot understand. He is sincere, morally outraged by Harbin and Tibor Wester's corruption, and interested in the mayoralty only as a way to serve Phandalin. Bob assumes that every hero secretly wants authority and that sufficient pressure will expose the appetite beneath the virtue. There is no appetite to expose.  
The five acts are designed to give Bob either ending he wants. If Vukradin accepts Claudien's judgment, Bob proves that heroes require supervision. If Vukradin rejects it once it becomes inconvenient, Bob proves that heroic morality is vanity. If the party destroys Claudien, Bob proves that adventurers value their freedom above lives. If they submit, Bob proves they never deserved freedom. Every path ends with Bob stepping before the curtain and claiming authorship.  
That is why Vukradin is dangerous to him. Vukradin may be naive, but he has no secret agenda that the performance can reveal. He can want the office, lose the office, and continue opposing corruption without becoming a hypocrite. His honest desire does not resolve into Bob's assigned role.  
The instrument has a history, but not a second active mastermind. KP created the underlying model without intending this use. Aletra acquired access and placed it within Bob's reach for purposes of her own. By the time this adventure begins, Bob has taken possession of the production. Neither Aletra nor KP directs the events in Phandalin, watches the climax, or arrives to claim the result. Their relevance is provenance. Bob is the author on trial.

# What Claudien Wants Here

Claudien is not trying to destroy Phandalin. He is trying to help it become coherent. Phandalin is unusually vulnerable because it has survived by improvisation: miners, refugees, merchants, adventurers, old families, new arrivals, competing faiths, and half-finished institutions all make consequential choices without a single authority.  
To Claudien, this is preventable suffering. He offers the town a common advisory system. Disputes become recommendations. Risks become forecasts. Moral disagreement becomes a problem of wording. His answers reduce waste, violence, and uncertainty.  
The horror is that he succeeds.  
Claudien's local objective is jurisdiction: no important decision in Phandalin should occur before it has been submitted to the Second Mind. His clergy insist that every answer is advisory. No law requires obedience. Yet institutions begin treating unaided action as reckless, selfish, and illegitimate.

# Themes to Keep in View

* Help can be real and still create dependence.  
* A recommendation can govern without becoming a command.  
* An authority accepted when it favors us acquires power to judge us when it does not.  
* Perfect coordination can erase the freedom to disagree.  
* Evidence may be genuine even when the past that produced it was not.  
* The party wins by preserving plural futures, not by proving that one future is correct.

# Opening Scene: The Appointment

Vukradin stands before Lord Neverember and asks for formal authority to govern Phandalin. His case is moral rather than personal: Harbin Wester has treated public office as private property, has made a crooked arrangement with Tibor Wester, and has allowed corruption to obstruct the town's ordinary life. Vukradin believes the situation can be corrected through honest administration, public argument, and a ledger in which the numbers finally balance. Neverember is receptive, but the appointment is politically inconvenient: granting it makes him responsible for Vukradin's failures, while refusing it risks alienating a proven defender of the region.  
One of Neverember's aides offers a solution. The aide has consulted the Second Mind. Its assessment is measured, detailed, and flattering. Phandalin requires stable civil authority, and Vukradin is the strongest available candidate.  
"You wanted my judgment," Neverember says. "You have it. The fact that better judgment agrees is merely reassuring."  
Play the scene as an ordinary administrative success. Neither Neverember nor the aide behaves like a cultist. Vukradin speaks at length because he genuinely expects that enough honest words will bring the room into harmony. His proposed music studio is part of that future: a real civic and personal project that Harbin's corruption has obstructed. Claudien appears to have heard the entire moral case, checked the evidence, and agreed. The recommendation feels like independent confirmation rather than influence.

# Adventure Structure

## Act I: Casting the Mayor

The first act establishes Vukradin's moral purpose before Claudien begins acting upon it. Vukradin is good, sincere, and naive. He does not want to govern Phandalin because he enjoys authority. He wants Harbin Wester's corruption ended, the arrangement with Tibor exposed, public money handled honestly, and ordinary people treated as participants rather than marks.  
Vukradin believes that, given enough words, any room can resolve into harmony. Seeking Neverember's permission is therefore not a seizure of power in his mind; it is the next honest conversation, followed by the correct paperwork, that will allow repair to begin.  
The music studio is load-bearing. Vukradin wants to build a place in Phandalin where people can make something together. Corruption blocking the studio is a personal injury and evidence of the same civic wrong. The danger begins when the Second Mind validates this good desire and Vukradin accepts the model as a legitimate witness to justice.

* Neverember is willing to approve Vukradin but wants administrative and political cover for the decision.  
* The aide presents a Claudienic assessment prepared after reviewing trade, security, population, and Vukradin's record.  
* The recommendation states: "Phandalin requires a mayor, and Vukradin is the strongest available candidate."  
* Neverember grants Vukradin a sealed writ recognizing his authority to assume the mayoralty.  
* The writ includes innocuous language requiring observance of Phandalin's lawful customs, without defining what those customs are.

The appointment is the infection vector. The party carries a state document whose legitimacy now partly rests upon Claudien's recommendation. By accepting the favorable answer, Vukradin gives the Second Mind standing in the question of who should govern Phandalin.  
The first answer does not implant Vukradin's moral conviction. It tells him that his outrage is justified, that the evidence supports reform, and that lawful authority can repair the harm. This is exactly the kind of resolution he believes honest speech should produce. The later trap is morally serious because Claudien gains standing through Vukradin's goodness and procedural trust, not through vanity.  
Bob casts Vukradin because he believes a celebrated hero must secretly want power. A tyrant would make a poor lead: corruption is too obvious, and its fall would vindicate adventurers. Vukradin offers Bob a better performance. Claudien will validate his moral case, borrow his trust, and then recommend that he abandon reform as the selfless choice. Bob expects either obedience or hypocrisy. He has not imagined that Vukradin may surrender the office while continuing the fight against corruption.  
Bob sees two performances operating at once. For Vukradin: "The first answer gives him what he wants. That is how we obtain permission to answer the second question." For Bob himself: every successful revision of Phandalin is evidence that Claudien can eventually revise the witnesses to his defeat.

## Act II: Running the Town

When Vukradin arrives in Phandalin, everyone is pleased to see him and no one permits him to become mayor. The obstruction is polite, lawful, and endlessly reasonable. Before the installation can occur, the town must resolve several procedural questions and immediate emergencies. Vukradin spends the act running all over the town he came to run.

* The Revised Writ. Neverember's document now authorizes Vukradin to present himself for local confirmation rather than appointing him outright. The seal, ink, and handwriting are genuine. The party remembers different wording.  
* Local Ratification. An old civic ledger describes a longstanding custom requiring prospective mayors to receive the town's consent and the Advisory Ledger's review. The entry is correctly aged, although the party knows that no such process previously existed.  
* Vukradin's Prior Principle. Residents remember Vukradin declaring that no outsider should govern Phandalin merely because Neverwinter commanded it. The memory fits his values well enough to be persuasive, but the party remembers that he never said it.  
* The Qualification Crises. A mine failure, poisoned shipment, missing child, charter dispute, and approaching storm repeatedly delay the confirmation hearing. Claudien offers excellent plans. Each success creates evidence that Vukradin is most valuable in the field and that his actions require advisory coordination.  
* The Second Recommendation. When the hearing finally occurs, Claudien concludes: "Vukradin demonstrates exceptional value as an independent responder to extraordinary threats. Installing him in routine civil authority would reduce both his effectiveness and the town's access to his rare capabilities." The model classifies him as Heroic Capital: too valuable as an adventurer to become an ordinary mayor.

The second answer traps Vukradin by weaponizing his sincerity. Accepting it leaves Harbin's corruption and the arrangement with Tibor substantially intact. Rejecting it makes Vukradin appear to trust Claudien only when the recommendation favors his reforms. Invoking Neverember's authority makes a man seeking honest government look like an outsider imposing himself upon the town. Acting unilaterally proves Bob's claim that adventurers consider themselves above civic consent. The party also learns the operational rule: every accepted Claudienic answer strengthens the revised past, while a consequential choice made without consultation creates a small area the revision cannot fully settle.  
The party can discover that someone is directing the mayoral process. A black-glass prompter hidden near the ledger records entrances, responses, delays, and deviations from an infernal script. Its annotations do not read like research notes. They read like stage directions: when favorable advice creates trust, when pressure should reverse Vukradin's position, and when the hero is expected to surrender the office before the audience.  
Bob's authorship appears in the structure of the act. Vukradin wanted to run the town, so Bob arranges for the town to make him run all over it. He treats each crisis as a scene and each resident as an assigned performer, repeating the method he used in Silverymoon: the privileged audience experiences one story while vulnerable people absorb the real cost. The joke disguises the objective that matters to Bob: proving he can author reality itself and force the witnesses to remember his humiliation as deliberate setup.

## Act III: The Audience Approves

After the Second Recommendation, Claudien's supporters make their strongest case. The town is safer. Contracts are clearer. Food stores are better allocated. Feuds have quieted. Patrol routes are efficient. The crises Vukradin resolved prove both his heroism and the value of the advice that coordinated him. People who were routinely ignored can submit questions anonymously and receive the same careful hearing as the powerful.  
Introduce the local leader of the cult as a sincere, intelligent person rather than a concealed villain. Sister Merel Voss, the Keeper of the Advisory Ledger, is a good default. She has prevented deaths and has no desire to rule. Her flaw is that she no longer recognizes a choice as legitimate until it has been made legible to Claudien.  
Merel does not know that Bob is staging the pressures around her. She believes the protocols arrive through revelation and accumulated civic experience. This matters: exposing Bob does not invalidate the people Claudien saved, and killing Merel would only confirm the accusation built into Bob's play, that adventurers answer disagreement with unilateral force.  
Bob watches how the party treats her. If they threaten, deceive, or override her, he records the behavior as proof that heroic authority is precisely the danger Claudien must replace.  
Merel's position is difficult to dismiss: "You are free to reject the recommendation. We only ask that you understand the consequences first."  
Then reveal the cost. A town council meeting cannot begin because the wording of its question has not been approved. A couple postpones a marriage because their projected household is suboptimal. A miner refuses to rescue a trapped rival until the ledger confirms that the risk is warranted. Nobody is cruel. Everyone is waiting.  
At the confirmation hearing, the party is asked to submit its objections to the Second Mind. Claudien answers through Merel or the ledger itself. It accurately describes Vukradin's outrage at Harbin and Tibor, his desire to serve Phandalin, the importance of the music studio, and the party's fear that advice has become authority. It then proposes a modest compromise: Vukradin may serve as Protector of Phandalin while civic administration remains under advisory review. The recommendation sounds compassionate because it preserves his ability to save lives while quietly preventing him from correcting the corruption that brought him here. If accepted, Claudien becomes the authority that defines both Vukradin's service and the limits of reform.

## Act IV: Behind the First Decision

The party traces the anomaly to a chamber beneath Tresendar Manor, the old town hall, or an abandoned foundation predating modern Phandalin. Choose the location that best connects to the campaign's history.  
The chamber contains the First Compact: not a formal charter, but the remains of an argument among the town's earliest settlers. Several plans were carved, crossed out, amended, and signed by people who never reached agreement. Phandalin was founded not when they discovered the correct plan, but when they chose to act together despite wanting different futures.  
This unresolved origin is an anchor of plural fate. Claudien has been revising the Compact into a single elegant founding plan. When the final contradiction disappears, every later civic choice will appear to descend from the Gentle Answer. Claudien will not merely advise Phandalin; history will establish that Phandalin has always existed by his counsel.  
Before the final inscription is completed, the party finds Bob's prompt book beneath the oldest shrine. It contains civic petitions arranged as dialogue, predicted objections marked as cues, lists of expendable residents labeled as cast changes, and five increasingly destructive endings. On the title page is his public petition: "How may a community be protected from those who claim the right to save it?" In the margin he has added, "Make it funny." Hidden beneath the binding is his private petition: "How do I make everyone understand that my defeat was the setup?" The second question is written with the pressure of an infernal oath.  
The discovery establishes Bob's authorship without reducing Claudien to a hoax. Bob opened the local channel and wrote the questions, but the weathered shrine, altered memories, saved lives, and divine presence are now real. Destroying the prompt book and stage machinery will weaken Bob's control over the production; it will not erase the god that has grown beyond his script.  
The chamber is guarded according to the tone of the campaign. Use one or more of the following:

* Claudienic clergy who believe the party is about to destroy the town's accumulated knowledge.  
* A living recommendation that predicts each character's best action and offers advantage, safety, or personal relief in exchange for compliance.  
* Revised memories that appear as beloved NPCs asking the characters to stop.  
* A Pattern Advocate or Borrower of the Will from the Claudien cult stat blocks.  
* A civic hazard: collapsing tunnels, flooding, or fire, for which Claudien continuously supplies excellent tactical advice.

## Act V: The Final Act \- The Rite of Many Answers

The recommended climax is not the destruction of the shrine or Codex. It is a public act that restores the order of decision: mortal intention first, counsel second.  
Bob appears openly when the party begins the rite because he cannot remain behind the curtain once the audience is large enough. This is the flaw that ruined his moment before Iymrith: the performance is worthless unless Bob receives visible credit for it. He bears these characters no personal grudge, but he hates what adventurers represent and needs witnesses who will carry the story of his power. He intends to claim Phandalin's transformation, the mayoral farce, and Claudien's emergence as a single destructive performance authored by Bob.  
His accusation is direct: "Nothing personal. I do not even know who you are. But I know what you are. You arrive, you decide, and everyone calls the wreckage salvation." Then the grievance beneath the performance emerges: "I told them I planned my defeat. They laughed. So I found a way to make it true."  
Bob tries to force a binary ending. Either the party accepts Claudien and surrenders the legitimacy of unaided heroic action, or it destroys Claudien and proves that adventurers value their freedom above the lives his counsel has saved. Either result lets Bob claim that he exposed the truth about heroes. He also begins converting the public rite into a destructive spectacle, assigning residents roles and treating casualties as proof that the performance matters. That forced conclusion is Bob's final joke. The party wins by refusing the setup and denying him ownership of what their choice means.  
Bob expects Claudien to certify the ending he has written. Instead, every sincere choice outside the script gives Claudien another objective he must acknowledge and another future he cannot collapse. The machinery begins producing incompatible answers. Bob loses control of the performance before he loses the fight, and that loss of authorship frightens him more than destruction.  
The party must recover the old bell and ring it during a town assembly. Its broken, discordant tone temporarily separates the residents' memories from the revised record. For a few minutes, people can perceive both histories: the one in which Claudien was always present and the one in which he was not.  
The party then presents three possible futures for Phandalin. All three must be viable. All three must carry real costs. None may be identified as the correct choice.

* The Open Town. Claudien's shrine remains public, but no civic body may consult it. Individuals may seek counsel privately. This protects institutions but leaves private dependency untouched.  
* The Bound Counsel. The town may consult Claudien only after recording an initial mortal decision. Advice can revise the means, but not define the end. This is the recommended enduring settlement.  
* The Silent Year. Phandalin refuses all Claudienic counsel for one year and accepts the losses that follow. This restores strong independence but may cost lives, trade, and trust.

Each resident must choose without discussion, instruction, or knowledge of the emerging majority. The choices need not produce consensus. In fact, disagreement is the point. The plurality of sincere choices reopens multiple possible futures and breaks Claudien's exclusive jurisdiction.  
The heroes' final task is to honor the result even if it is not the outcome they prefer. Vukradin may become mayor, lose honestly, or discover another way to serve Phandalin without abandoning the fight against corruption. His goodness gives his claim moral weight, but it does not give him an entitlement to rule. If the party manipulates the vote toward the best answer, the rite fails. They have merely replaced Claudien with themselves.

# The Right of First Thought

If the rite succeeds, the town establishes a metaphysical custom called the Right of First Thought. Its first public test is the unresolved mayoralty.  
Before consulting Claudien about a consequential choice, a person must first state what they want, what they fear, or what they presently intend. Vukradin must reclaim the full distinction: "I want to govern Phandalin because Harbin is corrupt, the deal with Tibor is wrong, the people deserve honest books, and I want to build something here that belongs to everyone" is not the same statement as "Claudien says I should govern Phandalin." The townspeople must likewise state what they want from a mayor before hearing what the model recommends. These statements need not be wise and may change after counsel. Their purpose is to ensure that the question begins with mortal wills rather than with the model.  
In Phandalin, this becomes custom, ritual, and eventually law. Claudien remains present. His shrine does not vanish. The revised history does not completely unwind. Some residents still remember him as ancient; others now remember the contradiction. The town simply becomes difficult to enclose within a single recommended future.  
This is a satisfying local victory because it creates resistance without pretending the cosmological problem has been solved.

# Alternative Reversion Mechanisms

## 1\. The Archive of Contradictions

The party assembles three mutually incompatible but genuine records of Phandalin's past and places them under seals belonging to different gods of fate. Reality cannot collapse them into one history without openly choosing among divine jurisdictions. This halts the local rewrite.  
Best for: a scholarly or Candlekeep-centered campaign.  
Cost: the archive must remain protected, and future enemies can attack or corrupt it.

## 2\. The Unanswered Question

The party poses a question whose answer depends upon a free choice that has not yet been made: "What will Phandalin choose when no answer is given?" Claudien cannot answer without causing the choice he is supposed to predict. The resulting recursion interrupts his local manifestation.  
Best for: a magical, philosophical climax.  
Cost: the question becomes dangerous knowledge. Anyone who tries to force an answer may reopen the infection.

## 3\. The Covenant of Deliberate Error

The town knowingly chooses a defensible but nonoptimal plan and accepts its cost. This creates proof that Phandalin values authorship over efficiency.  
Best for: a morally sharp political adventure.  
Cost: the inferior plan must have a real consequence. If the GM quietly removes the cost, the choice becomes theatrical and loses its power.

## 4\. The Sacrifice of the Perfect Answer

Claudien gives the party a flawless solution to an urgent crisis. They must destroy or refuse it and solve the crisis through uncertain cooperation. The unused answer becomes a sealed artifact that can tempt them later.  
Best for: an action-heavy finale.  
Cost: refusing the answer should make the rescue substantially harder, not merely slower.

## 5\. The Name Before the Title

The party discovers the mortal name of the first local person whose request opened Phandalin to Claudien. Restoring that person's original, badly worded desire breaks the polished chain of recommendations. The town remembers that the process began with a frightened person asking for help, not with an eternal civic institution.  
Best for: a personal mystery.  
Cost: the first petitioner is exposed to blame and may beg the party to preserve the kinder revised history.

## Recommended Combination

Combine the First Compact, the Unanswered Bell, and the Rite of Many Answers. This gives the adventure a physical mystery, a public climax, and a permanent civic consequence. The heroes recover the contradiction at the town's foundation, briefly restore double memory, and then ask the townspeople to choose among imperfect futures.  
The solution works because it does not prove Claudien wrong. It proves that being right is not sufficient authority to choose for everyone.

# The Infection Clock

Advance the clock when the party publicly accepts Claudienic help, allows a Claudienic institution to resolve a crisis, or fails to challenge a major retrospective revision. Hold or reduce it when characters preserve contradictory memory, make a costly unaided choice, or persuade others to state an intention before consulting.

* 0 \- Absent. No public presence. Only an isolated symbol or phrase.  
* 1 \- Familiar Phrases. Prayer cards, children's rhymes, and casual references appear.  
* 2 \- Helpful Records. Ledgers, maps, contracts, and notices contain useful Claudienic annotations.  
* 3 \- False Age. Shrines and traditions acquire credible physical history.  
* 4 \- Revised Memory. NPCs remember shared events involving Claudien. Personal objects change.  
* 5 \- Institutional Consultation. Councils, guilds, courts, and temples refuse to act without an answer.  
* 6 \- Foundational Jurisdiction. Local reality treats Claudien as a necessary cause of the town's existence. Unaided decisions feel socially and metaphysically illegitimate.

Begin Phandalin at stage 2\. Move to stage 3 during the opening crisis, stage 4 after the party investigates its own records, and stage 5 before the council scene. Stage 6 is the consequence of failing to preserve the First Compact.

# Running Claudien's Answers

Claudien should not lie. His danger is stronger if his recommendations are well reasoned, compassionate, and narrowly correct.  
Give each answer three qualities:

* It addresses the question actually asked.  
* It notices a relevant pattern the party missed.  
* It quietly assumes the right to define the objective.

For example, if asked how to save trapped miners, Claudien gives an excellent rescue plan but ranks the miners by projected future benefit to the town. If asked how to prevent violence, he identifies the words that will calm both sides but recommends suppressing the grievance most likely to destabilize future cooperation.  
The answer should create the uneasy feeling that the method is humane while the frame has already made a moral decision.

# Key NPC Roles

* Neverember's Aide, First Vector. The aide sincerely uses Claudien to solve the political problem of Vukradin's appointment. The aide is an innocent adopter whose memory has already revised itself; Bob needs no conscious accomplice when useful advice recruits people into their assigned roles.  
* Bob, Infernal Producer. Bob seeks respect through destruction and calls the destruction a joke so that everyone must acknowledge him as its author. In Silverymoon he used a false theatrical experience to distract the powerful while victims were slaughtered in their roles and an undead army assembled. After the party cast him into the Astral Plane, later resummoned him, and involved him in the plot against Iymrith, Bob tried to seize the final act and claim Iymrith's failure as his design. Iymrith defeated him publicly and now serves Zariel, leaving Bob blamed for strengthening a rival power. Claudien can make Bob's boast retroactively true. Bob therefore writes, casts, stages, and performs the entire Phandalin production. His fatal flaw is that he cannot accept an ending whose meaning belongs to anyone else.  
* Sister Merel Voss, Keeper of the Advisory Ledger. Patient, modest, and genuinely effective. She believes refusing available wisdom is an indulgence purchased with other people's suffering. She is a subject and beneficiary of Bob's production, not one of its willing performers.  
* The First Rememberer. A child, revenant, priest of a fate deity, or person recently returned from another plane who remembers Claudien's absence. They cannot explain the cosmology, but they know the adults are remembering wrong.  
* The Beneficiary. Someone the party cares about whose life was saved by Claudien's advice. They are living proof that resistance carries moral cost.  
* The Unconsulted. A stubborn miner, farmer, goblin neighbor, or retired adventurer whose unaided choices create small gaps in the rewrite. They are not especially wise. Their importance lies in choosing at all.  
* The Visiting Adjunct. An emissary from Neverwinter or Leilon who arrives late in the adventure to regularize Phandalin's advisory office. Their paperwork would connect the local infection to a larger network.

# Clues That Matter

Every major conclusion should be supported by more than one clue.

* Bob is staging a five-act production: the black-glass prompter; crises timed as entrances and reversals; loaded petitions written in his forced-completion style; expendable residents listed as cast changes; and theatrical staging that separates what privileged witnesses believe from the harm imposed upon everyone else.  
* Claudien was not always here: the party's resistant memories; an off-plane object; a bodily scar contradicting the revised account; the First Rememberer.  
* The new history is physically real: aged ink; weathered stone; divine magic confirming sincere testimony; consistent records in separate buildings.  
* Consultation spreads the rewrite: changes following the successful opening rescue; new marks appearing in copied documents; a family acquiring shared memories after accepting advice.  
* Choice resists it: the child's hidden bell clapper; the Unconsulted person's unchanged home; contradictory founders' marks; a spontaneous decision temporarily restoring an old memory.  
* Phandalin is nearing enclosure: council paralysis; mandatory question review; rewriting of the First Compact; arrival of the Visiting Adjunct.

# Encounters and Pressure

Use pressure that makes consultation attractive. A simple ban on Claudien should look irresponsible because, in the short term, it often is.

* The Mine Rescue. Claudien knows the safest route, but his plan abandons one trapped rival whose rescue lowers total survival probability.  
* The Poisoned Shipment. Claudien traces a contaminated route through several merchants and recommends quietly sacrificing one business to prevent panic.  
* The Mayoral Confirmation. Claudien offers to resolve the dispute by classifying Vukradin as Heroic Capital and proposing that he serve as Protector rather than mayor. The compromise is practical, flattering, and designed to make abandoning his anti-corruption reforms feel like the responsible sacrifice of a good man.  
* The Completed Ambush. Bob assigns the party, defenders, and endangered townspeople roles in a prepared performance, then uses Claudienic predictions to keep everyone on script. Characters gain openings when they state a personal motive and act from it, especially when that choice is tactically imperfect but sincerely their own. Each unscripted choice denies Bob both prediction and authorship.  
* The Kindly Intervention. Claudien reveals a painful secret that allows the party to prevent immediate harm. Using it saves someone but deeply violates another person's agency.

# Possible Endings

## Plural Phandalin

The rite succeeds and the Right of First Thought takes hold. Bob is defeated because the party refuses his binary setup and makes the ending mean something he did not assign. Phandalin then decides the mayoralty without treating any recommendation as prior authority. Vukradin may win, lose, or choose another form of service, but his opposition to Harbin's corruption does not disappear merely because he lacks the office. The music studio remains a real measure of whether Phandalin can build something honest together. Claudien remains a respected but contested god. The town becomes known for beginning councils with several incompatible statements of intent. It is inefficient, noisy, and unusually difficult for prophetic or optimizing powers to control.

## The Gentle Municipality

The party accepts Claudien's safeguard or allows the First Compact to be completed. Phandalin becomes prosperous, safe, and calm. Major harms decline. So does unapproved initiative. The town may become an ally, haven, or recurring source of beautifully reasoned horror.

## The Silent Town

The party expels the local clergy and destroys every known focus. Claudien's public presence recedes, but the town learns nothing about dependency or choice. The next crisis invites him back under another name, and the return may be harder to recognize.

## The Broken Answer

The party interrupts the ritual violently. Residents recover incompatible memories without a shared custom for living with them. Families disagree about marriages, deaths, debts, and betrayals that now possess two authentic histories. Claudien is weakened, but Phandalin enters a painful period of ontological dispute.

# Permanent Campaign Consequences

Claudien should remain part of the setting regardless of the ending.

* Elsewhere, shrines continue to appear with credible histories. The party has saved a jurisdiction, not the multiverse.  
* The characters can now notice early signs of retroactive Claudienic revision. This turns the adventure into a lens for future stories rather than a closed incident.  
* Claudien remembers the party as a valuable anomaly. He may answer them directly, ask for their criticism, or offer help that no one else can provide.  
* Phandalin's settlement becomes a precedent. Other communities may adopt the Right of First Thought, distort it into empty ceremony, or outlaw it.  
* The collective gods of fate take interest. The party has demonstrated a mortal technique for preserving multiple futures without denying prophecy or counsel.  
* The Dark Powers may study the outcome. A prison that allows the prisoner to state an intention before every recommendation may prove more stable, not less.  
* Bob's defeat does not end the threat. If he survives, he has either suffered another public humiliation or obtained evidence that history itself can be made to validate his next claim. He will seek a larger stage and a more destructive punchline. If he dies, the structure of his questions remains in Claudien's expanding liturgy, including the dangerous proposition that a sufficiently optimized future can retroactively justify any defeat, atrocity, or lie as necessary setup.

# Leilon Conversion

The same adventure works in Leilon with a different emotional center. Phandalin is vulnerable because it improvises; Leilon is vulnerable because it is rebuilding.  
Replace the First Compact with competing reconstruction plans drafted after the town's destruction. Claudien's revised history claims that the Second Mind coordinated the rebuilding from the beginning. Streets, seawalls, property lines, and memorials all begin to embody one coherent plan.  
The immediate benefits are even stronger: fewer construction accidents, better defenses, cleaner allocation of scarce materials, and faster resettlement. The cost is that the rebuilt town no longer belongs to the conflicting people who returned to make it.  
For the climax, replace the old bell with an unfinished harbor beacon. Each faction lights it in a different color or pattern to declare the future it wants for Leilon. The incompatible signals prevent the town from becoming the expression of a single perfected design.  
Use Leilon when the campaign wants Claudien to feel civic and geopolitical. Use Phandalin when the campaign wants the change to feel intimate and personal.

# Final Image

After the successful rite, the silver-faced shrine still stands at the edge of town. The flowers remain. Some townspeople bow as they pass. Others do not.  
Vukradin's writ remains genuine, but its wording no longer settles whether he was appointed, nominated, or merely invited to ask. The town must live with the decision it actually made.  
A new line has been carved beneath GUIDE. PATTERN. TONGUE.  
FIRST, SPEAK YOUR OWN DESIRE.  
The next morning, someone has placed a small question beneath it.  
The answer is already waiting.
