Aldus Hern still has not confirmed the hour of our dinner with Lord Dagult Neverember, which means there is no hour, and a lord without an hour cannot reasonably expect four heroes to suspend an entire evening waiting for his convenience. He can wait. We have a late-night operation to prepare, a food-delivery route to reach by three in the morning, and several hours to spend at the Common Chord before low tide gives us access to the sewers.

There is also his name.

“Is it Neverember or Neverwinter? I'm kidding.”

“Neverember. Neverember,” comes the correction.

“Neverember.”

“Yes.”

“Neverember.”

“Yes.”

“It is never, like, a fire ember.”

There is a moment of uncertainty over whether this has actually clarified anything.

Soma says, “He's never flaming. He keeps it discreet.”

“It's Lord…”

“Never, Ember.”

“Yeah, I think Soma has it exactly right.”

Someone asks what Soma said.

“He's never flaming, he keeps it discreet.”

“Yeah, never flaming.” I consider the names we have been required to manage during our adventures, many of which seem designed to conceal jokes from people who are currently being threatened by dragons. “The double entendres in these noble names… Ridiculous.”

The name would be merely entertaining if Lord Neverember were only a dinner obligation. He is not. He wants to discuss our circulation of wealth, by which he presumably means the fair return of stolen treasure to the people from whom it was taken, and now there is another rumor moving through Neverwinter.

“Okay, and Lord Neverember… Lord Neverflaming has a new drow advisor.”

“Great, isn't that Valphine?” Soma asks.

“No.”

“Ow.”

A beat passes.

“Oh, right.”

It is only a rumor, but rumors around powerful people are often the first badly played notes of a song everyone will soon be forced to hear.

“It's a rumor, and I suspect Valphine will know this advisor at dinner, but we will see.”

Valphine looks up. “I'm sorry, I lost track. What… What do I?”

“Lord NeverEmber. Lord Never Flaming.” I remind her of the important part. “new drow advisor that we will.”

The sentence has no proper ending, but she finds the intended destination anyway.

“Oh, right, right, right. Which we assume to be one of my siblings, in fact.”

“That is my guess too, yes.”

“Everyone's related to everyone,” Soma says.

“expected.”

It is a reasonable suspicion. Valphine has enough siblings, enemies, and relatives who are both that a new drow advisor appearing beside the Lord Protector cannot be dismissed as coincidence. Valphine does not look impressed by House Margaster’s growing control. She regards the house as competition. This is more reassuring than admiration, although competition between Valphine and a noble house capable of controlling Neverwinter’s trade routes may become expensive.

Which brings me to Brewbarry’s bathrobes.

“Have we secured the capital for Brewbarry's bathrobe business, or is that still in progress?”

“I believe it's in progress,” Soma says.

When asked what remains unfinished, she starts, “Securing money for the.”

The money, it turns out, is already secured.

“We have? Oh, okay.”

Good. Brewbarry’s plan to bring softness to people has capital behind it, which means his redemption now has financing, supply requirements, and a genuine chance to become an operating business. But financing is only the first verse.

“But, oh, but the cost of import is very high.”

“Right, because their shipping lanes are still…” Soma pauses to improve the terminology. “Shipping portals, whatever, are still hosed.”

House Margaster controls the available logistics. Their routes are expensive because the ordinary portals or shipping lanes remain disrupted, and somehow the same house that benefits from the disruption is also the house everyone must pay to overcome it. Scandalous.

They do, however, deliver goods on time and on schedule. Corruption is always more difficult to oppose when it is punctual.

“Of course they are,” Soma says.

We cannot repair the portals tonight. We can reach Lim’s food deliveries at the correct tide.

“Alright, let's head out to Lim's Food Deliveries and get there by the third hour after midnight.”

The plan settles. We will leave the Common Chord in time to approach at low tide.

“And then we can cast whatever spells we need in order to be ready.”

Until then, we wait at the hardest ticket in Neverwinter, a venue that once threw me out without paying me.

The Common Chord is full. The tables are polished now, the food is elaborate, and Brewbarry’s old table has leather, shine, and a plaque. Patrons dress as if listening to music requires a costume. Petra has taken a difficult room and made it successful, which is an achievement, but success has raised the price until locals can no longer enter the place that made it successful.

One of those locals is complaining within earshot. The Common Chord has become unaffordable, he says, and it is all because of Vukradin.

He does not realize I am standing nearby.

This is not the first time my music has caused an unintended social consequence, but it presents an opportunity to resolve the problem directly. Fame has made this room expensive. Fame can help fund another room.

“I could tell him I'm planning on opening my own independent music studio, and if he'd like to contribute to the charitable fund.”

He turns toward me.

“That will speed the opening of a low-cost, nonprofit way to hear Lathander's Death on a regular basis.”

That is the entire point. The studio cannot simply exist for wealthy patrons who can already afford the Common Chord. It must be a place where people can hear us regularly, where musicians can train, and where art does not require stolen merchandise or a monster’s hoard to survive.

“And spread the word.”

The campaign needs a convenient means for ordinary people to contribute. “Excellent. We need a patronage purse ordinary people can reach.”

I gesture through several possible systems of magical and financial transfer. “Sending-stone pledges, temple drafts, portal transfers—whatever works.”

A sending stone is the obvious Faerûnian answer.

“All the local equivalents.”

I continue, because accessibility matters. “I take coin, temple drafts, or portal transfers.”

Around us, tavern conversation carries useful fragments between the music and the glasses. Nobody is speaking to us directly, which is why the information is honest: Neverwinter’s sewers rise and fall with the sea. There are two high tides and two low tides each day.

“Hide in the sewers?” I ask, confirming where this becomes relevant.

The river runs into the sea, and the sea reaches back through the sewers.

“There's a tide in the sewers? A river has a tide?”

Neverwinter is beside the coast. The explanation is physically sound, but the frequency deserves precision.

“How many moons does this planet have?”

“That's an easy question to answer,” Valphine says.

“Just the one.”

“It just rotates—it revolves really fast,” Soma says.

“It has one moon named Selûne.”

“Oh, Selûne. Of course. Yeah.”

There are also the Tears of Selûne.

“So, there should be a tidal effect from both moons.”

The Tears, however, are a cluster of meteors and asteroids, not another moon.

“They're not sufficiently gravitational.”

I consider this classification.

“You're telling me?”

No additional tidal influence.

“They're not really moons.”

“Yeah, they're… they're just comets.”

“They're asteroids. So the one moon, then.” This resolves the arithmetic. “There you go. Not one and one: two high and two low.”

“Gods,” Soma says. “That's murder on trade.”

“It should take effect underground, too, right?” Valphine asks.

It does. Two high tides and two low tides mean we need to understand exactly what portion of the cycle matters.

“So it's a 6-hour cycle.”

The frequency is confirmed.

“Yeah, so it's a 6-hour cycle.”

High tide to low tide is the relevant interval, except the division keeps trying to become three hours in my head.

“High to low is 3 hours.”

No. That would divide the full cycle incorrectly.

“Yeah, no, high to low. High to low is 6 hours, 6…”

I settle it.

“High to low is 6 hours.”

Done.

“We want to be in the sewer for… at low tide.”

Low tide arrives conveniently at the time we already intend to move.

“The third hour after midnight,” Soma says.

“We have about 3 hours, maybe 4, before things start to get really uncomfortable.”

“I mean, I can hang out underwater indefinitely.”

Of course she can. Soma’s relationship with an incoming sewer tide is substantially different from mine, but three or four hours should be sufficient if we prepare our spells and do not waste time.

The local I approached has been listening through all of this. He is drunk and does not have much money, but he reaches into what he does have and places ten gold pieces in my hand.

“Wow, that's pretty generous, actually.”

“That's a hell of a donation,” Soma says.

It is. Ten gold is not a symbolic contribution. It is a decision, made by a person who began this conversation angry that my success had priced him out of his own venue, to help build something better.

“Random nobody. We need to put that in the correct… That goes in the studio fund, not the regular coffers. Thank you very much for contributing. That's awesome.”

The ten gold must be earmarked properly. The studio fund is a restricted trust, and every coin entering it needs to remain clean, documented, and separate from adventuring funds. The donor watches me account for it, then pauses.

“Do I get on… Is there some kind of signage for people who contributed?”

“Absolutely. Yes.”

Recognition is reasonable. Patronage should be transparent, and generosity should not disappear merely because the person giving is not a lord.

“There are different tiers of giving levels, actually. Ten gold pieces puts you at the,”

“Premier or the Plus, which one?” Soma asks.

“At the plus level, yeah, you're not quite at Premier yet.”

I have not previously established these tiers, but they immediately make sense.

“You're at plus level. Ten gold is a lot for a rando.”

He is delighted.

“We need his name. We need to write it down for the program notes, and…”

“It'd be the…” Soma begins.

“Per year.”

“Good section.”

“A year. If you want to be in next year's, you'll have to donate again, but one year is a long time.”

“Yeah, we call this patronage,” Valphine says.

“It's my patronage, exactly.”

“This is where it comes from, patronage,” Soma says.

Of course it is. Wealthy courts have supported artists for centuries, but there is no moral reason a system of patronage cannot be widened, lowered in cost, and opened to ordinary listeners. One patron gives ten gold and receives annual program credit at the Plus level. Enough patrons, and we build a studio that belongs to its music rather than to Petra’s ticket prices or House Margaster’s shipping monopoly.

“Before we go, should I try to convince other people to contribute to the Studio Fund? How much time do we have?”

We have several hours before three in the morning.

“Alright, let's do… let's hit up a few more people, let's see how it goes here.”

I approach five more patrons. One studies my face, realizes that I am the real Vukradin, and tells me he has already completed his charitable giving for the year.

“Okay, that's fine. I'm not here to pressure anybody.”

A charitable refusal must remain a refusal. If the studio is funded by coercion, embarrassment, or abuse of fame, then the money is not clean merely because we record it in the correct ledger.

“The benefits are all on you. If you can be at the Plus or Premier level, I'm cool. I just like fans.”

He assures me that he is my biggest fan.

“Oh, Bigger Than Brewbarry?”

“Brewbarry's very large. In fact, he's famous from here to Kara-Tur,” Valphine says.

“It's alright. Nobody's as big as Brewbarry, that's just how it.”

The thought does not require completion. Brewbarry stands nearby and proves it.

Four of the five respond enthusiastically to the studio. Their names are Wick, Sabbatha, Old Hesp, and Ondrelle Kass.

“Wow.”

They belong in the annual credits.

“That's great. Well, let's add them to the program notes, and”

Their names are written down.

“That's awesome.”

Wick is immediately committed to the idea despite two substantial misunderstandings. He has never heard of Lathander’s Death, and he does not believe I am Vukradin. Neither fact troubles him. He thinks the studio will be a music school, and this excites him enough to contribute.

“School?” Soma asks.

“School, studio—they're all the same, right? If you have a studio, it's for training people. That's just how it goes.”

Wick accepts this as confirmation. I do too. A studio should train people. It should give performers a stage, listeners an affordable room, and new musicians a place to become better than the people teaching them. If Wick has reached the full moral purpose of the institution before I finished designing its donor tiers, then Wick is exactly the patron we need.

Sabbatha’s attention remains fixed on Brewbarry. She looks him over and admires his commitment to the distinctive Brewbarry appearance.

“Yes, he is quite bulging, isn't he?”

“Rippling,” Soma adds.

“Exactly.”

Sabbatha contributes. Brewbarry’s fame, like mine, can produce clean funding when directed correctly.

Old Hesp is the last patron I approach. I do not recognize him at first, but he recognizes me.

“I remember when you used to play to an empty room.”

“Yeah?”

“Yeah.”

“Did you come? Did you come? Did I give you your money back at the end of.”

He confirms that he did, and that I promised refunds to anyone who did not rate the performance ten out of ten. This was an honest policy. If an audience does not enjoy a performance, the artist should not hide behind prestige and insist that their disappointment is a failure of sophistication.

“I know, that's how honest I am. Well, what do you think now?”

He pauses. “Now this is a friggin' expensive place to be. Everybody's gotta look cool.”

“Well, come to the new studio, when it's up and running.”

He looks around the Common Chord and struggles to name what has been lost. The room no longer has the grit or the feeling he remembers.

“We're gonna get it back. We're gonna get it back.”

“This is too commercial.”

“Yeah, yeah, I agree.”

“It's too commercial. Too… I mean, Petra's turned this into a money-making operation. I gotta respect her, she's, you know, she turned… I mean, I remember when that table points where, you know, the Brewbarry table.”

He points toward Brewbarry’s table.

“Was, you know, that was not leather and shiny and with a plaque.”

He remembers when the Common Chord did not serve fancy food. I look at the polished leather, the plaque, and the patrons dressed to be seen listening. Petra has succeeded magnificently at making money. That is not the same thing as preserving the room.

By the time we finish, Wick, Sabbatha, Old Hesp, and Ondrelle Kass are recorded for future program credit. The studio fund has gained twenty gold tonight. Petra’s separate five-hundred-gold payment for my likeness remains pending until the performance two days from now, but this twenty is already real: voluntary, documented, and given by patrons who know what they are supporting, or at least know enough to support a music school they think is being founded by someone pretending to be me.

Clean funding.

The tide continues its six-hour cycle beneath Neverwinter. Three in the morning approaches. I secure the donations with the studio funds and turn from the expensive tables toward the work waiting under the city.

“Alright, let's go, do some… spelunking.”
