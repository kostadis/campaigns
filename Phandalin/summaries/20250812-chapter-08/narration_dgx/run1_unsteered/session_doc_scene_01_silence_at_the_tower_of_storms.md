The harpy sang. That's the whole of our interrupted business — one note, hanging in the air like a question nobody wanted to answer, and a couple of my companions went glassy-eyed and forgot themselves for a beat. Soma said it plainly enough: "And a couple people got charmed." But then the correction came, quick and almost apologetic: "Oh, no, it's only one." Just Harpy one. One voice, one song, and it had nearly unraveled us.

I didn't need to hear the rest. I knew what to do.

"Like I said, it's not going to matter. Silence." I pulled the magic from my lute and let it settle around us, a 20-foot pocket of nothing. No birdsong, no wind, no breathing. Just the absence of sound, thick as wool. "That should eliminate the charm condition." I placed the radius on the screen for everyone to see — precise, geometric, the way I like these things. "I put the 20-foot radius on the screen there for you."

Soma confirmed it, her voice muffled through the barrier: "Everyone is deafened inside the circle."

Good. That was the point. That was always the point.

"She can sing. She can sing all she wants. We can't hear her."

And the GM nodded along, finally clearing the condition from the board: "All right. So you are no longer affected by the charm."

I let myself feel that small vindication. "We can delete charmed. As I said, it wasn't going to matter." Because it wasn't. Not with me here. Not with my music turned against the noise of the world.

The harpy, the one that had been singing, flapped there for a moment, confused, furious, its song falling into the void. Then it turned and retreated, a blur of feathers over the parapet. "Ran away. Okay. He ran away." And the GM confirmed it: yes, gone, over the edge, frustrated. As it should be. A singer with no audience is just a noise. And I had stolen its audience.

Soma, though, she wasn't content to watch it flee. She rumbled low in her shell and then said, "Or I could go into spider mode. That'd probably be fine too." She asked for a spider icon to place on the board, explaining as she did, "Well, I think technically you're only supposed to have like ones that you're familiar with, right?" Limiting herself. Disciplined. Even in the middle of a fight, she kept her shapes to the ones she knew — wolf spider, bear, the forms that made sense to her. That kind of restraint was rare, and I noted it.

But before she climbed, the GM clarified a crucial detail about the charm. "So just to be clear, the folks who made the saving through originally are immune to the harpy song for 24 hours. The other folks are not."

I perked up. That changed things. "Oh, so I don't have to worry about hiding in the immune to all harpy songs or just the harpy song from Harpy?"

"Immune to all harpy songs," he said.

"Oh, wow."

And then, quick correction, even as I was basking: "No, no, no, sorry, to this harpies, not all, not all. So if the other harpy sings, they might, you have to re-roll."

Right. Of course. Not a free pass — just a reprieve. The world never hands you a clean chord; it always leaves a dissonance somewhere.

Soma, meanwhile, was already moving. She gestured at the tower wall. "I mean, in theory, I could probably scale this wall next to number two to help get into range, right?"

"Like, yeah. Yes."

"I mean, the spider is climby crazy. So let's just say I climb the wall then. So wherever it is, I'm on top of it."

She shifted into that long-legged form, all angles and patience, and began to climb — straight up the exterior, unbothered by the stone, settling herself right at the harpy's level. On top of it, like she said. "Whatever. I'm, you know, I'll be me. I don't know, hold on. We got some of the wolf spider. Trying to limit the shapes I use."

Then the bite. A sharp, powerful clamp, and she called it: "That's a hit."

"Nice. 11 damage."

"And yeah, yep. On harpy number two."

I read the number off the board and blinked. "Wow, these have a lot of hit points."

"Wow," Soma echoed.

You're not kidding, someone else muttered.

So they weren't glass. Fine. We'd just have to work for it.

Soma's spider clung to the wall, and I confirmed the geometry for her sake: "10 feet. Okay." The GM confirmed she was still inside the sphere — safe, silent, deaf to any song. Good. Let her bite in peace.

Brewbarry, though, had fire in him. He moved up, measuring the distance for his halberd — "So Brewbarry can make it to right here, and then it's just enough range for the halberd" — and then remembered the battle rage building in his chest. "And then with rage. And rage, yep." He rolled the attack.

"11."

I leaned in. "Oh, does it 10 misses?"

"10 misses. Yeah. All right. So I guess that's it for me. Yikes."

A miss. A ten against an eleven. So close it was almost a hit, and so far it was nothing at all. I watched Brewbarry's frustration flicker across his face, that barely-contained storm, and filed it away. We'd need him to connect eventually.

The harpy — number two, the one Soma had bitten — didn't wait for us to find our rhythm. It peeled away, wings beating backward, and the GM announced, "Harpy 2 disengages."

"Really?" I asked, surprised.

"Yes. Well, I mean, the harpies, harpies like to charm their enemies, not they literally know they cannot do it. Yeah. I think that's it's kind of like, you know, silent spells are very effective at dissuading harpies from attacking you."

Silence. My silence. Chasing them off like a bad note. I couldn't help the small, private satisfaction. "This is where ranged attacks would be useful," the GM added, and Soma agreed — "Yeah, clearly we're going to need ranged" — a truth settling over the table.

Valphine shifted, checking her options. "I believe I have a range spell. And let's see what this is."

I had to stop her before she fumbled. "You can't cast the spell with a verbal component."

"I know. Let's see if I can step outside and then come back in or something."

"No, no, hold on, hold on," the GM interrupted. "At the end of each of its turns, right, at the end. Okay."

She reconsidered, then settled on the practical: "So I can shoot it with an arrow, I guess."

"Yeah, you can definitely do. Nice. Ah, crit."

"Critical damage even. 4d6."

The GM asked who her target was, and she didn't hesitate. "Whoever. Can I see the one who was trying to charm me?"

"Yes, this is number three."

"Number three. Yes. Number three would be the one I would attack."

The bolt struck true — a solid, devastating hit. The harpy reeled, forced to roll a concentration save. "Okay, so I have to roll a concentration spell. He makes a concentration, but takes expensive damage." Not enough to break its focus, but enough to hurt. Enough to matter.

Then came the charm saves, at the end of the turns. Valphine remembered Brewbarry was in the line of fire too, and the GM confirmed both made it. "Yes. Yep. Yes, so you're both now no longer charmed. You're now no longer affected by the charm of that of Harpy number three."

And there it was — the rhythm of the fight, finally clear to me. "Which means you can step out of the silence, attack, and go back in. As long as another harpy doesn't start singing."

The peek-and-shoot. It was almost musical — a phrase, a rest, a phrase again. The silence was our home base, and the danger lived just outside its edge, waiting for the unwary.

I couldn't resist. "So I can probably go like out here, cast a spell against Harpy 3, and then run back inside, correct?"

"Yep."

"Okay, so I'll do that."

The GM checked my range. "Yes. 60 foot range, right?"

"Yeah, I mean, it's only 10 foot away, so yeah."

I stepped out of the sphere, felt the world rush back in — wind, feathers, the distant crash of waves — and loosed my magic at Harpy 3. It struck, and the GM confirmed: "So 60-foot range. So four damage on three."

"It's down to 28."

Then I ran. Back inside the silence, where the songs couldn't reach me and the world went quiet and safe again. My pulse hammered in my ears, my own heartbeat the only music in that pocket of nothing.

And that was the fight, in its first full pass. Harpies who couldn't sing their lies, a spider on the wall, a crossbow bolt finding its mark, and me — darting out, striking, retreating, holding the silent line. It wasn't glamorous. But it was working.
