The rough-hewn stairs climb the eastern face of the rock, and I take them two at a time. Eighty feet above the water, the plateau opens out, and there they are — wooden doors with rusty iron hinges and decorative lightning bolts carved into them. Rusty iron hinges. I say it out loud because it's the kind of detail that makes a place honest — this tower has been here a long time, and it has not been maintained by anyone who cared about it.

"Can I go open the door?"

"Can you open the door?" the DM asks, which I choose to read as permission.

So we go in. The harpies have stopped singing — that's one way to put it. I take the euphemism literally because the alternative is to think about what we did to them on that plateau, and I'd rather focus on the door. The room beyond is fifteen feet high and empty, with a dirty window in the south wall. I press my face to the grime and there they are: wrecks. Ships broken on the rocks below, timbers splayed like ribs. "Oh, yes. The what the harpies have been doing to the local navy. Yes."

And I see it immediately. "So many possessions that must be returned to their rightful owners."

"Who are dead underwater?" the DM says, and I can hear the eye-roll in his voice.

"It's, you know, what is it? They're heirs. Their successors. Can't just take things from them. Therefore, they died to these evil harpies."

"Oh, God," he says, and I take that as agreement.

The next room is what I came here for. The walls are adorned with frescoes — ships tossed on stormy seas with a dark and terrible god looming above them. "Roll a religion check," the DM says, and I roll, and it fails. "Anybody else?" I ask. Soma asks too. "Our cleric's not very religious, is she?" she mutters, which is a fair point. We all roll. We all fail. "Wow. Nope. We got nothing tonight."

"Well, what you got is it looks pretty," Soma says. "Pretty paintings. It's good art. Good art."

"Very nice," I say, because it is. The craftsmanship is genuine — you can tell the artist believed in what they were painting, even if what they believed in was a dark god looming over drowning sailors. "Is it frescoed onto the stone, or is it like a piece of art?"

"He said fresco, right?" Soma says. "It's fresco."

"I'd like something like this at the academy," she adds, and I can picture it — a section of Talos-worshipping wall mounted in a druid academy, students walking past it every day. It has a certain chaotic energy.

"You want to chisel out a section of wall and bring it along?"

"I could mold earth it out, right?"

"I don't think mold earth is going to be sufficient here."

"Ye have little faith."

I have plenty of faith. I just have it in the right things — in returning what isn't mine, in fair trade gold, in the principle that a man's possessions follow him to his grave only if his heirs get them. The fresco is a different question. It's art, and art should be preserved. But we're not here for the art. We're here to lift a curse.

The south wall has a stone altar carved with lightning bolts, and above it a metal rod descends from the ceiling, splitting in two before it embeds itself into the stone. A lightning rod. "That's like a metal lightning bolt or something," Soma says, and I nod. "The lightning rod, yeah."

"Nice. Okay. Well, do we like want to interact the altar in any way? I mean, feel free, guys. Go right on."

Gary — the player, not a character — checks the landing first, and that's where we find it. "A ledge enclosed by three-foot-high stone retaining wall serves as a nest," the DM says.

"Oh, the harpy nest? Oh, this is what you wanted to search, Valphine. Can we like torch it?"

"That's a good idea," the DM says. "I mean, it stinks. It does stay. It does smell."

"I mean, it smells like napalm," Soma says.

"I think that's a great idea," I say, because I can smell it from here — the rot, the salt, the feathers, the accumulated filth of creatures who spent their time luring sailors to their deaths. "I'll torch it right now. I'm not even going to search it."

"I'm just going to get out of there while you torch it," Soma says. "I think that's a fantastic idea."

But Valphine searches first — "I am blowing all my rolls to search it" — and she is, spectacularly. "You're just blowing all your rules tonight," Soma tells her. But the search doesn't matter, because "the good news is you didn't have to blow them very hard because the potion of water breathing was quite obvious."

A potion of water breathing. In a harpy nest. Which means a sailor had it on them when they drowned. "Okay, well, we'll need to just determine. Maybe we can use like a legend lore spell to determine who owned this potion of water breathing. We'll just add that to blood money. Consumables."

"That's quite a moral code you got there," Soma says.

"But it's very confused about it," the DM says.

"I'm confused," I mishear.

"No, I said everybody."

"Everybody else is confused. I'm quite confused."

The DM sets it out for us like a map. "You have to understand, you are engaged in some very revolutionary behavior against the overall world order of this realms."

"Yeah, this is a libertarian paradise, right?" Soma says. "Like, just take what you want by force."

"No, I wouldn't say it's a libertarian paradise. It's a liberal, it's not, no, it's, I would say a colonial paradise."

"Colonial. Mercantile. Yes." Of course it is. There are people who count as civilized and people who don't, and the ones who don't — sailors drowned by harpies, orcs killed by ogres, anyone whose possessions end up in a monster's nest — their things just become available. The system has a word for it. "Well, more blood loot. Typical."

"The Order of the Gauntlet is going to be so pleased to have you on their side," the DM says, and I reject it without thinking. "I don't trust those guys." He says he didn't say it — that they're very pleased to have me — but I heard what he meant. The Gauntlet likes order, and what I'm doing — returning things to heirs, refusing plunder, telling people that treasure belongs to the ones it was taken from — that's not order. That's revolution. They'll be pleased until they realize what I actually stand for, and then they won't be.

"Let's lift the curse on this tower of storms," I say.

"Oh, yeah," Soma says. "It might actually be something with that altar, huh?"

Maybe. But I know what lured the ships here. We all saw the harpies on that plateau. "But the point of coming here was to liberate the castle, not just get the treasure, right? We're trying to liberate the castle. This is what is luring all the ships to be smashed. So we have a moral obligation to defeat the harpies."

"Oh, right," the DM says, and I can hear him ready to tip his hand. "Actually, it's the green light, not the harpies."

"Sorry?" Soma says. "We don't know that, do we?"

"Yeah, you do, because what's his name? That's like the giant crab tool. Well, I guess you don't know."

"I don't think Crabby McGee told us that," Soma says, and she's right. The big crab — the thing we talked to down by the water — it told us about the tower, but it never once mentioned a green light. We defeated the harpies because we saw them, because they sang and lured ships to their death, because it was the right thing to do. If there's something else in this tower that draws ships to wreck — some light, some beacon — we haven't met it yet and we don't know its name.

"I don't know. You're right," the DM concedes.

The harpies are dead. The nest is about to burn. And somewhere in this tower, there's something I don't know about yet — something that may be far worse than the creatures we already fought. One thing at a time, I tell myself. One song at a time. The nest first. Then the curse. Then we'll find out about the green light.
