The silence fell like a curtain yanked shut. One moment the air was thick with that awful, searching melody — the kind that hooks into the base of the skull and pulls — and the next there was nothing but wind and the wet sound of a spider's mandibles working.

Soma had done it. Twelve points of damage through her bite, and the last singer's concentration shattered. I watched the harpy's throat convulse, the song dying in it.

"Brewbarry, you're free." Vukradin's voice cut through the quiet. "Hooray. Brewbarry can actually attack. But can he hit?"

I appreciated the honesty of that question. Charm broken, feet unstuck, and still the goliath stood there with the particular uncertainty of a man who has just been told the floor is safe to walk on after all. He had been, by his own account, the most useless thing on the field. The sting of that would linger longer than the song.

"Yeah, that's the key question," Brewbarry said, and I heard the grim resolve in it.

The harpy that had been singing — Harpy One, the one that had punished Soma's spider form with that glancing strike — still fluttered near the plateau's edge. Soma had taken the hit cleanly. I had heard the exchange from across the rocks: "16." "Oh, yeah." "That's just enough to hit." "I'm at AC16." The claws had stripped away her temporary ward and bitten into the flesh beneath, dropping her to 24. She had not flinched. Tortles are admirably economical with their pain responses.

"I see no reason to move or disengage," Soma said, her spider legs planted firm. "I'm going to see this harpy to the end."

That was the correct tactical read. The creature was wounded, its song broken, its options collapsing. A retreat would only give it air to recover, and this plateau offered no other exits. The end she spoke of was a foregone conclusion wearing feathers.

Brewbarry hefted his halberd, testing the reach. "So can I hit from here with my halberd?"

The reach was generous — the weapon's whole virtue. "Yes, you can," the DM confirmed, and I watched the goliath square his stance, the rage settling back into his shoulders like a familiar coat.

The halberd came around in a wide, brutal arc. I did not need to see the impact to know it. I heard it — the wet, final sound of metal finding what metal is for.

"That is a hit," Vukradin said, reading the result with the flat certainty of a man watching a door close. "That is a kill."

One stroke. The creature that had tormented us with its voice, that had held Brewbarry's feet to the stone, dropped from the air like a sack of stones. Death by halberd is not elegant. It is sufficient, which is a higher virtue.

And that left Harpy Three. The one I had been attending to before the chaos pulled me elsewhere. I had been denied my moment with it once already — the mace strike that had thudded home for three paltry points, the crossbow bolt that had punched through its wing on the way down. "One third of the way down," I had noted at the time, cataloging its descent for future reference.

Now I had my shot again. I raised my mace, sighted, and brought it down on the creature's skull with all the precise, indifferent force I could muster.

The impact was clean. The crack of bone was audible.

"Crit for four hit points."

The words came out flat, almost disappointed. Four points. A critical hit, and that was all the Morninglord saw fit to grant me. The creature reeled but did not fall.

"Four damage," Vukradin said, and there was a question hiding in his tone.

"Nice," I said, because I am a woman who takes what she is given. "Well, you know, like it's getting there."

It was getting there. That was the truth of it, however unsatisfying. A lifetime of Menzoberranzan training had taught me that victories are assembled from increments, that the grand strokes are only the final payment on a debt of small ones. Four points was a coin in the jar. It would spend.

Soma was already moving, her spider legs scuttling across the rock. "Yeah, I can clear that, right?" A pause, measuring the gap. "Yeah, no, I can get in there for sure. I got 40-range movement. Let's go."

"Chompy chompy," Vukradin said, which I chose to interpret as encouragement.

"Roll." A beat. "And I whiff. But at least I'm in the fear in the fray now."

There was something almost endearing about it. The great druid, spider-shaped, lunging at the last harpy and coming away with nothing but air and determination. Still, she had committed. She was in reach. That mattered more than the miss.

Vukradin turned to me, an idea forming behind his eyes. "You want me to drop the silent spell?"

The zone of silence had been a tactical necessity — it had kept the harpies' songs from reaching us, yes, but it had also muzzled my own magic. I had felt the constraint every time I reached for Lathander's light and found only the hollow echo of my own voice.

Brewbarry, still standing in the charmed corpse's shadow, grunted. "Well, I mean, I'm in mealy now."

"Yeah, but would you be better off with a spell attack?" Vukradin pressed, and the question was aimed at me as much as at the goliath.

"Probably," I said. The mace was incremental. The light was not.

"All right, let's drop the silence." The words were a release. "You can use a spell."

The silence lifted like a blanket pulled off a cage. The wind returned. My voice returned. And with it, the reach of my god.

I reached for the Sacred Flame, the radiant, indifferent fire that Lathander lends to those who understand what his light is actually for. The harpy was wounded, reeling, its last defenses stripped. I did not need to hit it hard. I needed to hit it at all.

"That's a Dex save for four damage," Vukradin said, parsing the result. "Really? I could have."

The flame licked at the creature's feathers, scorching, searing — four more points off its already skeletal reserves. The words of my companions washed over me: "Given the mace that, hey, don't knock it," from Soma, defending the incremental grind; "Four damage," from Vukradin, tracking the arithmetic; "It's half his hit points," from Soma again, finding the silver lining in a number that should have been higher.

Half its hit points. There it was. The coin in the jar had become half the debt.

Vukradin straightened, a particular smile crossing his face — the smile of a man who has found the perfect instrument. "This is when we use Vicious Mockery."

Of course. The bard's weapon was not steel or flame but the cruel precision of language. And against a creature whose entire existence was voice, what could be more fitting than to kill it with words?

Soma described it before it happened: "He's just taunting him on the way down or on the way down." A brutal poetry in that phrasing.

"Pretty much," Vukradin agreed.

The insult came — something about elderberries, something about a save against death itself. Soma supplied the particulars: "Yeah, it smells of elderberry. 13 save for your life. Or four hit points. That's here."

Four hit points. The same number I had carved from the creature with my critical blow. The same number that had felt so pitifully small when I landed it.

"He is mocked to death," Vukradin said, and the finality in his voice was absolute.

The harpy fell. Its song — such as it was — ended not with a shriek but with the ignominy of being out-insulted by a bard with a grudge against its vocal range. The creature that had enchanted us, held us, nearly fed on us, dispatched by the cruelest joke a dead feather could hear.

"That Yo Mama joke really cut deep," Soma said, and I found myself appreciating the absurdity of it. A dozen blades and bolts and bites had failed to end this fight cleanly. What finished it was a punchline.

I stood there in the wind, mace still in hand, watching the bodies settle on the rocks. Three harpies. One mocked to death, one halved to death, one — mine — chipped away at until it was someone else's kill entirely.

Vukradin eulogized them, because of course he did. "And also, I don't like harpies singing. Their music is just they're not joining the academy, that's for sure."

I could not help the small, sharp exhale that was almost a laugh. The harpies had nearly killed us with their voices, and the bard's final judgment was a review. An audition rejection. There was something deeply, structurally correct about that — the professional's dismissal of the amateur's racket.

"We'll no longer taunt them," someone said — I did not catch who, the wind was pulling at the words. "We did a good thing here today."

Did we? I examined the thought as I would a gem held to the light. Three dead creatures who had preyed on sailors and travelers, whose songs were traps, whose appetites had likely ended more than one ship's crew. Yes. A good thing, in the way that removing a tumor is a good thing — necessary, clean, and requiring a certain indifference to what is cut away.

Brewbarry was already thinking ahead, his mind having moved past the violence to its logistics. "I mean, I would like to retrieve my hand axes."

"Might want to get more of those," Vukradin said, and there was a genuine note of counsel in it. "Maybe, maybe. Yeah."

"I have a whole armory of hand axes available," Brewbarry said, which I interpreted as both boast and promise.

The DM's voice cut through the planning with the flat authority of a rule being laid down: "You do not have infinite hand axes."

"Oh." Vukradin's surprise was almost comical. "Oh, not like arrows?"

"No, because arrows you can retrieve 50%, and given that most combats have at most 20 rounds, unless you're going out into the wilderness, I'm assuming that you can have 40 arrows and that's it." The explanation came in a rush, a DM defending a house rule. "Okay, so. I mean, obviously, if you go into a large combat where you're killing hundreds of creatures, but then those combats don't exist in this variation of the game."

There it was. The universe, even at the table, imposing limits. No infinite hand axes. Arrows capped and partially recoverable. The fantasy of endless ammunition shattered by the same realism that had cost me four points on a critical hit.

I had spent a lifetime learning to work within limits. Lolth's blessings rationed, hidden, finite. Lathander's inexhaustible, but only when I remembered to reach for it. The hand axes would be found or they would not. The arrows would return or they would not. What mattered was the next strike, and the one after that.

Brewbarry proposed what I had been about to suggest myself. "Short rest? Yeah, and search the bodies."

"Yes," I said, not loudly, but firmly enough to be heard over the wind. My hand was already moving toward my mace, checking its edge. The weapon had served. The god had served. The fight was over.

"Well, then I will search for my hand axes," Brewbarry said, and there was a note of near-grief in it — the concern of a craftsman for his tools, thrown poorly and scattered wide.

"All right, you can grab your hand axes again," the DM said, with a mercy that surprised me. "They're like a few feet away from where you threw them because you threw so badly. Yeah."

I watched Brewbarry scoop his axes from the dirt, examining them with a tenderness that bordered on paternal. "They narrowly missed my own toes," he said, and there was wonder in it. "Okay."

"You're so used to using the halberd action that you kind of threw them like you were using halberds," the DM explained, and I filed that observation away. The mechanics of violence leave impressions on the body. Muscle memory betrays intention. The halberd's weight had corrupted the hand axe's flight, and the goliath had nearly paid a toe for it.

The bodies lay where they had fallen. Three harpies, silenced. We had saved the ships. We had cleared the plateau. The threat was ended, and the wind was already beginning to erase the marks of the battle from the rock.

A short rest was called. Thirty minutes, Vukradin ruled, and no one argued. I found a patch of stone still warm from the sun and sat, letting the weariness settle into my bones.

"And his short rest is more than an hour, right?" Soma asked, a note of concern in her voice.

"30 minutes," Vukradin said, and it was settled.

I looked at the bodies, at the feathers already stirring in the wind, and I thought of the song that had almost held us. It had been beautiful, in the way that all predation is beautiful — a hook crafted with patience, a lure shaped by generations of hunger. The harpies had understood something that the surface world was always forgetting: that voice is a weapon, that song can shackle, that the most dangerous thing a creature can do is open its mouth.

They had been slain by that same truth. Not by sword or claw, but by the recognition that their own art could be turned against them.

Brewbarry's axes were back in his hands. Soma's spider form was beginning to dissolve, the tortle's familiar shell emerging from the chitin. Vukradin was already composing, I suspected, some triumphant ballad that would find its way into whatever academy would have the surviving harpies' peers.

The wind carried the smell of blood and salt. The sun, indifferent and radiant, continued its slow arc across the sky.

I had done four points of damage with a critical hit. It had been enough. It had been a coin in the jar. And the jar, in the end, had been enough.

That is how these things work. One strike at a time, one song at a time, one god's light at a time, until what was broken is ended and what was ended is still.
